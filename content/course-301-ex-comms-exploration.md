# AI 301 · The Specialist — Employee Experience / Internal Communications, exploration (pre-outline)

**Status:** working exploration, not an outline. Fourth role track. Follows the method the first
three used: start from the job, find where AI meets it, subtract everything 101, 201, and the
other three tracks already teach, and see what survives.

**Prior hypothesis, from the authoring brief:** *"Note the likely overlap with the volume trap
(201 and the HRBP track's M3): cheaper content production is this role's central risk, not its
opportunity."* That turned out to be right and incomplete. It is the central risk, and the reason
is sharper than "more content": this is the only People function that spends **a resource it
doesn't own** — everyone else's attention — and AI removed the last thing that rationed it.

---

## 1. What the job actually is

Not the job description — the calendar. Two adjacent jobs that most organizations merge, and a
track has to serve the merged version.

**Internal communications:**

- **The leadership channel.** All-hands scripts, the CEO's message, town hall Q&A prep, exec
  ghostwriting. A large share of the week is writing in someone else's name.
- **Change comms.** Reorgs, layoffs, RTO mandates, M&A, policy changes. The hardest work in the
  function and the work it is judged on.
- **The manager cascade.** Talking points, FAQs, and briefing kits so 200 managers can deliver the
  same message locally. Consistency is the entire product.
- **Channels and calendar.** Intranet, newsletter, Slack/Teams, digital signage, email. Who may
  broadcast to whom, and what goes where.
- **Crisis, internally.** An incident, a news story about the company, an executive departure.
- **Measurement.** Readership, open rates, pulse response, "did it land."

**Employee experience:**

- **The listening program.** Engagement surveys, pulses, exit interviews, focus groups — design,
  fielding, analysis, action planning.
- **Moments that matter.** Onboarding and the first 90 days, promotion, return from leave,
  offboarding. Journey and persona work.
- **Service delivery.** The HR help desk, the knowledge base, the "where do I find the policy"
  problem — increasingly an employee-facing AI assistant.
- **Recognition, workplace, and hybrid experience.**

## 2. What's distinctive about it

Six properties that aren't true of People work generally, and that a role course has to be built
on. The first two are the ones nothing else in the curriculum touches.

**Their output is consumed by every employee at once, unedited.** An HRBP's advice reaches a
manager. A recruiter's process reaches candidates one at a time. Comp's model reaches a CFO. This
function's output reaches ten thousand people simultaneously and is usually the *first* thing
anyone reads about a decision. The blast radius is categorically different, and so is the cost of
a bad one.

**They write in other people's names.** Executive ghostwriting is the core craft, and it is the
thing generative AI is most uncannily good at. When the CEO's layoff message was drafted by a
model from a three-line brief, the question "who is actually speaking here" stops being
philosophical.

**They spend a budget that isn't theirs.** Employee attention is finite, shared, and
non-renewable within a week. Every other function's AI story is "produce more." For this function,
production volume was already the presenting complaint before AI arrived.

**Their asset is being believed, and it depreciates.** Trust in the channel is what makes the next
message work. It is earned over years and spent in a single badly-judged send — and it is the one
input AI cannot manufacture.

**They own the only AI in the curriculum that talks directly to employees.** The HR assistant
answering a parental-leave question at 11pm is a publishing surface with no editor. Every other AI
use in this course produces a draft for a human to own; this one ships straight to the reader.

**They are the messenger for AI itself.** This function announces the AI policy, the AI-attributed
restructuring, and the "AI will augment, not replace you" message — while using AI to write it.
No other role has to sell the thing it is simultaneously using on the audience being sold to.

## 3. The subtraction

The failure mode for a role course is 201 with new nouns. This role is the most exposed to that
failure of any track so far, because its core work — writing — is what 101 and 201 spend the most
time on. Every candidate topic has to state its delta.

| Covered by | What it already gives an EX/IC person | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 101 M4 | Data tiers, redaction, the shown-to-the-person test | Survey verbatim handling mechanics are done |
| 101 M5 | Prompting as briefing; examples as the strongest lever | **A "prompting for better copy" module is dead on arrival** |
| 101 M6 | Hallucination, the failure taxonomy, verification by stakes | Fact-checking a newsletter is 101 |
| 101 M7 | Assist vs. decide, bias as fidelity | The general line is drawn |
| 101 M8 | Disclosure by the reader's stake; the agreement layer | **The disclosure principle is taught. Only the institutional-voice instance is new** |
| 201 M2 | Context packs | **A "build your brand voice pack" module is 201 M2 in costume** |
| 201 M3 | Document pipelines — *survey verbatims → themes → exec summary is the literal worked example* | **The engagement-survey analysis module is dead. Interpretation survives; mechanics don't** |
| 201 M5 | The autonomy ladder | The bot module inherits the ladder rather than rebuilding it |
| 201 M6 | People data in production; the boundary sheet | The general boundary is set |
| 201 M7 | The operating rhythm, measurement, retirement | **A "your comms calendar as workflows" module is 201 M7 in costume** |
| HRBP M3 | The volume trap, botsitting, the four-question audit, vendor teardown | **Biggest overlap in the curriculum — see below** |
| HRBP M7 | Manager capability as product | The cascade topic must not become manager coaching |
| Recruiter R1 | Two-sided signal collapse, the detection reflex | Different problem; no reuse |
| Comp M5 | The counterparty has AI too | The employee-as-counterparty framing is available, not repeatable |

**Three temptations killed outright.** The survey-analysis module (201 M3's worked example), the
voice-and-style-pack module (201 M2), and the comms-calendar-as-workflows module (201 M7). All
three feel role-specific. None are.

**And one that has to be handled rather than killed.** HRBP M3 already teaches the volume trap.
The delta is real but it has to be *stated*, not assumed: in the HRBP track the trap costs
**decisions** — you produce more artifacts and no more calls. Here it costs **attention**, and the
difference matters in one specific way: decisions are your own budget, and attention is everyone
else's, spent without their consent. That is a different argument with a different remedy (a kill
list, not a volume decision), and it earns its place only if the course says so out loud and cites
the HRBP version rather than pretending to invent it.

## 4. Candidate topics, ranked

By the test: *would an internal communicator read this and think "finally, someone understands my
actual job"?*

**A. Whose voice is this — executive ghostwriting with AI.** The highest-confidence topic of the
set. The craft is old; what changed is that the executive no longer has to be involved at all. And
there is now direct evidence that the reader can tell: with heavy AI assistance, perceived
*sincerity* collapses while perceived *professionalism* barely moves (§6). Nothing anywhere in
this curriculum, or in the market, teaches the institutional-voice case. *Delta:* 101 M8 gave
disclosure by reader's stake; this gives the input floor, the message-type gradient, and the one
legal surface aimed squarely at this function.

**B. Deciding what not to send.** The kill list, channel discipline, and measurement that isn't
open rates. *Delta:* the HRBP volume trap re-aimed at a resource the learner doesn't own, plus the
part that function never practices — subtraction. Strong, but it has to earn its distance from
HRBP M3 explicitly.

**C. Listening without surveillance.** Survey promises, re-identification in small teams, passive
sentiment analysis on internal channels, and the legal floor for monitoring. *Delta:* entirely
unserved. HRBP M6 covers ER documentation; recruiter R6 covers hiring tools; nobody covers the
employer reading its own workforce. The legal analysis here is genuinely counterintuitive (§6) and
counsel-gated.

**D. The employee-facing assistant.** The HR bot as a publishing surface with no editor. *Delta:*
201 M5 taught the autonomy ladder for agents that take actions; this is an agent whose action is
*speaking to an employee in the company's voice.* Different risk class, and the knowledge base —
not the model — is the product. **Boundary flag:** People Ops will want the operational half
(deflection, ticket routing, workflow). The split I'd defend is that EX owns voice, refusal, and
what the employee was told; People Ops owns throughput.

**E. Telling them about the AI.** The credibility trap: announcing AI change, using AI, to an
audience that can check the claims against their own week. *Delta:* unserved anywhere, and the
natural course close because the function's own credibility is the thing at stake.

**F. Localization and translation at scale.** Real and transformed — a mistranslated benefits or
safety message is a genuine incident. But it's a lesson, not a module.

**G. The manager cascade under AI.** Sharp beat: consistency was the entire point of a cascade,
and AI makes local variation free — 200 managers each generating their own version of the layoff
message. Currently a lesson inside B. Flagged as the most credible candidate for an eighth module.

### Deliberately not modules

- **Engagement survey analysis** — 201 M3's worked example. Interpretation survives inside C.
- **Brand voice packs** — 201 M2.
- **The comms calendar as workflows** — 201 M7.
- **Prompting for better copy** — 101 M5, and a 300-level course that teaches it has lost the plot.

## 5. What this suggests the course is

The shape that emerged is not the shape a template would have produced, and it is not the HRBP
track's shape either. Three observations:

**The diagnosis inverts.** Every other track opens by telling the learner they are less behind
than they fear. This one has to open by telling them they are *ahead* — on the wrong axis. The
function has adopted AI almost universally for drafting, which is exactly where it is most
dangerous, and barely at all for targeting and measurement, which is where it would help. That
inversion is the module, and it is corroborated by the profession's own benchmark data (§6).

**The decomposition is by work type, not leverage tier.** Five types — **produce, voice, listen,
design, counsel** — and they have wildly different relationships with AI. The punchline is that
the function is *identified* with produce (now commoditized) and *paid* for counsel (untouched),
and most communicators have never been trained in the second.

**The floor doesn't want to be one module.** In all three existing tracks the legal floor is a
single module in a fixed position. Here the law splits cleanly in two by which half of the job it
governs — publishing law (AI-content disclosure, editorial responsibility) belongs with the voice
module, and monitoring law (emotion recognition, works councils, §7, state notice regimes) belongs
with the listening module. Forcing them together would produce a module about two unrelated
statutory regimes joined only by the word "legal." This is the outline's biggest deviation from
the convention and it should be argued, not assumed.

## 6. Verification done before writing any of this

Five checks changed the content rather than confirming it. Recorded here because the pattern is
the point.

1. **The EU AI Act's workplace emotion-recognition ban is narrower than it reads.** Article 5(1)(f)
   has been in force since 2 February 2025 and is absolute except for medical and safety purposes.
   But the prohibition's text doesn't itself say "biometric" — the Commission's guidelines tie it
   to the Act's definition of an emotion recognition system, which *is* limited to inference from
   biometric data. So a webcam reading faces in a town hall is squarely prohibited, while
   text-based sentiment analysis of Slack messages or survey verbatims very likely falls outside
   it — and is unsettled where it drives employment decisions. I would have written "sentiment
   analysis is banned in the EU," which is wrong and would have been the module's centerpiece.

2. **The NLRB memo on electronic surveillance was rescinded.** GC 23-02 was withdrawn on 14
   February 2025, among 31 rescinded memos. Citing it as live guidance would have been an error.
   The honest version is better content: the *statute* didn't move, the *enforcement posture* did
   — so a listening program designed against a posture breaks when the posture flips, and should
   be designed against the duty instead.

3. **Article 50's transparency obligations became applicable on 2 August 2026** — ten days before
   this was written. Synthetic audio/video of a real person carries a disclosure duty that, unlike
   the AI-generated-text obligation, is *not* limited to public-interest publishing, so the
   AI-dubbed CEO video shown to employees is in scope. And the carve-out is the best teaching
   material in the track: the text obligation falls away where content had human review and a
   named person holds editorial responsibility. That turns a compliance question into the question
   the function should already have been asking — who owns this sentence?

4. **The sincerity/professionalism divergence is real, peer-reviewed, and better than expected.**
   Coman (Florida) and Cardon (USC), *International Journal of Business Communication*, n=1,100
   professionals: at high AI assistance, supervisors were rated sincere by 40–52% versus 83% at low
   assistance, while professionalism fell only from 95% to 69–73%. The two curves separate. I
   expected a single "employees distrust AI writing" number; what exists is far sharper — **AI
   makes the writing better and the writer less believable at the same time** — and it comes with
   a usable gradient: the penalty concentrates in relationship-oriented messages and largely
   spares informational ones.

5. **A statistic I nearly used was the opposite of what I thought.** Secondary coverage produced
   "75% of internal communicators use GenAI for drafting or editing." Gallagher's 2026 report
   actually says **75% of IC functions remain in early-stage AI adoption — experimenting ad hoc or
   not using it at all.** Nearly inverted. The figures I'd now anchor on, all from that report
   (n≈1,300 comms and HR professionals across 40 countries, 18th year of the State of the Sector
   series): employees inundated with messages are **30% more likely to lose trust in leadership and
   24% more susceptible to burnout**; **83%** cite information overload as a growing challenge;
   **61%** of organizations have no formal change communication strategy despite ranking change
   comms their top skill need; only **36%** feel AI-ready, rising to **61%** where formal
   governance exists; and high-maturity teams use AI to measure and generate insight while
   low-maturity teams use it to draft.

**And one deliberate absence.** Every available number about employee-facing HR assistants is a
vendor number with no published methodology — accuracy claims, ticket-deflection percentages,
error-reduction ranges, all of it marketing. So that module carries **no statistical anchor** and
is argued from craft, like the HRBP track's M4. A module about a product category where all the
evidence is marketing should say so in its first paragraph.

**Still unverified and blocking before drafting:** the leadership-credibility figures (33% say AI
communication erodes credibility vs. 13% enhances; 43% say it depends on transparency) — surfaced
in secondary coverage with no primary source or sample identified yet, and what carries the
closing module; the Gallagher figures against the primary PDF rather than secondary reporting (the
publisher's domain is blocked from this environment); the US state electronic-monitoring notice
regimes (New York, Connecticut, Delaware) and their current requirements; ECPA consent mechanics;
and works council consultation duties in the specific jurisdictions the content will name.

## 7. Open questions for review

- **Is this one track or two?** Internal comms and employee experience merge in most organizations
  and diverge in large ones, where EX owns the listening program and the service desk while IC
  owns the leadership channel. The outline serves the merged role. If a deployment's population is
  mostly one half, three of seven modules will read as someone else's job.
- **Does the attention ledger work for a learner without a channel?** The proposed spine assumes
  the learner can name an audience they broadcast to. An EX programme manager may not.
- **Is counsel teachable async?** M2's punchline is that the function is paid for counsel and
  trained in production. If the track asserts that and then doesn't teach counsel, it has
  diagnosed a gap and left it open.
- **The People Ops boundary on the assistant module**, per candidate D.
- **Counsel review of the listening module**, and of the Article 50 material in the voice module —
  two separate legal surfaces, so possibly two different reviewers.
