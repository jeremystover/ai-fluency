## Calibration prompt — before you start

*One claim. Commit before you read.*

> **"I could name, today, every AI system in my organization that makes or shapes a decision about a
> person — and for each one I could say who reviewed it."**

**True of us, or not true of us?** Commit in a sentence.

Then, the harder version: **name one consequential decision about a person currently being made or
shaped in your company by a system nobody in HR has reviewed.** Write it down.

If you cannot name one, that is not a pass. **It means either there isn't one — which would make you
unusual — or you cannot see them**, and the activity will ask you to go and find out which. "I could
not find one" is a finding, and it is graded as one.

## Module brief

Every other module in this track is about a decision you get to make. This one is about a liability
you already have.

The structure of it is unusual and it is the reason this module exists:

> **You own the trust deficit whether or not you owned the design decision.**

When a candidate believes the process treated them unfairly, when an employee believes a system
decided something about their career, when a journalist asks who signed off on the screening tool —
**those arrive at your door.** Not the CIO's. Not the vendor's. The function whose name is on the
employment relationship absorbs the consequences of decisions it frequently did not make and often
did not know about.

That is an accountability-without-authority problem, which is a genuinely bad structural position
and the honest starting point for the module. What you can do about it is narrower than you would
like and more than nothing.

Two things get taught. **What the exposure actually is** — the trust position, the statutory floor,
and the rule that survives every rollback. And **what a review that catches it looks like**, which is
mostly a matter of knowing where to look.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

1. State the trust asymmetry accurately, with its sample.
2. State the current EU position, including which obligations bind now and which were deferred.
3. Explain why "AI is never the decision-maker" survives regulatory change when a compliance posture
   does not.
4. Distinguish having a policy from having governance.
5. Locate one unreviewed decision about a person in your own organization.

## Lesson 1 · The asymmetry **[V]**

Start with the population whose trust you are spending.

**Only 26% of job candidates trust that AI will evaluate them fairly** — from a Gartner survey of
2,918 candidates. Roughly **79% want to be told when AI is used** in a process that concerns them.
And around **38% have walked away from a hiring process** because it included an AI interview.

That last figure is the one to hold, because it is behavioural rather than attitudinal. People are
not registering discomfort in a survey. **They are leaving.**

Set that against where the function sits. In **52% of organizations HR has no direct involvement in
AI strategy at all**; 28% report HR is regarded as a true strategic leader with a seat at the table,
and a further 19% say HR is seen as a strategic partner *and still excluded from core decision
forums* `[V]`.

**Put those together and the shape of the problem is exact.** The systems that spend employee and
candidate trust are increasingly selected, configured, and deployed by people who do not carry the
consequence of spending it — and the function that does carry it is absent from the decision in half
of organizations.

This is why Module 3 came first. **A trust deficit you own and cannot influence is not a governance
problem. It is a scope problem you have already lost**, and the review right this module describes
is the minimum recovery.

One honest note on the figures: these are candidate-side, and candidate trust is the best-measured
part of this because hiring is where the research money has gone. Employee-side trust in systems
that touch pay, performance, and progression is less well measured and there is no reason to assume
it is higher.

## Lesson 2 · The floor, and why deferral is not runway **[V]**

The statutory position, stated as of writing, with the warning that this is the passage most likely
to be out of date and most likely to be misquoted to you by somebody selling something.

The EU AI Act classifies employment uses — recruitment, selection, promotion, termination, task
allocation, and performance monitoring — as high-risk. Those obligations moved to **2 December 2027**
under the Digital Omnibus on AI, **Regulation (EU) 2026/1744**, which entered into force on 27 July
2026, six days before the original deadline.

**Read only that and you would conclude you have until 2027. You do not.**

The **AI literacy obligation** and the **prohibition on emotion recognition in the workplace** have
been in force since February 2025. And since **2 August 2026**, the Act's **transparency
obligations** apply — to covered systems *regardless of high-risk classification*. A system
interacting directly with a person must disclose that it is AI, at or before the start of the
interaction. **Recruiting chatbots, HR service assistants and onboarding bots operating in Europe
are inside that**, with a ceiling of €15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover.

Three things for someone in your seat specifically.

**The emotion-recognition prohibition is absolute.** Not a risk to be managed with consent or
disclosure. If a vendor offers engagement inference from webcam, affect detection in interviews, or
sentiment scoring in a virtual session, and you have European operations, the answer is no — and
these products are marketed to People functions on the grounds that they finally measure engagement.

**The AI literacy obligation is partly yours**, and it is the one item on this list that is also an
opportunity. It lands on whoever builds capability.

**And the rule that outlives all of it.** Statutory positions have now moved twice in eighteen
months, in both directions. A compliance posture built on the current date will expire. **A posture
built on a principle will not**, and the principle worth building on is the one that has survived
every version of every regime:

> **AI is never the decision-maker. Automate the assembly, not the reasoning.**

A system may gather, structure, summarize, rank for human review, and flag. **A person decides, and
that person is nameable.** That rule is stricter than the current law in most jurisdictions, which
is precisely why it is durable — you will not have to rewrite it when the law moves again.

## Lesson 3 · Policy is not governance **[V]**

Here is the gap the numbers make visible.

Among organizations currently using or piloting AI, roughly **49% have a policy regulating workforce
AI use.** Of those, only about **a quarter believe their policy is clear and durable.**

Multiply those and the picture is stark: something like **one organization in eight has an AI policy
its own people think will hold.** The rest have either nothing, or a document.

**The difference between a policy and governance is not the document. It is whether anything has
ever been refused.**

Module 2's boundary case was exactly this: writing the policy is operating system, and **refusing a
peer's request for an exception is governance.** A policy that has never been enforced against
someone with standing is not a control. It is a statement of intent that the organization has not
yet had occasion to ignore.

Which produces a diagnostic worth running on yourself, and it takes one minute: **when did your
organization last say no to an AI deployment, and who was told?** If the answer is never, you do not
have governance, whatever is written down. If the answer is "we've never had a case that warranted
it," check that against Lesson 1's asymmetry — a function excluded from AI strategy in half of
organizations is not a function that is seeing the cases.

### The scenario nobody has an answer for

Read this one properly, because your policy almost certainly does not address it.

*An employee uses the company's AI coaching tool for six weeks. Over that period she describes
increasing isolation, a sense that she doesn't belong, and a loss of confidence. The tool responds
supportively and appropriately each time. It never escalates — it was not designed to, and no
escalation path exists. She resigns, and alleges the company knew and did nothing.*

**Did it know?** The information was in a system the company owns, procured, and deployed. No human
saw it. There was no route by which a human would have.

Every position on this is uncomfortable. Building escalation makes the tool a surveillance system and
destroys the confidentiality that made it useful. Not building it means the organization holds
disclosures it cannot act on. Telling employees plainly that nothing they say is monitored is honest
and reduces the tool's value to the people who most need help — **which is Module 4 of the Talent
Development track's finding arriving as a governance problem: the people served worst are the ones
who needed it most.**

There is no settled answer. What there is: **a decision, made deliberately, written down, and
disclosed to the people using the tool.** The failure is not choosing wrong. It is that in most
organizations nobody has chosen at all, and the answer will be determined by whatever the vendor's
default happens to be.

> ### Try this — 3 minutes
> Ask whoever owns your employee-facing AI tools one question: *"If someone disclosed something
> serious to this system, what happens?"* Note whether the answer is a designed behaviour or a
> guess. In most organizations it is a guess, and the guess is usually wrong in the reassuring
> direction.

## Key takeaways

- **You own the trust deficit whether or not you owned the design decision** — accountability without
  authority, which is the honest structural position.
- **Only 26% of candidates trust AI to evaluate them fairly** `[V]` (Gartner, n=2,918); ~79% want to
  be told when it is used; **~38% have walked away from a process that included an AI interview** —
  behavioural, not attitudinal.
- **And HR is absent from AI strategy in 52% of organizations** `[V]`, so the systems spending that
  trust are chosen by people who do not carry the consequence.
- **Deferral is not runway** `[V]`. EU high-risk employment obligations moved to 2 December 2027

  have bound since February 2025, and the transparency obligations since 2 August 2026 — which puts

- **The emotion-recognition prohibition is absolute**, and these products are marketed to People
  functions specifically.
- **A posture built on a date expires; one built on a principle does not.** *AI is never the
  decision-maker: automate the assembly, not the reasoning*, and the deciding person is nameable.
- **Roughly 49% have a policy and about a quarter of those think it is clear and durable** `[V]` —
  around one organization in eight has one its own people believe will hold.
- **The difference between a policy and governance is whether anything has ever been refused.**
- **The disclosure scenario has no settled answer**, and the failure is not choosing wrong — it is
  that nobody has chosen, so the vendor's default decides.

## Take a position

**The claim:** *"You own the trust deficit whether or not you owned the design decision."*

The strongest counter-argument is that **accepting this is how a function becomes a permanent
scapegoat, and refusing it may be the more responsible move.** Accountability without authority is a
structurally broken position, and the standard executive response to being handed one is to decline
it until the authority arrives. A CPO who absorbs the consequences of the CIO's architecture — and
absorbs them visibly, competently, and without complaint — has removed the only pressure that would
ever have moved the decision rights, and has taught the organization that the People function is
where AI problems go to be managed rather than solved.

The sharper version: **this module tells you to accept a liability and then offers you a review
right as compensation, which is not a trade.** A named decision right over systems touching
employment decisions is a real thing to have, and it is not equivalent to owning the outcome when
one of them harms somebody. If the exchange is genuinely unequal, the correct move is Module 3's
decline — say plainly that the function will not carry the consequences of decisions it is excluded
from, and let the organization discover what that means.

Your position has to survive that. In particular it has to say **what you would actually do the day
after a system you never reviewed produced a bad outcome for a named individual** — because both
positions have to answer that question, and only one of them has to answer it in public.

## Applied activity — "The unreviewed decision"

**Time:** 30 minutes · **Submit:** the finding plus a 300–400 word write-up · **Graded against the
rubric below.** Score doesn't matter. Doing the work is where the learning lands.

**Go and find one consequential decision about a person currently being made or shaped in your
organization by a system nobody in HR has reviewed.** Then decide what to do about it.

**Step 1 — Look in the right places (10 min).** They are not where you expect, because the ones you
expect have already been reviewed. Candidate screening and ranking. Scheduling and routing that
determines who gets seen. Internal talent marketplaces and who gets surfaced. Skills or readiness
inference. Engagement or sentiment analysis. Performance-adjacent summarization. Anything embedded
in a platform you bought for another purpose — **the feature that arrived in a release note is the
highest-yield place to look.**

**Step 2 — Name one (5 min).** What it is, what it decides or shapes, who deployed it, and — the
diagnostic question — **would the affected person know?**

**Step 3 — Apply the durable rule (5 min).** Is it assembling, or is it reasoning? If a person
decides, name them. If no person decides, or the person is approving a ranked list they cannot
interrogate, say so — **that is the finding, and 101 M7's line applies.**

**Step 4 — Decide what you do (8 min).** Not what should happen in principle. What you will do, by
when, and with whom. Escalate, require review, require disclosure, or accept it and record that you
did. **Accepting it deliberately and writing that down is a legitimate answer** and a better one
than an escalation you will not follow through.

**Step 5 — The disclosure question (2 min).** For one employee-facing AI tool, answer the Try This
question: if someone disclosed something serious, what happens? Designed behaviour or guess.

**If you genuinely cannot find one**, submit the search instead: where you looked, who you asked,
and what you concluded. **That is a full-credit answer** — and if the reason is that nobody could
tell you what systems are running, say that plainly, because it is a larger finding than any single
system would have been.

Then the write-up: what you found, what you will do, your position on the module's claim with its
counter-argument addressed — **including what you would do the day after a system you never reviewed
harmed someone** — and the honest one: **whether you knew about this system already and had not
acted.** That answer is common and it is the most useful thing in the submission.

- D. Only systems making fully automated decisions are covered

**Q4.** Why does the module recommend building a posture on a principle rather than the current statute? `[V]`

- C. Because principles are easier to communicate to employees than legal requirements
- D. Because regulators give credit for principles-based compliance

> **B.** Being stricter than the law is exactly what makes it durable. You will not have to rewrite

**Q5.** What distinguishes having a policy from having governance?

- D. Whether the policy covers vendor systems as well as internal ones

> **C.** Writing the policy is operating-system work; refusing a peer's exception is governance. The

**Q6.** Roughly what proportion of organizations have an AI policy their own people consider clear and durable? `[V]`

- D. About one in twenty

> **C.** The rest have either nothing or a document. The multiplication is what makes the gap
> visible; either figure alone reads much better than the position actually is.

**Q7.** What does the module say is the failure in the AI-coaching disclosure scenario?

- A. Deploying a coaching tool without confidentiality guarantees
- B. Failing to build an escalation path into the tool
- C. That nobody has made a decision at all — so the behaviour is determined by the vendor's default rather than by the organization ✓
- D. Not informing employees that the tool retains conversation data

> **C.** Every available position is uncomfortable and the module does not claim to settle it. What
> it requires is a deliberate decision, written down and disclosed — B is one legitimate choice
> among several, not the answer.

**Q8.** A learner searches thoroughly and cannot find an unreviewed decision. How should that be scored?

- A. As incomplete, since the activity requires a named system
- B. As full credit if they submit the search — where they looked, who they asked, what they concluded — and especially if the reason is that nobody could tell them what systems are running ✓
- C. As evidence their organization has mature AI governance
- D. As a reason to complete the activity using a hypothetical system

> **B.** The inability to see the systems is a larger finding than any single system would have been,
> and C is exactly the wrong inference — not finding one may mean there isn't one, or it may mean
> you cannot see them, and the search is what distinguishes those.
