## Module brief

Everything in this track has been building a position. This module is about the conversation where
you have to fund it, and it has one governing sentence:

> ***"We cannot tell if it worked"* and *"it did not work"* produce an identical conversation with a
> CFO.**

Not similar. Identical. From the other side of the table there is no difference between an
initiative that failed and one whose result you can't demonstrate, both are unfunded next year,
and the second one costs you credibility as well, because you asked for money and can't say what
happened to it.

Which relocates measurement entirely. **It is not a reporting activity that happens after. It is a
design constraint that binds before**, because a baseline you didn't take at the start can't be
reconstructed at the end. That isn't a discipline problem. It is arithmetic.

This module asks for something narrower than rigour, because rigour isn't available and pretending
otherwise would waste your time. **One measure, on one initiative, that could come back negative,
with a threshold you agreed before you started.** One.

And it teaches the thing you need before you can do that honestly, which is how to tell a number you
can defend from a number you were handed.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

1. Explain why measurement binds before rather than after, and what that means for baselines.
2. Distinguish a citable number from a vendor number, and recognize scope-laundering.
3. Set a pre-committed threshold and say what it buys you.
4. Make the argument to a board or comp committee, which is a different argument.
5. Produce the case, and account for how your positions moved across the track.

## Lesson 1 · The numbers you will be handed **[V]**

You're the target market for a great deal of confident measurement. Here's the method, and then a
worked example that is uncomfortably close to home.

**Three questions on any claim: what is the evidence, what is the sample, and what would falsify
it?**

### The example, and why it is in this module

You'll be shown a figure along these lines: **"only 28% of AI use cases meet ROI expectations."**
It circulates widely, it sounds like exactly the sober counterweight a skeptical executive wants,
and it is frequently offered to People leaders as context for their own AI investment.

Run the three questions.

*What is the evidence?* A Gartner survey: a real one, competently conducted.
*What is the sample?* **782 infrastructure-and-operations leaders**, fielded November–December 2025.
*What would falsify it?* It is a survey finding; it is what it is.

**And there is the problem. It is not about HR.** It is a finding about infrastructure and operations
— server estates, platform migrations, the technology function's own AI projects. Quoting it in a
People business case, or accepting it as context for your function's investment, is **using a real
study of a neighbouring thing to support a claim it does not make.**

That is the most effective form of laundering available, because **every check anyone runs will
pass.** The source exists. Gartner is credible. The figure is accurately quoted. Nobody has done
anything dishonest, and the number is still wrong for your purpose.

**This module puts that example first rather than in a footnote, because the track has just spent
five modules telling you to be skeptical of vendors, and the figure most likely to mislead you is
one you would have reached for yourself** — precisely because it is sober, credible, and supports a
cautious posture you already hold. Skepticism aimed only at optimistic claims isn't skepticism.

The question that catches it every time: **was the thing studied the thing being claimed?**

### What this means for your own case

The standard applies to you first. A CPO who puts a laundered figure in a board paper has done the
thing this module warns about, and the person who catches it will be a director with an
infrastructure background who recognizes the survey.

**The number you can defend is usually smaller, more local, and less impressive than the one you
were handed.** It is also the one that survives being checked, which is the only property that
matters in the room this module is about.

## Lesson 2 · A measure that can fail **[V]**

### Where the function actually stands

Roughly **47% of CHROs report they have not established clear productivity measurements** for AI,
from the 2026 CHRO Survey Report, run by the CHRO Association with the University of South Carolina's
Darla Moore School of Business. The same survey finds around **91% placing AI among their top
concerns.**

Hold those two together, because the combination is the module's problem stated as data. **Nine in
ten treat this as a top concern. Fewer than half can measure it.** That isn't a measurement gap. It
is an exposure: a function with its highest-priority initiative and no instrument, in front of a CFO
who will eventually ask.

### The design

Work backwards, and this part isn't contested.

**Start from the business result.** Not the initiative and not the adoption metric: the thing the
company wants to be different. Time-to-productivity for new hires. Regretted attrition in a specific
population. Cost per case in service delivery. Manager capability, if you can operationalize it.

**Then the behaviour that produces it.** What has to happen differently for that result to move?

**Then the intervention.** Now you know what you're buying and, often, that you're buying the
wrong thing.

**Then the baseline — today, before anything starts.** This is the step that can't be recovered
later. If you can't state today's number, **say so and go and get it**, because a case without a
baseline is a case whose result can't be demonstrated, which we have established is the same as a
case that failed.

### The threshold

Now the part almost nobody does.

**Name, before you start, the observation that would tell you this did not work, and the point at
which you would stop.**

*"If cost per case in HR service delivery hasn't fallen by 20% within two quarters of full
deployment, we stop and reallocate."*

Three things that sentence buys you.

**It makes the evaluation capable of returning a negative.** If every possible outcome leads to
"continue and refine," you were never measuring.

**It changes the conversation.** You're no longer asking for belief. You're proposing a bet with a
stated losing condition, and that is a form of argument the CFO recognizes as their own, which is
worth more than any figure you could quote.

**And it protects you**, because a threshold agreed in advance was agreed while everyone was
optimistic. Set afterwards, it will be set to whatever the initiative achieved, and everyone
involved will believe they were being fair.

**One warning, carried from Module 4.** Your threshold has to survive the J-curve. A service
transformation gets worse before it gets better, so a threshold measured at month three will trigger
during the trough and stop something that was working. **Set the measurement point past the trough,
and say where the trough is expected**, or you have built a mechanism that kills your own
initiative on schedule.

## Lesson 3 · The board is a different argument

The CFO wants a defensible number. **The board wants to know that somebody is in control of a risk
they cannot evaluate themselves.** Those are different asks and the second one is the one this
function is worst at.

Three things a board actually wants from you on this, and none of them is a productivity figure.

**That the exposure is known.** Not eliminated — known. Module 5's inventory is the answer to this,
and "we have identified the systems making or shaping decisions about people, here's the list, here
is who reviewed each" is a better board answer than any ROI claim, because it demonstrates control
of the thing they are actually worried about.

**That somebody is accountable by name.** Boards don't accept diffuse accountability on a topic
they read about in the press. The question behind the question is *if this goes wrong, who do we
call*, and "the AI steering group" isn't an answer.

**That there is a decision rule rather than a set of decisions.** A board can't evaluate individual
deployments. It can evaluate a rule, and Module 5's (*AI is never the decision-maker; a person
decides and that person is nameable*) is exactly the kind of thing a board can hold you to. **Give
them something to hold you to, deliberately**, because the alternative is that they invent one under
pressure and it will be worse.

**And the thing not to do**, which is the most common failure in this room: **do not bring a
technology briefing.** Boards don't need to be taught what a model is, they won't admit it if
they do, and the CPO who spends fifteen minutes explaining the technology has confirmed that they
think the board's problem is comprehension rather than control.

- **Three questions on any claim: evidence, sample, falsifier.**
- **The "28% of AI use cases meet ROI" figure is real and about infrastructure and operations** —
  782 I&O leaders `[V]`. Quoting it about HR is scope-laundering, and it is the example most likely

  studied the thing being claimed?*
- **Roughly 47% of CHROs have not established clear AI productivity measurement while around 91%
  place AI among their top concerns** `[V]` — a top-priority initiative with no instrument.
- **Work backwards: result, behaviour, intervention, and the baseline today.**
- **Name the losing condition before you start.** It makes the evaluation capable of returning a

## Take a position

**The claim:** *"A number you can't baseline today is a number you'll lose with in eighteen
months, whatever the technology does."*

The strongest counter-argument is that **the baseline rule systematically selects for the trivial.**
The initiatives with clean baselines are the transactional ones (cost per case, time to hire,
ticket volume) and those are exactly the operating-system work Module 2 told you is compressing
anyway. The things that would actually change the company, and the things only a CPO can drive (
whether managers are better, whether the culture survives a restructure, whether the succession
bench is real) have no clean baseline and never will. **A function that only bets where it can
measure has optimized for surviving budget reviews rather than for mattering**, which is a
sophisticated way of becoming irrelevant on schedule.

There is a sharper version, and it should worry you more. **Demanding a threshold may just relocate
the dishonesty.** You'll get thresholds set where they are certain to be met: agreed in advance,
technically pre-committed, and meaningless by construction. That is worse than no threshold, because
it carries the authority of having been committed to, and everyone involved can point at it.

Your position has to survive both. Specifically: **what would you do with the initiative you believe
in most, if it genuinely cannot produce a falsifiable measure inside a budget cycle?** That
initiative is in your portfolio right now, and this module has just told you it is indefensible.

## Applied activity — "The case" *(course close)*

**Time:** 35 minutes · **Submit:** the case plus the reckoning, in 350–450 words · **Graded against
the rubric below.** Score doesn't matter. Doing the work is where the learning lands.

Two parts. The first is the artifact; the second is the point of the course.

### Part one — the case (25 min)

**One page**, for whoever holds your budget.

**Step 1. The chain.** Business result → behaviour → intervention. In that order. If you find
yourself starting from the intervention, start again.

**Step 2. The baseline, today.** An actual number with its source. **Or an honest statement that no
baseline exists, and exactly how you'll establish one before anything starts.** Both are full
answers. An estimate presented as a measurement isn't.

**Step 3 — The threshold.** The observation that would tell you to stop, the number, and the date —
**set past the J-curve**, with the trough's expected depth stated so the threshold doesn't trigger
inside it.

**Step 4. The board version.** Three sentences: what the exposure is and that it is known, who is
accountable by name, and the decision rule you're offering to be held to.

**Step 5. The claim you are not making.** Name one impressive figure you could have used and
deliberately left out, and why. **If you were tempted by the 28% figure, say so** — that is the
module's test and admitting it scores better than avoiding it silently.

### Part two — the reckoning (10 min)

You committed a position at the start of each module. Go back and read them.

- **M1**. You personally used AI on the judgment parts of your job in the last month
- **M2** — more of your calendar went to strategic counsel than to running the operating system
- **M3**. It is clear who owns AI, and your peers would all give the same name
- **M4**, a major consolidation would dip and recover within two quarters
- **M5** — you could name every system making decisions about people, and who reviewed each
- **M6**. You could give your CEO a number you would be willing to have checked

**Which moved, and why?** Not which were right, which changed, what changed them, and whether they
moved in **a consistent direction.** Most people in this seat find their positions were optimistic
in one specific way, and naming that pattern is the most valuable thing you'll take from this
course.

**The rubric grades the account of the change, never the accuracy of any position.**
