## Calibration prompt — before you start

*One claim. Commit before you read anything.*

Here is a factual assertion about your own organization:

> **"Our function's money is spent on the same links our function's time is spent on."**

**True of us, or not true of us?** Commit to one, in a sentence.

Most people commit to "true of us" without having looked, because it sounds like it ought to be
true. It usually isn't, and the gap between where the time goes and where the money goes is the
single most useful line in the map you're about to build.

*This module also closes the prediction you left open in Module 2 — the share of your team's week
spent producing. You'll measure it here against something real.*

## Module brief

Module 2 gave you the chain. This module puts your own function on it, with numbers, checked against
something that isn't your memory.

That last clause is the whole module. Everyone who has read Module 2 can produce a plausible
six-link split in ninety seconds, and that split will be wrong in a predictable direction: it will
understate Produce and overstate Decide, because Produce is unglamorous and Decide is what we would
like to be doing. Self-report on time allocation is one of the least reliable instruments in
professional life, and it is unreliable in a flattering direction.

So the map has three columns — **time, headcount, budget** — and one rule: **each column comes from
an artifact, not from recall.**

What you leave with is a one-page compression map of your own function. It is the evidence base for
everything in Part Three, and Module 10's proposal to an executive sponsor is argued from it
directly. It is also, in most organizations, the first time anybody has drawn this.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

1. Choose an instrument for measuring where a function's time goes, and say what each instrument
   over- and under-counts.
2. Map headcount and budget across the chain, and read the gaps between the three columns.
3. Recognize the common function shapes and what each one implies about where to invest.
4. Identify the two entries every map gets wrong, and correct them.

## Lesson 1 · The instrument problem

You need a source of truth about your own week that isn't your impression of your own week. Four
candidates, each with a known distortion.

**Last week's calendar.** Cheap, honest about meetings, blind to everything else. It systematically
over-counts Decide and Distribute — because those generate meetings — and under-counts Produce,
because drafting happens in the gaps between meetings and never appears as an entry. Use it if you
lead a team whose work is mostly coordination.

**The last twenty items in your task tracker.** Better on Produce, worse on the invisible work.
Under-counts Decide almost completely, because "told the CFO the framing was wrong" is not a ticket.
Use it if your function runs on intake requests, and correct for the missing counsel.

**Module 1's send audit.** The only instrument that is complete for what it covers, and it covers
Produce and Distribute only. Excellent for the two links it sees and silent on the other four. Use
it as a cross-check on the Produce number rather than as the whole map.

**A reconstruction of five working days.** Least reliable, most complete, and perfectly acceptable
**if you label it as a reconstruction.** Write it day by day rather than as a total — day-by-day
recall is materially better than week-level recall, and the difference is visible in the answers.

The choice matters less than the labelling. **An acknowledged estimate is usable; a confident
invention is not** — which is the standard this whole track applies to vendors and therefore has to
apply to itself.

One more distortion, and it is the one that catches people who are doing the exercise properly:
**an instrument that only sees your own time will miss the function's shape if you lead a team.**
If you have direct reports, map the team, not yourself. A director's week is mostly Decide, and a
map of it will tell you the function is in great shape while four people underneath are doing
nothing but Produce.

## Lesson 2 · The two columns that aren't time

Time is the column everyone draws. The other two are where the findings are, because they are
usually distributed differently — and a difference between columns is a diagnosis.

**Headcount: which links have a named owner?** Not who touches the link — who is accountable for
it. Ask it as a question with a person's name as the only acceptable answer: *if this link fails,
whose performance review does it appear in?*

In most functions this produces an uncomfortable pattern. Produce and Distribute have names.
Interpret sometimes has one, usually shared with an analytics team. **Sense and Verify very often
have nobody**, which means nobody's review is affected by whether the organization was listened to
or whether the message landed — and work that appears in nobody's review is work that stops
happening the first busy month.

**Budget: where does the money go?** Platform licences, agency retainers, design tooling, video
production, translation, survey instruments, the intranet contract. Assign each line to the link it
serves.

This column almost always sits in a different place from the time column, and the most common
pattern is worth naming: **time in Produce, money in Distribute.** The function spends its hours
making things and its budget on the platform that delivers them. Which means the largest lever the
function controls financially is pointed at the link that was mostly solved before AI arrived, and
the links AI just opened — Sense and Interpret — have no budget line at all.

That sentence, with your own numbers in it, is the strongest single line in a Module 10 proposal.

> ### Try this — 2 minutes
> Name the person whose performance review is affected if nobody in the organization understood
> last quarter's biggest announcement. If the answer takes more than five seconds, you have found
> your ownerless link, and it is Verify.

## Lesson 3 · Reading your own map

Four shapes recur. Find yours, then read the prescription — and note that none of them is a failing
grade, because they are responses to real constraints.

**The production shop.** Sixty to eighty percent of time in Produce, budget in tooling that makes
producing faster, no owner at Sense or Verify. The most common shape by a distance, and the one AI
most directly threatens — because it is the shape most exposed to a link whose cost just collapsed.
*Prescription:* the fastest available return is Verify, not Sense. It's cheaper to start, it
protects the output you're already making, and it's the one Part Two of this track builds.

**The broadcast desk.** Heavy Produce and Distribute, significant platform budget, sophisticated
segmentation, and still no Sense. Common in large distributed organizations that invested in a
communications platform. *Prescription:* you already own the instrument. Most modern platforms
capture search and engagement data nobody in the function reads. Your Sense capability is a report
away, not a purchase away.

**The two-person function.** Everything, thinly, with Decide happening constantly and invisibly
because the same person does all six links in the same hour. *Prescription:* the map's value here
isn't reallocation, it's evidence — this is the shape where a compression map is most persuasive to
a sponsor, because it makes visible that six jobs are being done by two people.

**The embedded advisor.** Real Decide time, genuine access to leadership, and no Sense
instrumentation at all — so counsel runs entirely on anecdote and the last conversation the advisor
happened to have. *Prescription:* the highest-leverage and most uncomfortable one. Your advice is
currently as good as your sampling, and your sampling is whoever talks to you.

## Lesson 4 · The two entries every map gets wrong

Correct these before you submit, because they distort the map in opposite directions.

**Proofreading is not Verify.** It is quality control on the artifact, which makes it Produce. Verify
asks two different questions: *was what we published actually true*, and *did it land?* Checking a
benefits summary against the plan documents is Verify. Checking it for typos is Produce. Teams that
log proofreading as Verify produce maps showing healthy verification coverage over a function that
has never once asked whether a message worked — and that is the single most common error in this
exercise.

**Meetings about a message are usually Decide, not Produce** — and they get logged as Produce
because a draft was on screen. The test from Module 2 applies: if you left that meeting and produced
nothing, would the organization be different? If yes — a framing changed, a claim was removed, an
order of announcement was altered, someone was told no — that was Decide. Most functions are doing
substantially more Decide work than their maps show, which matters, because Module 10 asks you to
argue for a mandate over exactly that link.

Correcting both usually moves five to fifteen percentage points around the map. It is worth the ten
minutes.

## Key takeaways

- **Each column comes from an artifact, not recall.** Self-report on time allocation is unreliable
  in a flattering direction — it understates Produce and overstates Decide.
- **Every instrument has a known distortion.** Calendars over-count Decide and Distribute and miss
  drafting; task trackers miss counsel almost entirely; the send audit is complete for two links and
  silent on four; a five-day reconstruction is fine **if labelled as one** and written day by day.
- **If you lead a team, map the team.** A director's week is mostly Decide, and mapping it will show
  a healthy function over four people doing nothing but Produce.
- **Ownership is the question with a name as its only answer:** if this link fails, whose review
  does it appear in? Sense and Verify usually have nobody — and work in nobody's review stops
  happening the first busy month.
- **The most common pattern is time in Produce, money in Distribute** — the financial lever pointed
  at the link that was already solved, with no budget line at all against the two links AI just
  opened.
- **The two corrections:** proofreading is Produce, not Verify. Meetings that changed what gets said
  are Decide, not Produce. Together they usually move five to fifteen points.

## Take a position

**The claim:** *"You cannot argue for a mandate you can't draw."*

The strongest counter-argument is that **this is a demand for evidence that the people who grant
mandates have never once required.** Functions win territory through relationships, timing, and
being useful in a crisis — not through analysis. Nobody was ever handed budget because their map
was well drawn. And there is a real cost to the alternative: a map showing 70% of time in the
collapsing link is a document that makes an excellent case for cutting your headcount, produced by
you, at your own initiative. On this reading the compression map is not a mandate argument; it is
evidence for the prosecution, and the sophisticated move is to build the capability quietly and
present it once it works.

Take a position on that, in writing, in the activity. The version worth submitting is specific about
who in *your* organization would receive this document and what they would do with it — because the
counter-argument is strong in some rooms and weak in others, and knowing which room you're in is the
actual skill.

## Applied activity — "Your compression map"

**Time:** 25 minutes · **Submit:** the map plus a 250–350 word write-up · **Graded against the
rubric below.** Score doesn't matter. Doing the work is where the learning lands.

One page. Three columns. Six rows.

**Step 1 — Pick your instrument and say so (2 min).** Calendar, task tracker, send audit, or a
labelled five-day reconstruction. Name it, and name what you know it will under-count. If you lead a
team, map the team.

**Step 2 — Time across the six links (10 min).** Assign entries to links. Split entries that span
two and say so. Apply both corrections from Lesson 4 — proofreading out of Verify, message meetings
out of Produce — and note how much they moved.

**Step 3 — Headcount (5 min).** For each link: the name of the person whose performance review is
affected if it fails. "Nobody" is a legitimate and common answer, and writing it is the point.

**Step 4 — Budget (5 min).** Every line you can see — platforms, agency, tooling, translation,
video, survey instruments — assigned to the link it serves. Approximate is fine; proportions matter
more than precision.

**Step 5 — Read it (3 min).** Which shape from Lesson 3 is closest? Where is the largest gap between
where time goes and where money goes? Which link has both no owner and no budget?

Then the write-up: your mapped Produce percentage against the prediction you committed in Module 2 —
direction and size of the miss, and one sentence on what it reveals; which shape you are and whether
the prescription fits; your position on the claim above with the counter-argument addressed,
including who would actually receive this document in your organization; and **the one number on
this page you would not want your CFO to see.** Naming it is the strongest form of this submission —
and it is usually the same number that makes your case in Module 10.

## Knowledge check — 8 questions

*Unlocks after the applied activity is submitted. Retakes are free and unlimited.*

**Q1.** Why must each column of the map come from an artifact rather than recall?

- A. Because artifacts are easier to share with executives
- B. Because self-report on time allocation is unreliable in a flattering direction — it understates Produce and overstates Decide ✓
- C. Because recall cannot produce percentages
- D. Because artifacts are required for the Module 10 proposal

> **B.** Everyone who has read Module 2 can produce a plausible split in ninety seconds, and it will be wrong predictably: Produce is unglamorous and Decide is what we'd like to be doing.

**Q2.** What does a calendar systematically over- and under-count?

- A. Over-counts Produce; under-counts Decide
- B. Over-counts Verify; under-counts Sense
- C. Over-counts Decide and Distribute because those generate meetings; under-counts Produce because drafting happens in the gaps and never appears as an entry ✓
- D. It counts all six links evenly, which is why it's the preferred instrument

> **C.** Every instrument has a known distortion, and the choice matters less than the labelling. An acknowledged estimate is usable; a confident invention is not.

**Q3.** Why should someone who leads a team map the team rather than themselves?

- A. Because team data is more accurate than individual data
- B. Because a director's week is mostly Decide, so mapping it shows a healthy function over a team doing nothing but Produce ✓
- C. Because individual maps cannot be compared across organizations
- D. Because headcount and budget are team-level figures anyway

> **B.** The map is of the function, not the mapper. This is the distortion that catches people who are otherwise doing the exercise carefully.

**Q4.** How should link ownership be established?

- A. By asking who spends the most time on it
- B. By asking who has the relevant skills
- C. By asking whose performance review is affected if the link fails — a question whose only acceptable answer is a person's name ✓
- D. By checking the org chart

> **C.** Touching a link is not owning it. And work that appears in nobody's review is work that stops happening the first busy month, which is why "nobody" is the most important answer this column can produce.

**Q5.** What is the most common gap between the time column and the budget column?

- A. Time in Decide, money in Produce
- B. Time in Produce, money in Distribute — the financial lever pointed at the link that was mostly solved before AI, with no budget line against Sense or Interpret ✓
- C. Time in Distribute, money in Verify
- D. Time and money both concentrate in Produce

> **B.** The function spends its hours making things and its budget on the platform that delivers them. That sentence with your own numbers in it is the strongest single line in a Module 10 proposal.

**Q6.** Why is proofreading classified as Produce rather than Verify?

- A. Because it is performed by the same person who drafts
- B. Because it happens before publication rather than after
- C. Because it is quality control on the artifact — Verify asks whether what was published was true, and whether it landed ✓
- D. Because it is too small a task to constitute its own link

> **C.** Teams that log proofreading as Verify produce maps showing healthy verification coverage over a function that has never once asked whether a message worked. It is the single most common error in this exercise.

**Q7.** A team has heavy Produce and Distribute, a significant platform budget, sophisticated segmentation, and no owner at Sense. Which shape is it, and what follows?

- A. The production shop — start with Verify
- B. The broadcast desk — the Sense capability is a report away rather than a purchase away, because the platform already captures the data ✓
- C. The two-person function — use the map as evidence for headcount
- D. The embedded advisor — the sampling problem is the priority

> **B.** Most modern communication platforms capture search and engagement data nobody in the function reads. This shape already owns the instrument and isn't using it.

**Q8.** What is the strongest argument *against* building a compression map at all?

- A. That six links is too granular to measure reliably
- B. That mandates are won through relationships and crises rather than analysis — and a map showing 70% of time in the collapsing link is evidence for the prosecution, self-produced ✓
- C. That the data required is not available in most organizations
- D. That executives prefer qualitative narratives to functional maps

> **B.** It is a genuinely strong counter and the module says so. Whether it holds depends entirely on who would receive the document in your organization, which is why the activity asks you to name them.
