## Module brief

Seven modules have been about control: what is running, whether it works, whether it should be on,
what it says to employees, what it rests on, what it can reach, and who signs. This one is about what
all of that was *for*, and it has to answer a question the track has been deferring.

If the register, the reviews, the exclusion list and the signed map do their job, you'll produce
capacity: some of your own, and more of other people's. **The question this module refuses to let you
avoid is where it goes**, because capacity that nobody assigns doesn't stay capacity. It dissipates,
and then it gets counted as a saving anyway.

Then two things specific to your role that no other track can close on. **You're the only person who
can answer where an agent goes in the org structure**, because you administer position management —
and it turns out a convention is emerging, and it is one you have already built. And **you're the
only role in this curriculum whose bet can include a subtraction**: turning something off is available
to you and to nobody else in the People function.

Finally the reckoning. Seven claims, seven answers, and what the pattern says about where your
judgement leans.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

1. Explain why unassigned capacity gets stranded `[V]`, and why the reinvestment commitment belongs
   inside the enablement decision rather than after it.
2. State the emerging convention for where an agent sits in an org structure `[V]`, and what breaks in
   your reporting when work moves to agents.
3. Write a bet with four terms, including the one only this role has.
4. Distinguish a bet from a migration using a single test.
5. Read your own delta across seven contested claims, and say what direction your judgement leans.

## Lesson 1 · Redeploy, don't strand **[V]**

The most instructive thing that has happened in this field is a reversal, and it is worth knowing in
detail because the shape of it is the lesson.

In **2023**, IBM's chief executive said AI would replace thousands of back-office roles. Back-office
hiring was frozen, and roughly 30% of some 26,000 non-customer-facing positions were described as
automatable within five years. The company then built one of the most effective HR service
deployments in existence, the AskHR agent this track cites in Modules 2 and 4, which now reportedly
settles around 94% of routine requests.

In **February 2026**, IBM announced it was **tripling entry-level hiring** — software engineering, HR,
across the board. The reasoning its CHRO gave wasn't complicated: if you stop investing in
entry-level hires, in three to five years there is no pipeline and the well dries up. The jobs were
rewritten rather than eliminated (entry-level engineers who had spent the bulk of their week writing
code moved toward client work and collaboration) and total employment went up rather than down.

**Read the lesson carefully, because the obvious reading is wrong.** This isn't a story about
automation failing. AskHR worked; this track relies on it twice. The story is about what happened to
the capacity:

> **Capacity you do not deliberately redeploy gets stranded, and the pipeline is the first thing you
> lose and the last thing you can rebuild.**

Everything else can be re-hired at market rate. A three-year gap in your junior intake can't be
closed by recruiting, because the people who would now be mid-level were never brought in.

**The industry-scale version, labelled honestly as a prediction rather than a finding.** In February
2026 Gartner predicted that **by 2027, half of the companies that attributed headcount reduction to AI
will rehire for similar functions under different titles.** Two caveats belong in the same breath: it
is a forecast, not an observation, and it concerns customer service rather than HR. And a third detail
worth carrying, because it cuts against the discourse: only around **20% of organizations reported
reducing agent headcount because of AI at all**: the majority held staffing flat while absorbing more
volume. So the pattern is less common than the headlines suggest, and among those who did it, the
predicted reversal rate is high.

**What this means for how you write an enablement decision.** Module 2 established that a kill
condition is nearly free to write before anyone is invested and nearly impossible afterwards. The
reinvestment commitment behaves identically:

> **Name where the capacity goes in the enablement decision itself, in the same document, at the same
> time.**

Not as a follow-up, not as a phase two. "We will redeploy the freed capacity to higher-value work" is
the sentence that means nobody has decided, and it will still mean that in eighteen months. Name the
work. Name whose it is.

## Lesson 2 · Where the agent goes on the chart **[V]**

Here's a question you'll be asked and nobody else in the People function can answer, because the
answer is a configuration decision in a system you administer.

It is already live, whether or not anyone has decided it. In a survey of more than 1,200 managers and
executives reported in mid-2026, about **31% said their leadership is already framing AI as a teammate
or an employee, and close to one in four said agents are formally listed on organizational or workflow
charts.** Somebody in your company may have put an agent on a chart already.

**And a convention is emerging. It is a good one, and you have already built it:**

> **An agent is not a person and does not occupy a headcount box. What goes on the chart is the
> accountable human owner — every agent reports to a named person who answers for its outputs.**

That should feel familiar, because it is **Module 1's accountability column and Module 7's signature,
arriving from the org-design direction.** The chart question resolves into the register question. If
you have done Modules 1 and 7, you have answered it, and you can say so in a meeting where everyone
else is speculating.

Four practical consequences, all of them yours:

**Do not create worker records for agents.** It breaks headcount, corrupts every ratio derived from
it, and creates a person-shaped object in your system of record with no employment relationship behind
it. Whatever short-term convenience it buys, you'll be unpicking it for years.

**Do record the agent as an asset with an owner.** It belongs in an inventory (your Module 1 register
is already one) not in the position-management structure. Where that inventory lives is a decision;
it is probably not the HCM.

**Do decide what happens when the owner leaves.** This is the offboarding cascade with a new node in
it, and it will happen. An agent whose accountable human has departed is exactly the condition Module
5 describes for employees whose manager left: nothing breaks, and nobody notices.

**And do notice what breaks in reporting.** Span of control, cost per employee, revenue per employee,
if work moved to agents and your denominators didn't change, **your ratios now measure something
different from what they measured last year**, and nobody is going to announce that. You're the person
who will be asked why the numbers moved.

**The honest limit:** this is genuinely unsettled, and the reporting question has no good answer yet.
What is available to you is to decide it deliberately and write down what you decided — so that when a
convention does arrive, you know what you did and can change it on purpose.

## Lesson 3 · The bet, and the fourth term

Every track in this curriculum closes on a bet with three terms. **Which change**, **what you would
measure at 90 days** (Module 2 built that baseline) and **what you would stop doing to fund it.**

You get a fourth, and it is yours alone:

> **What would you turn off?**

No other role in this curriculum can subtract. An HR business partner can't retire a process. A
recruiter can't disable an integration. A comp analyst can't remove a field. **You can**, and it is
frequently the better bet, because every capability you turn off is one that no longer has to be
reviewed, measured, logged for six months, disclosed, consulted on, or signed for. **Subtraction
reduces the surface that Modules 3 through 7 all have to govern.**

The first candidate is usually sitting in your own register: **the capability with the widest reach
and no named owner.** It is the highest-value thing you could switch off, it is normally available,
and nobody will fight for it precisely because nobody owns it.

Then the test that separates the two things this module is about:

> **Is there a state of the world in which you do not proceed?**

If the vendor is signed, the date is in the newsletter, the training is built and the announcement has
gone out, then you're describing a migration. That may be entirely correct (migrations are legitimate
and organizations need them) but **call it one.** The word "bet" implies a decision that could go
either way, and using it for something that can't is how a function gradually loses the ability to
stop anything. It is Module 2's *installation with optimistic paperwork*, arriving one level up and at
the end of the course.

## Lesson 4 · The seat you already have **[V]**

One last piece of data, and it resolves into something more useful than it first looks.

SHRM's *State of AI in HR 2026* found that in **52% of organizations, HR has no direct involvement in
overall AI strategy.** Read that against Module 1's finding from the same research (that HR technology
management is the second-highest AI adoption area in the function) and something odd falls out.

**The function is excluded from the strategy conversation, and its systems are where the AI actually
runs.**

Which means you're in every AI conversation your company has, at the implementation layer, whether or
not your CHRO is in the strategy one. That is a functional seat the function's own leadership
frequently doesn't have. And unlike a seat granted by an invitation, it comes with artifacts nobody
else can produce: the register, the evaluation plan, the signed decision-rights map, the exclusion
list.

**Which is what the function is for, after.** Not the back office. **The control surface**: the place
where the whole function's AI is actually governed, because it is the only place where it can be. Every
other track in this curriculum ends its legal module by telling its learner to find out who the
deployer is. You're the answer to that question, and it turns out to be a position of unusual leverage
rather than unusual exposure.

One honest note to close on, because Module 2 raised a problem this track never fully solved. You're
the person whose queue can demonstrate that another team's AI is generating rework. That doesn't get
politically easier with seniority. What changes it is the artifact: **a finding attached to a register
row with a named owner and a date is a different conversation from a complaint**, and producing that
document is the whole of what this track has been teaching.

> ### Try this — 3 minutes
> Open your Module 1 register and find the capability with the widest reach and no named owner. Ask
> yourself honestly whether anyone would object if it were switched off next Friday. If the answer is
> that nobody would notice, you have found your subtraction — and the strongest single item for the
> page you're about to write.

## Take a position

**The claim:** *"A bet you can't reverse isn't a bet, it's a migration. And the only person who can
tell the difference is the one who has performed the rollback."*

The strongest counter-argument is that **this valorizes reversibility well past the point where it is
useful, and most things worth doing are not reversible.**

An HCM implementation isn't reversible. A process redesign that retrains four thousand people isn't
reversible. The integration you retire isn't coming back, and neither is the institutional knowledge
of the process you removed: the people who knew how it worked will have moved on. If rehearsed
reversibility is the gate, you spend a career on small, safe, marginal changes and never do the thing
that would actually alter your function's cost structure. And organizations that genuinely transformed
their operations mostly did it through **commitments they could not walk back**, which is precisely
what made those commitments credible internally: the irreversibility was the signal that the
organization meant it.

On that reading, demanding a rehearsed rollback for everything is a sophisticated way of never being
accountable for a direction. It converts the systems function into a brake, which is the role it is
already suspected of playing.

Your position has to say **where the line falls** — which changes genuinely require reversibility, and
which are commitments that legitimately shouldn't have it. And notice what the counter-argument is
made of: **it is Module 1's own reversibility gate turned against the track's closing artifact.** If
reversibility is the gate on automating a decision, but irreversible commitment is what makes strategy
credible, then you need a principle that distinguishes them rather than a preference between them.

## Applied activity — "Stays on, changes, or comes off"

**Time:** 35 minutes · **Submit:** the one-page decision, the delta reckoning, and a 400–500 word
write-up · **Graded against the rubric below.** This is the course close. Score doesn't matter; the
reckoning is the point.

### Part 1 — The decision (15 min)

One page, for the capability your register has been tracking since Module 1. It has exactly three
permitted outcomes: **stays on, changes, or comes off.**

- **Which**, and the outcome.
- **The baseline**, from Module 2: the actual numbers and the query.
- **The 90-day measure**, and what result would count as a move.
- **The funding line**: what you'll stop doing to pay for it.
- **The reinvestment commitment**: where the capacity goes, named specifically enough that somebody
  could check in six months.
- **And the subtraction**: what comes off. If your answer is nothing, say why — but check the
  widest-reach unowned row in your register before you decide that.
- **The review date**, and who holds it.

### Part 2 — The delta reckoning (12 min)

Go back through Modules 1 to 7 and collect the seven claims you contested. For each one: **what you
committed at the time, and what you concluded after the module.**

Then answer three questions:

1. **How many of the seven turned out to be true of your organization?** Against your prediction at
   the top of this module.
2. **Which single claim moved you most**, and what specifically moved it: a number you looked up, a
   blank you couldn't fill, a test result you didn't expect.
3. **And the one that matters: which direction does your judgement lean?** Did you consistently
   assume your organization was in better shape than the evidence showed, or worse? Nearly everybody
   is systematically off in one direction, and knowing which is more useful than any individual
   answer.

### Part 3 — The write-up (400–500 words)

Your position on this module's claim, addressing where the line falls between a change that needs
reversibility and a commitment that legitimately shouldn't have it. What the delta pattern says about
the direction of your own judgement. And the last thing: **the one artifact from this track you'll
actually keep maintaining** (the register, the evaluation plan, the signed map, or the exclusion list)
and what would have to be true for it still to be current in a year. Only one. A commitment to
maintain all four is a commitment to maintain none.
