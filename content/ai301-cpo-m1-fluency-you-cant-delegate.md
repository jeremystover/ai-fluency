## Calibration prompt — before you start

*One claim. Commit before you read.*

> **"In the last month I personally used AI on the judgment parts of my own job, not to draft
> communications or summarize a document, but on a decision I own."**

**True of us, or not true of us?** Commit in a sentence, and name the decision if there was one.

Then: **how many times in the last month?** A count, not an impression. Your own history will tell
you if you go and look.

Most people in this seat answer this with a number they have never checked, and the checking is the
first act of the practice this module is about.

## Module brief

You can already use the tools. **This series is about what to decide.**

That is the positioning and it is also a promise about what is excluded, which is worth stating in
the first minute so you know what you aren't paying for. **No AI fundamentals. No prompting primer.
No tool tour. No vendor showcase. No "three types of AI."** Those are commodity, they are freely
available, and if you need them, 101 and 201 exist and will do it better than a course pretending
to serve executives.

**The exclusions are the product.** Most of what is sold to this audience spends half its runtime on
remedial content, because it is written for a broad HR population and priced for a senior one. One
widely-sold senior credential includes a hands-on lab covering AI music creation. Whatever its
merits for someone else, no sitting Chief People Officer will finish it believing it was built for
them.

So this track organizes around **the decisions the role has to make**, not around AI capabilities,
and not around HR functional domains. Six modules, six decisions, and each one produces something
you have written down and would defend.

And it starts here, with the only module that is about you personally, because of a dependency that
doesn't go away: **you cannot credibly lead a transformation you have not performed.**

Not "shouldn't." *Can't* — because the specific thing an executive is being asked to supply is
judgment about where this technology is reliable, and that judgment isn't transferable from a
briefing. You can be told that models are confidently wrong. Knowing what it feels like when one is
confidently wrong *about something you know well* is a different piece of knowledge, and it is the
one you need in the room.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

1. State the credibility chain and say honestly what evidence supports it.
2. Describe what executive-altitude AI practice looks like, and how it differs from delegated use.
3. Explain why delegated fluency produces a specific failure at the top of an organization.
4. Apply the track-record standard to your own use, rather than a confidence rating.
5. Establish one recurring personal practice and report what it surfaced.

## Lesson 1 · The credibility chain, argued from craft **[V]**

The chain has three links: **personal fluency → functional transformation → enterprise standing.**

Each one enables the next. You can't redesign your function's work without knowing what the tools
actually do. Module 4 will ask you to make a cut that requires exactly that judgment. And you
can't hold a position in a C-suite argument about enterprise AI without having transformed your own
function, because Module 3's scope fight is won by demonstrated competence and lost by assertion.

**Now the evidence, handled honestly, because this track's whole proposition is that you're the
most skeptical person in the room.**

There is a survey finding that points the right way: HR professionals reporting transformation in
their core processes were **nearly three times as likely** to say AI had made them better leaders —
70% against 25%. It is from a vendor's 2026 study of **264 HR professionals in their own community
survey**, released July 2026, and it is cross-sectional.

That is three problems in one citation. **A small self-selected sample. A publisher with an interest
in the result. And a correlation with no direction**, leaders who were already good at this are
plausibly the ones who transformed their processes, which would produce the identical finding.

So this module doesn't lead on it. **The argument stands on the mechanism, not the number**, and
the mechanism is the paragraph above: judgment about reliability isn't transferable from a
briefing. If you find that argument unconvincing, the survey won't rescue it, and you should say
so in the activity.

Notice what just happened, because it is the standard this track holds itself to and the one Module
6 will hold vendors to. A number that supports our case was available. Using it would have been
easy and slightly dishonest. **We stated its sample and set it aside** — and if we had led with it,
you would have been right to discount everything after.

## Lesson 2 · What executive-altitude practice actually is

Not a tool demo. Not a productivity habit. **A standing practice aimed at your own judgment.**

The distinction that matters: most executive AI use is *delegated drafting*. You or someone near
you uses a model to produce a communication, a summary, a first pass at a document. That is useful
and it isn't this. It develops no judgment, because the output is something you were going to
review anyway.

Three uses that are genuinely at your altitude, and they share a property: **the model is aimed at
your reasoning rather than your output.**

**Rehearsal before a decision you own.** Take the position you're about to argue (the
restructure, the policy, the recommendation to the board) commit to it explicitly, and require the
model to build the strongest case against it. Not a balanced view. The strongest case. Then ask what
the three objections are that you haven't anticipated. The value isn't the model's opinion, which
is worth little. It is that **you have to state your position precisely enough to be attacked**,
which most executive reasoning never has to survive.

**Pattern-finding across your own record.** Your calendar, your decisions, your notes, your meeting
transcripts if you keep them (held somewhere private and appropriate) read for patterns you can't
see from inside. What kinds of decisions do you defer? Which relationships get your time and which
get the leftovers? Where did you say you would follow up and not? An executive's self-knowledge is
mostly anecdote, and this is the first cheap instrument for it.

**Testing your own reasoning on something you know cold.** Take a subject where you're genuinely
expert and interrogate the model on it. This is the single fastest way to develop calibrated
skepticism, because **you'll catch it being confidently wrong in your own domain**, and that
experience isn't substitutable. Every executive who has had it stops needing to be warned about
hallucination, and starts asking better procurement questions.

The common thread: none of these produce a deliverable. All of them produce judgment, and judgment
is the thing you're actually paid for.

> ### Try this — 3 minutes
> Take a decision you made in the last month that you're confident about. State it to a model in
> one sentence, then ask for the strongest case that you got it wrong. Read the response for
> whether it names an objection you had genuinely not considered. Most executives find one within
> the first attempt, and the finding is not about the model.

## Lesson 3 · The delegation trap

Here's the specific failure this module exists to prevent, and it is a failure of executives rather
than of tools.

The natural move for someone in your seat is to delegate. It is what the role is. So AI fluency gets
delegated too: to a chief of staff, to an HRIS lead, to whoever seems technical. They learn the
tools. They bring you recommendations. You make decisions on their summaries.

**That produces confident wrongness at the top of the house, and it is the most expensive place for
it.**

Two mechanisms.

**You lose the ability to interrogate what you're told.** When your analytics lead says a model can
predict flight risk with useful accuracy, the question that matters isn't whether they are honest (
they are) but what the model is actually doing, what it was trained on, and what happens to the
people it is wrong about. **Asking that question well requires having been wrong yourself, in a way
you noticed.** Otherwise you're choosing between competing assertions on the basis of who seems
most confident, which is how organizations buy things that don't work.

**And your own errors stop being corrected.** Everyone else in the organization has someone above
them who might catch a mistaken belief about what AI can do. You don't. A CPO who is wrong about
this is wrong in a room where nobody is positioned to say so — and the belief propagates downward
into procurement, into policy, into what your function commits to.

There is a version of this argument that goes too far, and the module should name it before you do.
**Nobody expects a CFO to do the accounting.** Executives lead functions whose craft they don't
personally perform, all the time, and demanding otherwise would be absurd.

The distinction is that a CFO's judgment about accounting rests on a professional apparatus (
audits, standards, credentials, regulators) that produces reliable second-hand knowledge. **No such
apparatus exists here yet.** Vendor claims are unaudited, benchmarks are self-reported, and the
category is two years old. Until that infrastructure exists, first-hand knowledge is doing work that
it won't always have to do, which is a genuinely time-limited argument, and this module says so
rather than pretending the principle is eternal.

## Lesson 4 · The standard this track holds you to

One methodological commitment, stated here because it governs every activity that follows.

**Never "how confident are you?" Always "what did you actually do last quarter?"**

Self-reported confidence about AI is worthless as a measure. It correlates with exposure to
marketing, with temperament, and with how recently someone saw an impressive demo. It doesn't
correlate with capability, and at senior levels it is systematically inflated because admitting
uncertainty in this domain currently reads as being behind.

**Behaviour is different.** What you did is checkable, by you, against a record. It doesn't flatter
and it doesn't require you to be honest with yourself in the abstract, only to go and look.

So every module in this track opens with a claim about your organization that you can go and verify,
and every activity produces an artifact rather than a reflection. Where the answer is *"we can't
check that"* (and it will be, more than once) **that is a finding about your function and it is
graded as one.** A CPO who discovers that nobody can say who has access to human development
support has learned something more valuable than the number would have been.

Which is also the answer to the first module's activity. It doesn't ask what you think about AI. It
asks you to start one practice and report what it surfaced.

## Key takeaways

- **You can already use the tools. This series is about what to decide** — and the exclusions are
  the product: no fundamentals, no prompting primer, no tool tour, no vendor showcase.
- **The credibility chain: personal fluency → functional transformation → enterprise standing.**
  Each link enables the next, and the argument rests on the mechanism rather than the survey.
- **The supporting evidence is weak and this module says so** `[V]`: 70% against 25%, from 264
  self-selected respondents in a vendor's own community, cross-sectional. Stated and set aside.
- **Executive-altitude practice aims the model at your reasoning, not your output**: rehearsal
  before a decision you own, pattern-finding across your own record, and testing it on something you
  know cold.
- **Catching it being confidently wrong in your own domain is not substitutable**, and it is what
  turns a briefing into judgment.
- **Delegated fluency produces confident wrongness at the top**, where nobody is positioned to
  correct it: and where it propagates into procurement, policy, and commitments.
- **The CFO objection is real and time-limited.** Second-hand knowledge works where an audit
  apparatus exists. Here it doesn't yet.
- **The standard: never "how confident are you," always "what did you actually do."** And where you
  can't check, the inability to check is the finding.

## Take a position

**The claim:** *"You can't credibly lead a transformation you haven't personally performed."*

The strongest counter-argument is that **this confuses craft with leadership, and it is exactly the
confusion that keeps executives busy on the wrong things.** CEOs lead finance transformations
without closing the books. The scarce resource in your seat is attention, and an hour spent on a
personal AI practice is an hour not spent on Module 3's scope fight, which is the decision that
actually determines whether your function has a role in this. On that view, personal fluency is a
comfortable substitute for a political fight, and it feels like progress precisely because it is
easier.

There is a sharper edge. **The evidence offered for the chain runs both ways**, and the module
concedes it: leaders who were already effective are plausibly the ones who transformed their
functions, in which case personal fluency is a *symptom* of the thing that matters rather than a
cause of it — and prescribing the symptom is a category error.

Your position has to survive both, and in particular it has to say what you would cut from your own
month to make room for the practice, since that is the actual decision rather than whether the
principle is appealing.

## Applied activity — "The standing practice"

**Time:** 30 minutes to set up, then the practice itself · **Submit:** the practice plus a 300–400
word write-up · **Graded against the rubric below.** Score doesn't matter. Doing the work is where
the learning lands.

**Establish one recurring personal practice, run it at least twice, and report what it surfaced.**

**Step 1. Choose one (5 min).** From Lesson 2, or your own, provided it aims at your reasoning
rather than your output. Rehearsal before a decision you own. Pattern-finding across your own
record. Interrogating it on something you know cold. **One.** A list of three you intend to start is
worth nothing here.

**Step 2. Make it recurring (5 min).** Name the trigger — a standing slot, or an event that
reliably occurs, like "before any decision that goes to the exec team." A practice without a trigger
is an intention, and you have enough of those.

**Step 3. Run it, twice (the real time cost).** Not once. The first run is novelty; the second is
where you find out whether it survives your actual week.

**Step 4. Report what it surfaced (15 min).** Specifically. Not "it was useful" — **the objection
you had not anticipated, the pattern in your own record you had not seen, the thing it got
confidently wrong in your own domain.** One concrete finding, described precisely enough that
somebody could disagree with your interpretation of it.

**Step 5 — Score the claim (5 min).** Your opening count against what your history actually showed.
Direction and cause.

Then the write-up: the practice and its trigger, the concrete thing it surfaced, your position on
the claim above with its counter-argument addressed, including **what you would cut to make room**
— and, honestly, whether you expect this to survive the next eight weeks. **A learner who runs it
twice, finds it does not survive contact with their calendar, and says precisely why, has produced a
more useful submission than one who reports enthusiasm.**
