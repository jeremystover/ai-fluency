## Calibration prompt — before you start

*One claim. Commit to it before you read anything, and be honest — nobody sees this but you.*

> **"More than half of what my function published last year could be rebuilt to an acceptable
> standard, in a weekend, by a competent person outside L&D using current AI tools."**

**True of us, or not true of us?** Commit, in a sentence, and say what you're basing it on.

Then, if you can put a number on it: **what percentage of your portfolio falls on the "yes,
rebuildable" side?** You'll check that number against your actual catalog in the activity, and the
gap between the two is the point.

If you genuinely cannot check it — no catalog inventory, no way to see what shipped last year —
**write that down instead.** That is a finding about your function, not a failure to do the
exercise, and it is graded as one.

## Module brief

Here is the uncomfortable shape of this course, stated in the first three minutes so you can decide
whether to trust it.

Learning and development is the third-highest concentration of AI use cases in HR — behind
recruiting and HR technology, ahead of everything else — and roughly seven in ten L&D professionals
report already experimenting with or integrating AI `[V]`. That is not a warning about a coming
disruption. It is a description of a change that has already happened, and it landed on the part of
this work its practitioners were proudest of.

Which produces a diagnosis that runs opposite to the one every other People function gets. The HR
business partner's course opens by telling them their core territory is the *least*-penetrated part
of HR, and that the anxiety they've been sold is mostly manufactured. That is true, and it is not
true here. **Your core craft is the most AI-saturated work in the function.**

So this module does two jobs that are, for this role, one job: the diagnosis and the map. The
finding is the same object seen twice.

**L&D work runs a chain.** A request arrives. Someone diagnoses it. Someone designs. Someone
produces. Someone delivers. Something transfers, or doesn't. Someone tries to prove it. Seven
stages, and your hours have always concentrated in the middle three while your value has always
concentrated at the two ends.

**AI collapsed the middle.** Which means the two things this field has historically been worst at —
deciding what is worth building, and proving it did anything — are now the entire job.

And there is a cost to that nobody has named, which is what Module 2 is about: the expensive middle
was doing work nobody counted. It was the reason bad training requests died.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

1. Locate any piece of L&D work on the seven-stage chain, and explain why the value sits at the ends
   while the hours sit in the middle.
2. Sort a real portfolio with three questions — cheap now, ours only, needs a human on the hook —
   and defend the boundaries.
3. Explain what the second bucket actually depends on, and what would have to become true for it to
   shrink.
4. Name what this track will not teach you, and where that material already lives.
5. Produce a triage of your own portfolio with a percentage in each bucket and one named program you
   would kill.

## Lesson 1 · The chain, and where the value went

Not a maturity model. Just the sequence work actually moves through.

**Request → diagnosis → design → production → delivery → transfer → evidence.**

- **Request.** Someone asks for something. A manager, a function head, an exec, a post-incident
  debrief.
- **Diagnosis.** Deciding whether this is a training problem at all, and what the actual performance
  gap is.
- **Design.** Objectives, assessment, sequencing, practice, spacing, transfer strategy. The
  discipline with a real research base.
- **Production.** Slides, modules, scripts, job aids, facilitator guides, video, assessments.
- **Delivery.** Live, virtual, asynchronous, blended.
- **Transfer.** Whether anything changes on the job.
- **Evidence.** Whether you can show it did.

Now put your hours against it. For nearly every L&D function that has ever been timed, the mass sits
in **design and production** — and production alone is usually the largest single block in the role.

And put your *value* against it. What did the function contribute that nobody else could? Two
things, and they sit at the ends: **the judgment that this request should or shouldn't become a
program**, and **the evidence that it worked.** The middle is craft — real, learnable, and, it turns
out, the part a general-purpose tool got good at first.

**That is the barbell, and it is not a metaphor imposed on the role.** It is what the timesheet says
once you sort it. Everything else in this track hangs off it: Module 2 is the left end, Module 6 is
the right end, and Modules 3 through 5 are what stops you from getting hurt in between.

> ### Try this — 3 minutes
> Take the last thing your team shipped. Say out loud how many hours went into producing it, and how
> many went into deciding it should exist. If the second number is smaller than the first by more
> than a factor of ten, you have found the shape of your function without needing the rest of the
> lesson.

## Lesson 2 · The inversion **[V]**

The reason this track cannot open the way the others do.

**Where AI actually landed in HR.** In SHRM's *State of AI in HR 2026* — a survey of 1,908 HR
professionals fielded in December 2025 — reported AI use concentrates in recruiting (27%), HR
technology (21%), **learning and development (17%)**, and employee experience (14%). L&D is third
of sixteen practice areas, and the three ahead of or alongside it are all places where the work is
transactional and countable.

**And L&D's own adoption runs ahead of the function's.** LinkedIn's *Workplace Learning Report 2025*
put roughly **71% of L&D professionals** experimenting with or integrating AI into their work.
Whatever else is true, this is not a population that needs persuading to try it.

Hold those two together and the diagnosis writes itself. The business partner is told: *your core is
the least-penetrated part of HR, the pressure is real but the panic is sold to you.* You get the
opposite. **The saturation is in your craft, and you brought it there yourselves, enthusiastically,
because it worked.**

Two honest qualifications, because a module that asks you to interrogate evidence has to survive its
own standard:

**"Use cases" is not "work replaced."** The SHRM figure counts where organizations report applying
AI, not what proportion of L&D work is now machine-made. A function with ten AI use cases and two
hundred people is counted the same as one that restructured. The figure establishes concentration,
not depth.

**And self-reported experimentation is a weak measure.** "Experimenting with or integrating" is a
generous bucket. Someone who used a model to draft one email is inside it.

So the honest version of the claim is narrower than the headline and still sufficient: **among the
sixteen things HR does, this is one of the three where AI has landed hardest, and this profession
adopted it faster than its neighbours.** You do not need the strong version for the rest of the
track to hold.

## Lesson 3 · The three questions

The triage. Run every piece of your portfolio through them, in order.

**Question 1 — Is it cheap now?** Could a competent person outside your function, with current
tools and access to your source material, rebuild this to an acceptable standard in a weekend?

Note what the question does *not* ask. Not "is it good." Not "did it take you six weeks." Whether it
was expensive to make is a fact about the past. **The question is what it costs to replace, now.**

Most people find this bucket lands above half, and the number is not the lesson. The lesson is what
you do about it — which is Module 6's problem, and you cannot get there without the number.

**Question 2 — Is it ours only?** Could anyone outside this organization make it? This is the bucket
that depends on something only you hold: your actual processes, your real incidents, your specific
systems, your regulatory posture, the way decisions genuinely get made here. A compliance module on
general anti-harassment principles is not in this bucket. One built around three anonymized
incidents that actually happened in your business, with your escalation path, is.

**Question 3 — Does it need a human on the hook?** Not "would a human do it better" — whether
someone has to be *accountable in the room*. Development conversations where the point is the
relationship. Facilitation where the value is that a senior person showed up. Anything where
somebody has to be answerable for a judgment about a named individual.

**Order matters.** Run 1 first and it will swallow work that belongs in 2 or 3, because almost
anything looks rebuildable if you only consider the artifact. Run 3 first and everything looks
human-critical, because everyone believes their work needs them. The discipline is to run 1 as
harshly as you can, then rescue from it only what genuinely answers 2 or 3.

## Lesson 4 · What the second bucket rests on, and what this course won't teach

**Bucket two is the one people argue about**, so here is the argument, stated as an argument rather
than a fact.

The material in bucket two is protected by a specific property: it is **undocumented**. Your
escalation path in practice rather than on the intranet, why the 2023 reorg makes one team's
onboarding different, which manager behaviours actually get someone promoted here. A model cannot
supply it because nobody wrote it down.

That is a real mechanism, and it is worth being precise about what it does and doesn't promise.
It does *not* say bucket two is safe because the judgment involved is too subtle. It says the
information is **absent from the record**. Those are very different claims, and the second one has a
condition attached: **it holds exactly as long as the information stays undocumented.**

Which means the thing that would most shrink bucket two is not a better model. It is your own
organization getting better at capturing how it actually works — which is something you might
otherwise think of as an unambiguously good idea, and which Module 6 will ask you to have a position
on. Take one now: *what would have to become true for bucket two to halve?* If your answer is "a
much better model," you have not engaged the mechanism.

**And what this course will not teach you**, stated plainly because the exclusions are the product.

You have finished 201. That course already taught document pipelines — chaining a long input into
structured outputs with checkpoints, whose worked example is literally a transcript becoming a
structured summary. **Building an e-learning module from a SME interview is that.** It also taught
the context pack: reusable standing material so you stop re-explaining your world. **A course
style-and-standards pack is that.** And it taught the operating rhythm: cadence, measurement,
retirement criteria. **"Your L&D calendar as workflows" is that.**

Three modules a Talent Development course would obviously reach for, and all three are already
yours. A 300-level course that quietly re-teaches the 200-level is charging you twice for the same
material and betting you won't notice. If you skipped 201, the honest instruction is to go take it
rather than accept a thin summary here.

What survives that subtraction is the rest of this track, and none of it is production.

## Key takeaways

- **The chain is request → diagnosis → design → production → delivery → transfer → evidence.** Your
  hours concentrate in the middle. Your value concentrates at the ends. That gap is the whole track.
- **AI collapsed the middle**, which promotes the two things this field has historically been worst
  at — deciding what to build, and proving it worked — into the entire job.
- **L&D is the third-highest AI use-case concentration in HR at 17%, and ~71% of L&D professionals
  report experimenting or integrating** `[V]`. The diagnosis here is the opposite of the business
  partner's: the saturation is in your craft.
- **But "use cases" is not "work replaced," and self-reported experimentation is a generous
  bucket.** The narrow version of the claim is enough.
- **Three questions, in order: cheap now / ours only / needs a human on the hook.** Run question one
  as harshly as you can, then rescue only what genuinely answers two or three.
- **Bucket two is protected because the information is undocumented — not because the judgment is
  subtle.** That protection lasts exactly as long as the documentation gap does.
- **Production with AI is 201 M3. A standards pack is 201 M2. An L&D calendar of workflows is 201
  M7.** This track teaches none of them, on purpose.

## Take a position

**The claim:** *"The part of your job you were proudest of is the part that just became cheap."*

The strongest counter-argument is that **rebuildable is not the same as replaceable, and the triage
may be measuring the wrong thing.** A competent outsider with good tools can reproduce the artifact
— the deck, the module, the job aid — and reproduce almost none of what made it land. The political
read on why the program had to be framed *this* way rather than the obviously better way. The scope
that was negotiated down in a room. The credibility of the person who delivered it, which is why
anyone showed up. On that view, question one measures **artifact complexity** and quietly calls it
value, and a function that triages on it will cheerfully surrender the things that were working for
reasons the triage cannot see.

That argument is strong enough that it changes what an honest bucket-one number means. Your position
has to say whether the triage survives it — and if you think it does, what specifically stops
question one from mistaking "hard to make" for "valuable."

## Applied activity — "The triage"

**Time:** 30 minutes · **Submit:** the triage plus a 300–400 word write-up · **Graded against the
rubric below.** Score doesn't matter. Doing the work is where the learning lands.

Take your **actual portfolio** — what your function published, ran, or maintained over the last
twelve months. Not the plan. The catalog.

*If you are an individual contributor without portfolio authority:* run this on the programs you
personally own or maintain, and in Step 4 name the person who would have to agree rather than making
the call yourself. Naming the decision-maker is graded as the equivalent of making the decision.

**Step 1 — Inventory (8 min).** List what exists. Titles and rough scale are enough — you are not
building an asset register. If you cannot produce this list, stop and write down why: **an L&D
function that cannot enumerate its own catalog has found something more important than this
exercise, and it should be the write-up.**

**Step 2 — Run the three questions (10 min).** In order. Cheap now / ours only / needs a human on
the hook. Every item lands in exactly one bucket — force the choice, because the forcing is where
the judgment happens. Give a percentage for each bucket.

**Step 3 — Defend two boundaries (5 min).** Pick the two items you found hardest to place and say
why they went where they did. These are worth more than the eighty items you placed instantly.

**Step 4 — Name the kill (5 min).** **One program you would stop.** Not pause, not refresh — stop.
Say what happens to the need it was serving, and who would object. A triage that ends without a
named casualty has not been run.

**Step 5 — Score the claim (2 min).** Your opening position, against what the inventory actually
shows. If you committed a percentage, compare it to the real one and account for the gap. Direction
and cause, not accuracy.

Then the write-up: what the distribution shows, the two hard boundaries and your reasoning, the
program you would kill and what happens to its need, your position on the claim above with its
counter-argument addressed, and — if your opening position turned out wrong — **what you were
reading instead of the catalog when you formed it.**

## Knowledge check — 8 questions

*Unlocks after the applied activity is submitted. Retakes are free and unlimited.*

**Q1.** Why does this track's diagnosis run opposite to the HR business partner track's?

- A. Because L&D professionals are more anxious about AI than business partners are
- B. Because the business partner's core territory is among the least AI-penetrated parts of HR, while L&D's core craft is among the most — so the reassuring version of the diagnosis would be false here ✓
- C. Because L&D has more budget exposure than the business partner function
- D. Because business partners were taught this material in 201 and L&D professionals were not

> **B.** The shape of the finding is the same in both tracks — where has AI actually landed — and it
> points in opposite directions. Telling this audience "you're not behind, calm down" would be
> comfortable and untrue.

**Q2.** What does the seven-stage chain reveal that a list of L&D responsibilities does not?

- A. Which stages require the most specialized training to perform
- B. That the hours concentrate in the middle stages while the distinctive value concentrates at the two ends — so collapsing the middle promotes the ends to being the whole job ✓
- C. That delivery is the stage most exposed to automation
- D. That the stages must be performed in strict sequence to be effective

> **B.** A responsibilities list is unordered and hides the asymmetry. Only a sequence shows that
> time and value sit in different places.

**Q3.** In the triage, what does question one — "is it cheap now?" — actually ask?

- A. Whether the item was inexpensive to produce originally
- B. Whether the item is low quality
- C. What it would cost to replace the item today, regardless of what it cost to make ✓
- D. Whether the item is still being used by the business

> **C.** What it cost you is a fact about the past. The triage is about replacement cost now, which
> is why "but that took my team six weeks" is not an answer to it.

**Q4.** Why does the module insist the three questions be run in order?

- A. Because the buckets must be equally sized for the analysis to work
- B. Because running question three first makes everything look human-critical, and running question one first without the later rescues would swallow work that genuinely belongs in two or three ✓
- C. Because questions two and three are harder and should be attempted when fresh
- D. Because the order matches the sequence of the chain in Lesson 1

> **B.** Both failure modes are real and they point in opposite directions. The discipline is a harsh
> question one followed by honest rescues, not a gentle pass through all three.

**Q5.** What actually protects the material in bucket two?

- A. That the judgment involved is too subtle for a model to reproduce
- B. That it is legally restricted from being processed by AI systems
- C. That the information it depends on is undocumented — absent from any record a model could have been trained on ✓
- D. That it changes too frequently for a model's knowledge cutoff

> **C.** And the distinction matters enormously, because the undocumented-information claim comes
> with a condition: it holds only while the information stays undocumented. The subtlety claim would
> be permanent, and it is not the one the module makes.

**Q6.** According to the module, what would most shrink bucket two?

- A. A substantially more capable model
- B. Your own organization getting better at documenting how it actually works ✓
- C. Vendors gaining access to more industry-specific training data
- D. A reduction in the regulatory constraints on AI in employment

> **B.** Which is uncomfortable, because that is normally considered an unambiguously good thing to
> do. A is the answer that skips the mechanism — the constraint is the record, not the reasoning.

**Q7.** Why does the module state what the track will not teach?

- A. To set expectations about the time commitment involved
- B. Because those topics are covered in optional supplementary material
- C. Because building content with AI is 201 M3, a standards pack is 201 M2, and a calendar of workflows is 201 M7 — a 300-level course that re-teaches them is charging twice for the same material ✓
- D. Because production topics are outside the scope of any 301 track

> **C.** They are the three most obvious modules a Talent Development course would reach for, which
> is exactly why they need naming. D is wrong: other tracks teach production-adjacent craft where the
> role makes it specialist.

**Q8.** A learner reports they cannot produce an inventory of their own catalog. How should that be treated?

- A. As a failure to complete the activity, which should be resubmitted once the list exists
- B. As a finding about the function that is worth more than the exercise, and which should become the write-up ✓
- C. As a reason to run the triage on a hypothetical portfolio instead
- D. As evidence that the function is too small for the triage to apply

> **B.** A function that cannot enumerate what it publishes has learned something structural about
> itself. The activity is a means of finding things out; when the means fails informatively, the
> finding is the output.
