## Module brief

Most legal training in this field teaches you the rules. This module teaches the **stack** — because
the rules here move at three different speeds, and **advice built on the wrong layer expires without
telling you.**

That sounds abstract. Here is what it costs.

A practitioner who learned in 2024 that intrusive electronic monitoring was presumptively unlawful is
**wrong today**, because the memorandum saying so was withdrawn. A practitioner who concludes from
that withdrawal that the exposure is gone is **also wrong**, because the statute never moved. Both
errors come from the same failure: not knowing which layer the proposition was sitting on.

And a live demonstration, which is the reason this module exists in this form. **The outline for this
module was written when the Board could not overturn its own precedent. It was drafted five days after
it could.** Nothing about the law's content changed in between. The composition did, and that is
sufficient. You are reading a module whose own subject moved during its production, and the dates
below are the only honest way to tell you so.

Five lessons. The stack, the thing that just moved, the duty to bargain over AI, what unions have
already won by contract, and the part that applies whether or not anyone in your workplace is
represented.

## Lesson 1 · The stack **[V]**

Five kinds of authority. They are not equally durable, and the difference is not a technicality.

### Statute — decades

**Section 7 of the National Labor Relations Act** protects employees who act together for mutual aid
or protection. **Sections 8(a)(5) and 8(d)** require an employer to give notice and a genuine
opportunity to bargain before changing wages, hours, or other terms and conditions of employment — the
*mandatory subjects*. And the Supreme Court's 1975 *Weingarten* decision holds that a represented
employee may request their union representative's presence at an investigatory interview they
reasonably believe may result in discipline.

**None of this has been touched by any of the last three years.** Not by AI, not by an
administration, not by a rescinded memorandum. When you are looking for something to build on, build
on this.

### Board doctrine — flips with composition

The National Labor Relations Board decides what the statute means in application, and **it changes its
mind when its membership changes.** This is not a defect anyone is hiding; it is how the agency works.

The example you need is the one that governs whether your management-rights clause covers an AI
deployment. In **2019**, in *MV Transportation*, the Board adopted a **contract coverage** test:
roughly, if the change fell within the compass of a clause the parties had negotiated, the employer
could act. In **December 2024**, in **_Endurance Environmental Solutions_ (373 NLRB No. 141, decided
10 December 2024, Case 09–CA–273873)**, the Board overruled it and restored **clear and unmistakable
waiver** — the standard it had applied for some seventy years before 2019 — and applied the
restoration **retroactively.**

**Now read what that case was actually about, because it is your case.** A waste-hauling company
installed video cameras in a fleet of roughly **400 trucks** to monitor employees, without bargaining
with the Teamsters local. Its defence was the management-rights clause, which reserved the right to
*"implement changes in equipment."*

> **That clause was not enough.**

Go and read your own management-rights clause. It very probably contains a phrase of about that
generality — equipment, methods, technology, systems, the means of operation. **A Board has held that
this class of language does not clearly and unmistakably waive the duty to bargain over installing
monitoring technology on people.** Your monitoring technology is more capable than a dash camera, and
your clause is not more specific.

### General Counsel priority — fastest of all, and rescindable overnight

The Board's General Counsel decides which theories the agency's prosecuting arm will advance. That is
real — it determines whether a charge gets a complaint — and it is **the least durable layer there is.**

**GC 23-02**, issued 31 October 2022, was titled *Electronic Monitoring and Algorithmic Management of
Employees Interfering with the Exercise of Section 7 Rights*. It set out an intention to urge the
Board to find intrusive monitoring and automated management presumptively unlawful where it tends to
interfere with protected activity. For two years it was the most-cited document in this field.

**It was rescinded on 14 February 2025**, in GC 25-05, among **29 memoranda withdrawn at once**, with
the agency's case backlog cited as a reason.

Two conclusions, and you need both:

- **A memorandum is a prosecutorial theory, not a right.** Its withdrawal removed the theory. **Section
  7 did not move.** The conduct GC 23-02 described is still capable of interfering with protected
  activity; what changed is who will bring the case and when.
- **Anything you learned from a GC memorandum has a shelf life measured in administrations.** If a
  proposition in your head traces to one, it is not knowledge. It is a weather report.

### And two more layers people routinely mistake for law

**Rulemaking.** The Board can also issue regulations, which move on a different clock again and are
undone by different mechanisms — litigation, a successor administration's rulemaking, Congress. A rule
is not a decision, and commentary frequently reports the two as though they were interchangeable.
When a secondary source tells you "the Board changed X," find out which instrument it used before you
rely on the report.

**Contract.** In a represented workplace, the collective bargaining agreement binds you **regardless of
who runs the Board.** This is the layer Lesson 4 is about, and it is the one most often mistaken in
both directions: practitioners describe a contract term as though it were the law, and — more
expensively — assume that because something is not the law, they do not owe it.

> **Five layers. The question to ask about any proposition is not "is this true?" It is "what is this
> true *of*, and what would have to happen for it to stop being true?"**

## Lesson 2 · The layer that just moved **[V]**

This lesson has more dates in it than any other in the ladder. That is the point of it, and the dates
are the content.

**The Board lost the ability to decide cases.** Member Gwynne Wilcox was removed on **27 January
2025**, leaving the Board below the three-member quorum it needs to issue decisions. It stayed there
for roughly **eleven months**. Regional offices kept processing charges and running elections; **no
Board-level decision issued at all.**

**Then it came back.** Scott Mayer and James Murphy were confirmed on **18 December 2025** and the
Board issued its first published decisions since the lapse in **January 2026**, restoring quorum with
David Prouty as the sole remaining member of the previous majority. General Counsel **Crystal Carey**
now runs the prosecuting side.

**And then it declined to change anything.** On **28 January 2026**, in *Lodi Volunteer Ambulance
Rescue Squad*, the new members declined to express a view on a Biden-era remedial precedent. The
reason is a piece of institutional practice worth carrying:

> **The Board's unwritten convention is that it does not reverse precedent without three votes for
> doing so.**

With two Republican members and one Democrat, the votes were not there. Commentary through the first
half of 2026 read this as stability, and for six months it was.

**And then, five days before this module was drafted, the condition was satisfied.** On **7 August
2026** the Senate confirmed **James Macy** and **David Prouty**, producing a **3–1 Republican
majority** with one Democratic seat vacant and James Murphy as Chairman. The three-vote condition that
made reversal futile in January is now met.

**Three consequences you can act on today.**

**One: the composition that decided _Endurance_ no longer exists, and the standard is in play.** The
clear-and-unmistakable waiver standard was restored by a majority none of whom now sit. Commentators
expect the new majority to seek reversal of employee-favourable standards, and the unilateral-change
standard is named among them. **This does not mean the standard has changed.** As of the date on this
module it is the law and it applies retroactively. It means: **do not treat it as a stable planning
assumption in either direction**, and re-check it before you rely on it.

**Two — and this is the trap.** If the standard flips back to contract coverage, that is good news for
an employer *prospectively* and tells you nothing about a change you made in 2025. Doctrine has been
applied retroactively in exactly this line of cases, in both directions. **A change you implement now,
under a standard you expect to loosen, is judged under whatever standard exists when the case is
decided.** Betting on a reversal is not a legal position.

**Three: there was no forum for eleven months, and now there is one.** Charges filed during the lapse
did not evaporate; they queued. A Board working through a backlog will reach conduct from 2025. If your
organisation implemented monitoring, scoring, or automated performance management during that window on
the reasoning that nothing was happening at the Board — **something is happening at the Board now.**

> **Counsel gate.** If your organisation made a unilateral change touching a represented population at
> any point since January 2025, that is a conversation with labor counsel and it is not urgent-adjacent,
> it is just urgent. Bring the implementation date, the clause you relied on, what notice was given,
> and to whom.

## Lesson 3 · The duty to bargain over AI **[V]**

Settled doctrine, unsettled application — and **the distinction is the lesson**, because almost
everything written about this collapses it.

**What is settled.** A change to wages, hours, or terms and conditions of employment is a mandatory
subject of bargaining. A mandatory subject may not be changed unilaterally: the employer owes notice
to the union and a genuine opportunity to bargain. AI that **monitors, scores, allocates, schedules, or
disciplines** plainly touches terms and conditions of employment. And after *Endurance*, a general
management-rights clause is unlikely to be clear and unmistakable enough to have waived it. None of
that requires a new legal theory.

**What is not settled.** **There is no Board decision applying the duty to bargain to an AI
deployment.** Not one. In May 2025 SAG-AFTRA filed an unfair labor practice charge against Llama
Productions, a subsidiary of Epic Games, over an AI-generated voice performance used in *Fortnite*,
alleging failure to bargain and a unilateral change replacing bargaining-unit work. **It is a charge,
not a holding**, and the Board has not opined on AI-driven job displacement.

Now the thing to notice about how this gets written up:

> **Secondary sources state the conclusion as though it were law. It is a strong inference from settled
> doctrine, with a pending charge and no decision.**

*Unverifiable is not the same as false*, and it applies to a legal proposition exactly as this
curriculum applies it to a vendor claim. The inference here is strong — probably strong enough to plan
on. But **a strong inference and a holding are different objects**, and the difference shows up in
three places: how you advise a business leader who wants certainty, how you price the risk of being
wrong, and whether you can cite anything if challenged.

**The practical answer does not depend on resolving it.** Under clear and unmistakable waiver your
existing management-rights clause probably does not cover an AI deployment affecting unit work. So:
**bargain it, or negotiate language that says what you may do.** Both are available now, neither
requires a Board decision, and the second is what the other side of the table has been doing while
this was being argued about.

## Lesson 4 · What the other side already won **[V]**

Here is the part that reframes the whole module. While practitioners waited for a Board decision,
**unions negotiated the obligations directly** — and what they negotiated is worth reading closely,
because it converges on something you have already been taught.

**Notice.** CWA's agreement at the Microsoft subsidiary ZeniMax requires notice to the union when AI
implementation affects bargaining-unit work. It became a template reused elsewhere in tech. Notice is
the single most common AI provision in agreements generally: roughly **38% of union members report at
least one contract provision addressing automated management or workplace surveillance**, and notice is
the most frequent form it takes.

**Volume, quietly.** NewsGuild-CWA holds roughly **85–90 agreements containing AI language**, **58 of
them ratified** newsroom contracts. This is not a pilot. It is a body of negotiated practice
accumulating faster than the case law.

**And then the provision that should stop you.** The **UPS–Teamsters** agreement prohibits using sensor
data as the **sole basis for discipline** and requires **human review of context** before AI-generated
performance data is acted on.

Read that again as a curriculum matter. **That is 101's assist-versus-decide line, and this track's
rung boundary, written into a collective bargaining agreement by the other side of the table.** The
distinction you have been learning as professional craft is, in some workplaces, already a
contractual obligation enforceable through grievance and arbitration.

> **And now the convergence, which is the most useful sentence in this module.**
>
> **Notice, human review, and records** are the three duties the AI employment statutes keep landing on.
> They are also the three things unions are winning at the table. **In a represented workplace you may
> owe them by contract years before any regulator asks** — and the enforcement mechanism is faster,
> because it is a grievance rather than an investigation.

Two operational consequences. **Your contract is now a compliance document for AI**, and somebody
should read it that way on purpose rather than discovering the provision in a grievance. And **the
convergence is a planning gift**: if you build notice, human review and a record into every deployment,
you are simultaneously ahead of the legislation, inside most negotiated language, and defensible under
the duty to bargain. You do not have to pick.

## Lesson 5 · Section 7 without a union, and what to do this quarter

**Why this module is not conditional.** Protected concerted activity does not require a union. Section
7 covers employees acting together about pay, hours, or conditions **in a workplace with no union, no
election, and no organising campaign.** AI monitoring that chills those conversations is exposure on
the same statutory basis it would be in a represented shop.

And the rescission of GC 23-02 **removed the theory, not the section.** If you have concluded that a
non-union workplace is outside all of this, you have made the layer error this module exists to
prevent — in the most expensive direction available, because you have concluded that a *statute* stopped
applying on the strength of a *memorandum* being withdrawn.

**A related caution about durability, at the doctrine layer.** Whether the *Weingarten* right extends
to **unrepresented** employees is a question the Board has answered both ways. It extended the right in
*Epilepsy Foundation* (2000) and withdrew it in ***IBM Corp.*, 341 NLRB No. 148 (9 June 2004)**, which
is where the doctrine currently sits. A subsequent General Counsel urged the Board to reverse *IBM*
again; the Board did not, and the General Counsel left. **Nothing about that sequence is unusual.** It
is what the doctrine layer looks like when you watch it for twenty years, and it is why the activity
asks you to date your propositions rather than merely list them.

### What you can do this quarter without waiting for anyone

Four things, none of which requires a legal opinion:

1. **Read your management-rights clause and write down the exact words** it uses for equipment,
   technology, or methods. That phrase is your position, and now you know what a Board has said about
   language of that generality.
2. **Date every legal proposition you rely on.** The activity builds the list. Undated propositions are
   the failure mode; a proposition with a date attached at least announces when to re-check it.
3. **Read your collective bargaining agreement as an AI compliance document.** Search it for notice,
   technology, equipment, monitoring, discipline, performance data. You may already owe notice.
4. **Build notice, human review, and a record into any deployment touching people.** All three are owed
   under converging pressure from three directions, and none of them requires knowing how *Endurance*
   will fare.

### The list you bring counsel

The point of a counsel conversation is to get an answer, and the way to get an answer is to arrive with
facts rather than a topic. Bring:

- **What the system does** to whom, at what cadence, and what decisions it touches — in the vocabulary
  of Module 1's ladder, which rung it operates on.
- **The exact management-rights language** you would be relying on.
- **The implementation date**, and whether it fell inside the January 2025 – January 2026 quorum gap.
- **What notice was given**, to whom, on what date, and in what form.
- **Whether your agreement contains any technology, monitoring or performance-data provision**, quoted.
- **Which of your propositions you could not source** — the activity produces this list, and it is the
  most valuable thing you will hand them, because it tells them where you are exposed to your own
  assumptions.
- **Your specific question**, written as a question. *"Can we do this?"* returns a shrug. *"Does
  'implement changes in equipment' clearly and unmistakably waive bargaining over a scheduling system
  that assigns overtime?"* returns an answer.

> **Do not skip the gate on the reasoning that your deployment is small.** *Endurance* was dash cameras.

## Take a position

**The claim:** *"An undated legal proposition is not knowledge. If you cannot say when the authority
last changed, you do not know it — you remember it."*

The strongest counter-argument is that **this standard is unmeetable, and pretending to meet it is worse
than admitting you rely on advice.**

You are not counsel. Nobody in an ER function can maintain a dated register of labor-law propositions
across the jurisdictions they operate in, and the attempt produces something worse than ignorance: **a
document that looks authoritative, was accurate on the day it was written, and is consulted for three
years afterwards.** A dated list is a *staler* artifact than a phone call, because the date creates
confidence that outlives the accuracy. The honest posture is not to date your own propositions. It is to
know which questions are legal questions and route them, every time, to someone whose job is to hold
this in their head.

And the sharper version: **this module is itself the argument against its own method.** Its dates are
correct as at drafting, its subject moved five days before it was written, and a learner reading it in
eighteen months will hold a confidently-dated document describing a Board that no longer exists. **If
the technique fails for the people who invented it, why would it work for you?**

Your position has to answer the operational question: **what happens in the meeting where you are asked
a question you have not routed?** Because that meeting happens, the answer gets given, and *"I'd have to
check"* has a real cost that is paid in influence rather than in liability. A standard that only works
when there is time is not a standard.

Note the module's own answer, and say whether it is sufficient: **date the proposition so the date tells
you when to stop relying on it, and mark the layer so you know how fast the clock runs.** A proposition
tagged *statute* is good for years. One tagged *General Counsel priority* is good until the next
election. **The date is not a claim to currency — it is an expiry.** Whether that survives being read by
a busy person in a hurry is the thing to argue.

## Applied activity — "The authority map, dated"

**Time:** 30 minutes · **Submit:** the map, the two you could not source, the counsel list, and a
300–400 word write-up · **Graded against the rubric below.** Score doesn't matter. Doing the work is
where the learning lands.

**No case facts.** This activity is about propositions and authorities, not matters. Nothing about a
specific employee, grievance, or investigation belongs in it.

**Step 1 — Build the map (12 min).** List the legal propositions your matter type actually relies
on — the ones you would state in a meeting without looking anything up. **Aim for eight to twelve.**
Include the ones that feel too obvious to write down; those are where the errors live. For each one:

| The proposition, as you would say it | Layer | Authority | Date it last changed | Date you last checked |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Layers: **statute · Board doctrine · General Counsel priority · rulemaking · contract · not
established.** If your jurisdiction is not the US, use its equivalents — the framework transfers even
where the citations do not; say which system you are mapping.

**Step 2 — The two you could not source (5 min).** At least two propositions will turn out to be
things you believe without being able to name where they come from. **Find them and write them down.**
This is not a failure to be minimised — it is the most valuable output of the activity, and a learner
who reports four is doing better work than one who reports none. For each: what you believe, what you
think it rests on, and **what you would have to be wrong about for it to matter.**

**Step 3 — Your management-rights clause, or its absence (5 min).** Quote the exact words your
agreement uses for equipment, technology, methods, or means of operation. Then one line: **would that
language survive the *Endurance* standard applied to the last technology your organisation deployed?**
If you have no represented population, do the equivalent — quote the policy language you rely on to
deploy monitoring, and say who has agreed to it.

**Step 4 — The counsel list (5 min).** Take the module's list and make it yours: the one question you
would put to labor counsel, written as an answerable question, plus the facts you would bring. **A
question that cannot be answered yes or no is not ready.**

**Step 5 — Score the prediction.** Your predicted percentage of propositions whose change-date you
could name, against the percentage you could actually name once the map existed.

Then the write-up: your position on the claim above, answering **what happens in the meeting where you
are asked a question you have not routed** and whether dating-plus-layering is sufficient; whether the
opening claim turned out to be true of you; and the honest one — **which proposition on your map were
you most confident about before you checked its date?**

- A. Because non-union employers are covered by the same Board rulemaking
- B. Because protected concerted activity under Section 7 does not require a union, and the rescission of GC 23-02 removed the theory rather than the section ✓
- C. Because non-union workplaces are subject to the duty to bargain once a majority signs cards
- D. Because *Weingarten* applies to unrepresented employees

> **B.** Concluding a non-union workplace is outside all of this is the layer error in its most expensive
> form. And D is the trap: *Epilepsy Foundation* (2000) extended *Weingarten* to unrepresented employees
> and *IBM Corp.* (2004) withdrew it, which is where the doctrine sits — a good illustration of how the
> doctrine layer behaves over twenty years.

## Sources and attribution

- **Statute** — Section 7 of the National Labor Relations Act (protected concerted activity), Sections
  8(a)(5) and 8(d) (the duty to bargain over mandatory subjects), and *NLRB v. J. Weingarten, Inc.*
  (1975). Stable; no verification issue.
- ***Endurance Environmental Solutions, LLC***, **373 NLRB No. 141, decided 10 December 2024, Case
  09–CA–273873** (Teamsters Local No. 100) `[V]` — restoring the clear-and-unmistakable-waiver standard
  and overruling *MV Transportation, Inc.* (2019) and its contract-coverage test, applied
  retroactively. **The facts as used here — a waste-hauling company installing video cameras in a fleet
  of roughly 400 trucks, defending on a management-rights clause reserving the right to "implement
  changes in equipment" — are drawn from the Board's own summary of the decision and corroborated
  across multiple firm analyses.** Reported majority: Chairman McFerran with Members Prouty and Wilcox,
  Member Kaplan dissenting; the composition is stated because Lesson 2 depends on it.
- **GC Memorandum 23-02**, *Electronic Monitoring and Algorithmic Management of Employees Interfering
  with the Exercise of Section 7 Rights*, issued 31 October 2022 `[V]`, **rescinded 14 February 2025 in
  GC 25-05 among 29 memoranda withdrawn**, agency case backlog cited.
- **Board composition** `[V]` — Member Wilcox removed 27 January 2025, leaving the Board below quorum
  for roughly eleven months with no Board-level decisions issuing; Mayer and Murphy confirmed 18
  December 2025 with first published decisions in January 2026; *Lodi Volunteer Ambulance Rescue Squad*
  (28 January 2026) declining to reach a Biden-era remedial precedent under the Board's unwritten
  three-vote convention; **Macy and Prouty confirmed 7 August 2026, producing a 3–1 Republican majority
  with one Democratic seat vacant and James Murphy as Chairman.** General Counsel Crystal Carey.
  **This is the fastest-moving material in the ladder and it moved during drafting** — the outline for
  this module was written under the two-member-majority posture and drafted five days after the third
  confirmation. The module says so in the text because concealing it would teach the wrong lesson.
- **Duty to bargain over AI** `[V]` — **no Board decision applying the duty to bargain to an AI
  deployment could be found, and the module states that as a finding rather than working around it.**
  SAG-AFTRA's unfair labor practice charge against Llama Productions (an Epic Games subsidiary), filed
  May 2025 over an AI-generated voice performance in *Fortnite* and alleging failure to bargain and a
  unilateral change replacing bargaining-unit work, remains pending with no Board decision located.
  Numerous secondary sources state the legal conclusion as settled; that framing is **not adopted.**
- ***Weingarten* for unrepresented employees** — *Epilepsy Foundation of Northeast Ohio* (2000)
  extending the right, ***IBM Corp.*, 341 NLRB No. 148 (9 June 2004)** withdrawing it, which is where
  the doctrine currently sits. Used as an illustration of doctrine-layer volatility over two decades.
- **Union contract provisions** `[V]` — CWA's notice provision at ZeniMax (Microsoft) and its reuse as a
  template in tech; NewsGuild-CWA's roughly 85–90 agreements containing AI language, 58 of them ratified
  newsroom contracts; the UPS–Teamsters prohibition on sensor data as the sole basis for discipline with
  required human review of context; and roughly 38% of union members reporting at least one provision
  on automated management or workplace surveillance, most commonly notice. Figures are as reported and
  the proportion should be treated as survey-derived.
- **A claim that was rejected.** A secondary summary reported the Board reinstating a prior joint-employer
  standard in April 2026. It could not be established whether that referred to a Board decision or to
  rulemaking, and the two are different instruments on different clocks — and a decision reversing
  precedent in April 2026 would sit in tension with the same period's reporting on the three-vote
  convention. **The example is not used.** The general point it prompted — that rulemaking is a distinct
  layer and commentary reports it interchangeably with adjudication — is kept, without the example.
