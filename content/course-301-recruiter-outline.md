# AI 301 · The Specialist — Recruiter / TA track · "Rebuilding the Signal" (draft v1)

**Audience:** Recruiters, TA partners, sourcers, and TA leads — anyone who owns a requisition end
to end, or the process that requisitions run through.
**Level transition:** L3 The Specialist → L4 The Translator.
**Shape:** 7 modules · 20–25 min each · ~2h55 · same package as the rest of the ladder — read,
micro dose, tutor, podcast, one interactive, one AI-graded activity, one knowledge check. Async
and self-serve.
**Prerequisite:** AI 101 (or the diagnostic test-out) and AI 201. This track assumes you can
already draft a JD, summarize a debrief, and build a market map with AI — 201 taught that, and
this track does not repeat it.
**Tooling stance:** Tool-agnostic. Every statistic, statute, validity coefficient, and vendor
claim is volatile-layer `[V]`.

---

## The through-line

> **The cost of producing signal collapsed on both sides of the table at once. This track is
> about rebuilding signal that's expensive to fake.**

The asymmetry that makes it urgent: any capability you gain, candidates gain faster — they have
more time per application than you have per applicant. Greenhouse reports an average of 254
applicants per posting, with applications per recruiter up 412% `[V]`. Meanwhile 75% of managers
who believe they can spot AI-generated content fail when tested `[V]`. The screen that used to
carry information now carries almost none, and most funnels have not been redesigned to notice.

## Where the learner stands (design premise)

They finished 201. They can draft, summarize, and map with AI, and they are drowning anyway —
because the tools that made them faster made the applicant pool faster first. Their instinct is
to buy detection, and that instinct is the one this track has to redirect.

The promise, in the learner's words:

> "Everyone has the same tools now, including the candidates. Tell me what still carries
> information, and how to build a process around it that doesn't punish honest people."

## The spine: one live requisition

Every module advances **one real requisition the learner is currently working.** No hypotheticals.
This is 201's capstone model, and it fits this role better than the HRBP track's calibration
spine does, because recruiting work is already req-shaped.

| Module | What it does to the req |
|---|---|
| R1 | Audit its signal, stage by stage |
| R2 | Locate it in the leverage map; name one narrow goal |
| R3 | Run its intake properly, and get it marked wrong |
| R4 | Redesign its loop; write the candidate AI policy |
| R5 | Design its capture layer |
| R6 | Audit the tools touching it |
| R7 | Build the case for the one change worth making |

**And every module ends with a claim the learner has to take a position on.** The claims are
genuinely contestable — see *The claims* below, and Decision 2 for how they're graded, since the
product has no free-form debate surface and shouldn't pretend otherwise.

## R1 · What broke

*~25 min · the diagnosis, and the inoculation*

- **Lesson 1:** The two-sided collapse `[V]`. 254 applicants per posting; applications per
  recruiter up 412%. Signal cost fell for candidates faster than screening capacity rose.
- **Lesson 2:** The symmetry rule. Any capability you gain, candidates gain faster — they have
  more time per application than you have per applicant. The corollary that governs everything
  after: *any screen that can be beaten by a tool will be.*
- **Lesson 3:** Detection is a screening decision. The reflex response to volume is to detect and
  filter — and every detection response is a decision about people, so 101 M7's rules apply in
  full. 75% of managers who believe they can spot AI content fail when tested `[V]`, and the
  false positives land hardest on non-native speakers and anyone who writes unusually. Seeds R4.
- **Lesson 4:** The inoculation, up front rather than buried `[V]`. MIT's 5%. Workslop's ~2 hours
  of rework per incident. Two thousand agents launched, three alive. You are not behind — the
  market is selling you the anxiety, and it is selling detection hardest.
- **Interactive:** choice — four proposed responses to application volume; find the one that
  isn't a screening decision wearing a process costume.
- **Activity:** *"Signal audit"* — one live req, every stage, one question each: **what would
  this cost someone who's faking it?** Score each stage 0–3. **Calibration:** predict the share of
  your stages that will score zero. Most people find their first three do.
- **Claim:** *"Your résumé screen is now a contest between two language models, and you're paying
  a recruiter to referee it."*

## R2 · Sorting your own job

*~25 min*

- **Lesson 1:** Three tiers — throughput, insight, redesign. Where AI money actually goes versus
  where the leverage is.
- **Lesson 2:** The archetype split. High-volume hourly is a genuinely different game: speed-to-
  contact is the constraint, and volume tactics win. Professional and senior hiring is a
  signal-integrity game where the same tactics are a trap. Most bad recruiting advice is advice
  from the other archetype.
- **Lesson 3:** The diagnostic. What's the friction, who does it hurt most, what does solved look
  like *in one sentence*, should it even be an agent, and who owns it.
- **Lesson 4:** Automation or agent. Static and rule-based wants automation; an agent earns its
  place only when there's a judgment step in the middle — 201 M5's autonomy ladder, applied to a
  funnel.
- **Interactive:** sorting — twelve pieces of recruiting work into throughput / insight / redesign.
- **Activity:** *"Friction map"* — your team's week mapped, plus one narrow goal in a single
  sentence. **Calibration:** predict your throughput/insight/redesign split before you map it.
- **Claim:** *"Most of what your team calls an AI opportunity is an automation project with better
  branding."*

## R3 · The highest-leverage hour is intake

*~25 min*

- **Lesson 1:** Why intake errors compound and scheduling errors don't. Everything downstream —
  sourcing, screening, the loop, the debrief, the offer — inherits the scorecard.
- **Lesson 2:** The conversion. A 45-minute hiring manager conversation into four artifacts: a
  scorecard, calibration examples, a sourcing hypothesis, and an interview guide.
- **Lesson 3:** Challenging the spec instead of taking the order. The must-have that isn't; the
  "five years' experience" doing duty as a proxy for something nobody has said out loud. Business
  acumen as the skill that makes intake worth an hour.
- **Lesson 4:** Rehearse it first. The adversary set aimed at intake — the manager who wants a
  unicorn, the exec who wants it filled in three weeks, the panel that will disagree in the
  debrief. Commit your position, forbid hedging, ask for the strongest objection.
- **Interactive:** choice — four scorecards; find the one that survives contact with a real
  pipeline.
- **Activity:** *"Marked wrong"* — run a real intake, produce the four artifacts, send them to the
  hiring manager, and ask them to mark what's wrong. Submit the marked-up version. **The
  correction is the learning.** **Calibration:** predict how many items they'll mark.
- **Claim:** *"You cannot AI your way out of a bad scorecard. Everything downstream inherits it."*

## R4 · Designing signal that's expensive to fake

*~25 min · the signature module*

- **Lesson 1:** The selection science `[V]`. Sackett et al. (2022) corrected decades of systematic
  overcorrection and re-ranked the field: structured interviews .42, biodata .38, general mental
  ability revised down to .31 — with unstructured interviews and credentials near the bottom. The
  methods that survive AI are, unsurprisingly, the methods that were always better.
- **Lesson 2:** Expensive-to-fake by design. Work-sample-first and observed. Live defense over
  polished submission. What "expensive" actually means — time, presence, and specificity that
  can't be pre-generated — and why it's a design property, not a detection problem.
- **Lesson 3:** The policy fork. Two coherent options: **ban-and-verify**, or **require-and-grade**
  (Canva requires it; Meta permits it in a coding round with AI-code verification built into the
  evaluation) `[V]`. **Incoherence is the only losing choice** — a policy nobody enforces teaches
  candidates that your process doesn't mean what it says.
- **Lesson 4:** The detection trap, paid off from R1. Detection tools are vendor claims like any
  other and get 101 M2's teardown; their false positives are a screening decision with disparate
  impact; and verification belongs at screening through official channels, not sprung at offer.
- **Interactive:** sorting — ten screening signals: expensive to fake / cheap to fake / free.
- **Activity:** *"Redesign the loop"* — one loop rebuilt for the live req, plus the candidate-facing
  AI policy in plain language, **under 150 words**. **Calibration:** rank five selection methods by
  validity before Lesson 1 reveals the table.
- **Claim:** *"Polished answers are free now. If your loop still rewards polish, you're selecting
  for tool access."*

## R5 · The closed loop

*~25 min · the module with the longest half-life*

- **Lesson 1:** Instrument → capture → analyze → feed back into design. The only asset in this
  entire track you cannot rent from a vendor.
- **Lesson 2:** Worked examples `[V]`. Zapier's goal-setting loop — 91% participation, 800
  conversations analyzed, insights routed back into enablement. Docebo's interview summaries
  becoming an archive of which questions preceded strong hires.
- **Lesson 3:** Integration beats invention `[V]`. Landing Point's build ran inside the ATS on
  roughly $200 a month and one engineer. Buy the boring; build only the part that's yours.
- **Lesson 4:** What to stop counting and what to start. Time-to-fill is a vanity metric at 254
  applicants per req. Then the highest-value use of the capture layer, which almost nobody does:
  **hiring manager calibration from transcripts** — who is actually consistent, and who moves the
  bar between candidates.
- **Interactive:** choice — four capture designs; find the one that will still answer a question in
  six months.
- **Activity:** *"The capture layer"* — designed for the live req: what gets recorded, who reviews
  it, **what single question it's meant to answer in six months**, and who owns it.
  **Calibration:** predict whether you could answer "which interview questions preceded our strong
  hires?" today.
- **Claim:** *"The only asset here you can't rent is your own record of who you hired and how they
  turned out."*

## R6 · The floor and the audit

*~25 min · counsel review required before ship*

- **Lesson 1:** The legal floor `[V]`. NYC Local Law 144's bias-audit and notice regime; Illinois's
  2026 notification requirement; the EU AI Act's Annex III classification of recruitment and
  selection — deferred to 2 December 2027 by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (in force 27 July 2026),
  though the emotion-recognition ban has been absolute since February 2025 **and the Article 50
  transparency obligations took effect 2 August 2026 regardless of risk class** — which puts a
  recruiting chatbot in scope now, not in 2027. And the duty that survives every rollback: **don't run a hiring tool you
  can't show is fair.** Most HR professionals in regulated states don't know their own state's
  law exists `[V]`.
- **Lesson 2:** The agent doctrine `[V]`. A tool that screens candidates on your behalf can be
  treated as your agent — employers lose "that's the vendor's problem" and vendors lose "we only
  make the software." What that changes about procurement, and about which questions get asked
  before signature rather than after.
- **Lesson 3:** Adverse impact you can actually compute. The four-fifths rule, pass-through rates
  by group, and what a bias audit measures — so that a vendor's audit is a document you can read
  rather than a document you file.
- **Lesson 4:** What to do this quarter without waiting for counsel, and the specific list to
  bring them so the answer isn't a shrug.
- **Interactive:** sorting — ten tools and uses: run it / run it with the audit / don't.
- **Activity:** *"Tool audit"* — every AI system touching your live req, with its deployer named,
  its audit status, pass-through by group where you can compute it, and three questions for the
  next vendor conversation. **Calibration:** predict how many of those tools you could name the
  deployer for.
- **Claim:** *"A vendor's bias audit is a marketing document until you can read it — and if you
  can't compute a pass-through rate, you can't read it."*

## R7 · Arguing for your function

*~25 min · the course lands*

- **Lesson 1:** Workslop is a trust liability, and yours lands on hiring managers `[V]`. Roughly
  two hours of rework per incident, and 42% of recipients rate the sender less trustworthy. The
  hiring manager relationship is what the function runs on, which makes sloppy AI output more
  expensive here than anywhere else in the company.
- **Lesson 2:** The one-page case: baseline, measure, owner, review date. Why "we're using AI" is
  not a case, and why a small change with a named baseline beats a large one without.
- **Lesson 3:** The headcount conversation, honestly. What the ratio pressure actually means, how
  to talk to your team about it without lying to them, and how to talk to your exec about it
  without pleading.
- **Lesson 4:** What the function is for, after. The argument that survives the compression:
  signal, not throughput. A team that produces decisions nobody has to redo.
- **Interactive:** choice — four cases for a change; find the one an executive would actually fund.
- **Activity (course close):** *"The case"* — one page for one change to the live req's process:
  baseline, measure, owner, review date. Plus the reckoning: what these seven modules actually
  changed about that req. **Calibration:** predict what share of your hiring managers would call
  your team's AI-assisted output trustworthy.
- **Claim:** *"If you can't name the baseline you're beating, you're building agent number 1,998."*

## The claims

Seven contestable positions, one per module, stated in the content and argued by the learner.
They are the track's version of the HRBP track's two unsettled debates — but structural rather
than occasional, because in this role almost every question is live.

## Prerequisite map

- 101 and 201 assumed. Modules reference 201 material rather than re-teaching it.
- R1 first — it's the diagnosis and it redirects the detection reflex before R4 has to.
- R2 before R5 and R7 (you can't design a capture layer or a case without knowing the tier).
- R3 before R4 (the loop inherits the scorecard).
- R6 open order, but before any procurement conversation the learner has scheduled.
- R7 last — it carries the reckoning.

## Per-module deliverables

Same package and pipeline as the rest of the ladder: draft at
`content/ai301-recruiter-mN-<slug>.md` → `scripts/convert-draft.mjs` → hand-tuned `blocks.json`,
`micro.json`, `knowledge-check.json`, `rubric.json`, `sorting.json` or `choice.json`,
`activity.json` → `generate-seed.mjs`.

## Decisions (v1)

1. **Async and self-serve.** The source was drafted as pre-work for a live session where the
   artifacts and disagreements get discussed. The artifacts survive as AI-graded activities; the
   disagreements survive as the claims, below.
2. **How the claims actually work, given what the product has.** `get_scenario_challenge` is the
   module's *sorting exercise* staged conversationally — a commit-then-reveal game, not a
   free-form debate surface. So the claims don't use it. Each claim is stated as a `callout`
   block near the end of the module; the learner's position is captured **in the activity
   submission**, with a rubric dimension that grades whether they engaged the strongest case
   *against* their own position; and because the tutor reads every block, a learner who opens
   chat gets a live argument on the same claim. Committed, graded, and arguable — without
   inventing a surface we don't have.
3. **The live req is the spine.** 201's capstone model, not the HRBP track's calibration spine.
   Recruiting work is req-shaped, and a track that runs on one real requisition beats one that
   runs on seven disconnected exercises. Calibration prompts still open every module — both
   threads run, as in 201.
4. **Seven modules, not six.** The source's final module carried governance, measurement, and the
   headcount conversation at once. Measurement moves into R5, where the capture layer is what
   makes the new metrics possible; governance becomes R6, because for this role it cannot be half
   a module — it gains bias audits, the agent doctrine, and adverse-impact computation; and the
   headcount conversation closes the course in R7 rather than appearing as an appendix to a legal
   module. Fallback to six exists: fold R7's workslop lesson into R3 and recombine.
5. **Adversarial rehearsal is a technique, not a module.** In the HRBP track it earns 45 minutes.
   Here the high-stakes conversations are fewer and more structured, so it lives inside R3, aimed
   at intake.
6. **Detection is seeded in R1 and paid off in R4.** It is the reflex this audience arrives with,
   so it gets addressed in the diagnosis rather than waiting until the design module — and the
   fairness argument (false positives as a screening decision) carries the weight, not a caveat.
7. **No external prerequisite gate.** The source opened with a self-check that routed unprepared
   learners to free material elsewhere. Our ladder does that: 101 and 201 exist, and the
   diagnostic places people.
8. **Verified before drafting.** Confirmed against source: Sackett et al. (2022) — structured
   interviews .42, GMA .31; the workslop findings (BetterUp Labs + Stanford — ~2 hours of rework
   per incident, 42% trust decline); Greenhouse's 254 applicants per posting and 412% rise in
   applications per recruiter. Unverified and blocking: NYC LL144's current audit specifics,
   Illinois's 2026 notification requirement, Mobley's posture, the 38.5%-of-interviews-flagged and
   75%-of-managers figures, MIT's 5%, and the Zapier / Docebo / Landing Point cases.

## Open questions for review

- **Length asymmetry.** This track is ~2h55 across 7 modules of 20–25 minutes; the HRBP track is
  ~4h15 across 7 of 20–45. Deliberate — recruiters have less desk time and the material is more
  operational — or should tracks converge on a convention?
- **The archetype split.** High-volume hourly and professional/senior hiring are different games,
  and R2 says so. Is that enough, or does high-volume eventually want its own track?
- **R3's dependency on a real hiring manager.** It's the best activity in the course and the only
  one requiring another person to respond. Alternate path for learners between reqs, or accept it?
- **Does R6 split?** Same overload risk the HRBP track's legal module has, and the same answer for
  now: ship as one, watch completion.
- **Counsel review of R6** before ship — heavier than the HRBP track's, since every statute here
  is aimed directly at hiring.
