## Calibration prompt — before you start

*One claim. Commit before you read anything.*

Here's a factual assertion about your own organization:

> **"Somebody here could tell me, today, how many separate communications we sent our whole
> workforce in the last thirty days."**

**True of us, or not true of us?** Commit to one, in a sentence, before you continue. Not what you
wish were true and not what you could find out with a week's notice, whether the number exists
somewhere right now.

Then predict the number itself. You'll count it for real in this module's activity, and the gap
between your estimate and the count is the most useful thing you'll learn today.

Most people in this function commit to "true of us" and discover the number exists for one channel.

## Module brief

Every other course on this ladder opens by telling you that you're less behind on AI than you
fear. This one opens by telling you the opposite, and in a direction you probably haven't
considered: **you're ahead — on the wrong axis.**

Your function adopted generative AI faster than almost any other part of the People organization,
and it adopted it almost entirely for drafting. Drafting is the one part of this job that was never
the constraint. What was always the constraint is the thing AI can't manufacture: the finite,
shared, non-renewable supply of employee attention, and the slowly-earned credit that makes the next
message land.

This module is about naming those two budgets, showing you what the profession's own benchmark data
says about spending them, and getting you to count (for one real audience) what your function
actually spent last month. That count opens the **attention ledger**, which runs under all ten
modules and becomes the baseline for the proposal you write at the end.

The uncomfortable part is that the count is usually higher than the person who owns the channel
believes, and there is no villain in the story. Nobody decided to flood anyone. Every message had a
sponsor, a reason, and an approver. The flood is what you get when the cost of producing one more
message falls to roughly zero and nothing else in the system changes.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

1. State the two budgets this function actually spends, and explain why neither is measured in words.
2. Describe what the profession's benchmark data says about communication volume as an
   organizational risk, and name the limitation of that evidence.
3. Explain the AI-maturity split in internal communications, and why the function adopted AI where
   it is most dangerous and least where it would help.
4. Distinguish an AI initiative that spends attention from one that protects it.
5. Produce a thirty-day send audit for one real audience, and convert it into an attention bill.

## Lesson 1 · The inversion **[V]**

Start with the finding that reframes the job.

Gallagher's *2026 Employee Communications Report*, the eighteenth year of its State of the Sector
benchmark, published March 2026, drawing on more than 1,300 communications and HR professionals
across 40 countries — reports that **high-volume communication environments see a 30% increase in
leadership-trust risk and a 24% rise in audience burnout**. Alongside it, **83% of internal
communications professionals say information overload is a growing problem in their organization.**

Read those two together and the shape of the job changes. Communication volume isn't a neutral
operational variable that occasionally annoys people. It is being measured as an organizational
risk, and the thing it puts at risk is the exact asset your function exists to build.

**Now the limitation, stated here rather than buried, because this course is about to spend nine
modules asking you to interrogate other people's numbers.** That finding comes from a survey of
*communicators*, not a survey of *employees*. Nobody asked ten thousand workers whether they trust
their leadership less this year. Practitioners reported on their organizations, and the report
models risk from those responses. It is the best benchmark this profession has and it isn't a
controlled measurement of employee trust. Hold it as a strong directional signal from people well
positioned to see the pattern, not as a coefficient.

That caveat doesn't weaken the argument, because the argument doesn't depend on the precise
number. It depends on the direction, and on something you already know from your own inbox.

**What AI changed is not the direction. It is the last constraint.** Writing used to cost time.
That time was a crude, arbitrary, but genuinely effective rationing mechanism: if drafting the
all-company note took ninety minutes, somebody had to decide the note was worth ninety minutes. The
decision was made badly and inconsistently, but it was made. Generative AI removed the ninety
minutes and didn't replace the decision with anything.

So the first-order effect of AI on internal communications isn't efficiency. **It is degradation**,
more volume, aimed at a fixed supply of attention, at an organization that was already reporting
overload before the tools arrived. And because the remedy is a norms problem rather than a
technology problem, it belongs to the function that sets communication norms. Which is yours.

## Lesson 2 · The two budgets

Words were never your constraint. These two are.

**Attention is finite, shared, and non-renewable inside a week.** A workforce of six thousand people
has a fixed number of minutes it can spend reading things that aren't its job. Every message you
send draws on that pool. So does every message Finance sends, and IT, and the office-move
committee, and the four managers who forwarded your announcement with their own framing on top.
You're one of many drawers on an account with no overdraft facility — and you're the only
function that can draw on all of it at once, which makes you its de facto steward whether or not
anyone gave you the title.

The property that makes this hard: **spending attention feels free at the moment of spending.** The
cost lands diffusely, later, on people who can't attribute it to you. Nobody writes back to say
"that update cost me four minutes I needed." They just read the next one less carefully.

**Belief is earned slowly and spent instantly.** It is the reason a message from your channel gets
read at all. It compounds over years of messages that turned out to be accurate, timely, and worth
the time, and it drops in one send that was none of those. It is also the one input in this entire
course that AI can't produce. A model can write you a paragraph that *sounds* trustworthy. It
can't make you trusted, because trustedness is a property of your track record, held in other
people's heads, and no amount of fluency touches it.

Together these give you the frame the whole track runs on:

> **Your function's product is not content. It is attention converted into belief. AI made the raw
> material free and left both budgets exactly where they were.**

Everything in the next nine modules is either a way of spending those budgets better or a way of
noticing when you're spending them badly.

> ### Try this — 3 minutes
> Open the last all-company message you sent. Multiply the recipient count by the number of minutes
> you'd guess a typical person spent on it: be honest, most skim in well under one. Now look at the
> total in person-hours. That is the invoice. Ask yourself what you would have had to demonstrate,
> to a finance partner, to spend that many hours of payroll on any other activity.

## Lesson 3 · Where the function actually is **[V]**

Now the diagnosis, which is the part most people guess wrong about themselves.

The same Gallagher benchmark reports that **75% of internal communications functions remain in
early-stage AI adoption — describing their maturity as ad hoc, or as discussion only.** Only **36%
of respondents feel AI-ready**, and that figure rises to **61% among those with formal governance
and structure in place.** Teams with high governance are reported as roughly **ten times more likely
to reach an enabled level of AI maturity** than those relying on unstructured adoption.

Two things follow, and the second is the diagnosis.

**First: the variable is governance, not tooling.** The teams pulling ahead aren't the ones
experimenting most. They are the ones that decided how AI would be used before they scaled its use.
That is an unusual finding in this market and it is worth sitting with, because it is the opposite
of what every product demonstration implies.

**Second, and this is the module's real point: the profession adopted AI precisely where it is most
dangerous, and barely at all where it would help.** The same benchmark separates the two ends of
the maturity curve by what they use AI *for* — **low-maturity teams use it to draft content;
high-maturity teams use it to measure outcomes and generate strategic insight.**

Look at what that means against Lesson 1. Drafting is production. Production is the input to
volume, and volume is the measured risk. So the function's most common AI use accelerates the thing
its own benchmark identifies as its central hazard. Meanwhile the uses that would *reduce* the
hazard, knowing which audiences actually need a given message, measuring whether anything landed,
finding the messages that could be consolidated or killed, sit at the far end of a maturity curve
three-quarters of the profession hasn't started climbing.

That is the inversion, stated as a sentence you can take to your director:

> **We are using AI to produce more of the thing we already have too much of, and not using it for
> the thing that would tell us which of it to stop.**

This isn't an argument against drafting with AI. Drafting with AI is fine, it works, and Module 5
will make you better at reviewing what comes out. It is an argument about proportion, and about
which end of the curve you want your function's next investment aimed at.

## Lesson 4 · What this course will not teach you

A short lesson, because for this audience the refusal is the credibility move.

**This course does not teach writing.** It doesn't teach prompting, it doesn't teach you to build
a brand-voice pack, and it doesn't teach you to run survey verbatims through a summarization
pipeline. Those are all real skills. They are also all already taught, one and two rungs below
this one: briefing a model is AI 101 Module 5, durable context packs are AI 201 Module 2, and the
document pipeline whose worked example is literally survey verbatims to themes to executive summary
is AI 201 Module 3.

A three-hundred-level course that re-teaches them is selling you remediation with a seniority
label on it. Most of the material aimed at communicators does exactly that, because it is written
for people who have never used the tools — and you have.

What this track teaches instead is where the value in the job moved, and what to do about it: the
chain your work actually runs on (Module 2), what to stop sending (Module 3), how to review output
you didn't write (Module 5), whose voice is speaking when a leader's message wasn't written by the
leader (Module 6), how to listen without surveilling (Module 7), what happens when a bot speaks for
you (Module 8), how to arm managers (Module 9), and what to claim (Module 10).

If you find yourself thinking *I already know this* during a lesson, that is a defect and you should
tell us. If you find yourself thinking *nobody has ever told me this was part of my job*. That is
the course working.

## Key takeaways

- **Volume is a measured organizational risk, not an operational annoyance** `[V]`. High-volume
  communication environments show a 30% increase in leadership-trust risk and a 24% rise in audience
  burnout, and 83% of the profession reports overload growing. Note the sample: communicators, not
  employees: directional, not a coefficient.
- **Two budgets, neither denominated in words.** Attention is finite, shared, non-renewable within a
  week, and not yours. Belief is earned slowly, spent instantly, and is the one input AI can't
  manufacture.
- **Writing time was a crude rationing mechanism, and AI removed it** without replacing the
  decision it used to force. The first-order effect on this function is degradation, and the remedy
  is a norms problem — which makes it yours.
- **Governance, not tooling, separates the teams getting value** `[V]`. 36% feel AI-ready overall,
  61% where formal governance exists, and high-governance teams are around ten times more likely to
  reach enabled maturity.
- **The inversion** `[V]`: 75% of functions are still ad hoc with AI, and the maturity split is
  diagnostic: low-maturity teams use it to draft, high-maturity teams use it to measure and target.
  The profession adopted AI where it is most dangerous and least where it would help.
- **This course refuses the 100-level job.** No prompting, no voice packs, no survey pipelines —
  101 and 201 own all three.

## Take a position

**The claim:** *"Your function's biggest AI risk isn't a hallucination. It is that you can now afford
to send everything you used to have to choose between."*

The strongest counter-argument isn't that volume is fine. It is that **volume is a symptom and this
framing misdiagnoses who the patient is.** Internal communications rarely originates the messages it
sends; it carries traffic other functions demand be carried, on deadlines it doesn't set, for
sponsors with more authority than it has. Telling a communications team its central risk is
over-sending is like telling a switchboard operator the phone rings too much. On that reading, a
kill list is a fantasy about a power the function doesn't hold, and the honest advice would be about
influencing demand upstream rather than rationing supply downstream.

Take a position on that, in writing, in the activity. The version of your answer worth submitting
names the specific traffic you genuinely can't refuse, and then says what you would do about the
traffic you *can*.

## Applied activity — "The send audit"

**Time:** 20 minutes · **Submit:** the audit plus a 200–300 word write-up · **Graded against the
rubric below.** Score doesn't matter. Doing the work is where the learning lands.

Pick **one real audience you broadcast to**: your whole company, one business unit, one site. This
same audience carries through all ten modules, so pick the one you actually own.

**Step 1 — Count (10 min).** Every separate communication your function sent that audience in the
last thirty days. Every channel: email, intranet post, Slack or Teams announcement, newsletter,
digital signage, all-hands deck, manager cascade kit. If it was pushed at people rather than pulled
by them, it counts. Where a number genuinely can't be retrieved, write down that it can't be
retrieved — **that is a finding, not a failure, and it scores at full credit with an account of what
you tried.** In many organizations no single person can see across all channels, and discovering
that is worth more than a tidy estimate.

**Step 2. Price it (5 min).** Recipients × your honest estimate of minutes spent per message =
the attention bill, in person-hours. Label your per-message minute estimate and say where it came
from. An acknowledged estimate is usable; a confident invention isn't.

**Step 3. Settle the opening claim (2 min).** You committed to "true of us" or "not true of us" on
whether anyone here could produce this number today. What actually happened when you went to look?

**Step 4 — Score the prediction (3 min).** Your predicted send count against the real one. Name the
direction and size of the miss, and one sentence on what it reveals.

Then the write-up: what the bill came to, what surprised you, your position on the claim above with
the counter-argument addressed, and **one message in those thirty days that you would not send
again.** Naming a real one you personally approved is the strongest possible version of this
submission.

## Knowledge check — 8 questions

*Unlocks after the applied activity is submitted. Retakes are free and unlimited.*

**Q1.** The benchmark finding that high-volume communication environments show a 30% increase in leadership-trust risk and a 24% rise in audience burnout `[V]` comes from a survey of over 1,300 respondents. Who were they?

- A. Employees across 40 countries, reporting on their own trust and burnout
- B. Communications and HR professionals, reporting on their organizations ✓
- C. Chief executives, reporting on their communications functions
- D. A matched panel of employees and their managers

> **B.** It is a practitioner survey, which makes the finding a strong directional signal from people well positioned to see the pattern rather than a controlled measurement of employee trust. This course asks you to interrogate other people's evidence, so it states the limitation of its own.

**Q2.** What did AI actually remove from the internal communications system?

- A. The need for editorial judgment, which was always the real constraint
- B. The cost of distribution, which had been falling for a decade anyway
- C. The time cost of writing, which had been functioning as a crude rationing mechanism on volume ✓
- D. The requirement to target messages to specific audiences

> **C.** Ninety minutes of drafting forced somebody to decide the message was worth ninety minutes. The decision was made badly and inconsistently, but it was made. AI removed the ninety minutes without replacing the decision with anything.

**Q3.** Why is employee attention described as a budget you don't own?

- A. Because the CFO controls the communications budget
- B. Because it is finite and shared, you draw on it without the holders' consent, and the cost lands diffusely on people who can't attribute it to you ✓
- C. Because legal restricts what you may send to employees
- D. Because managers control whether their teams read anything

> **B.** Spending attention feels free at the moment of spending. Nobody replies to say an update cost them four minutes they needed. They just read the next one less carefully, which is why the cost is invisible to the sender.

**Q4.** According to the maturity split `[V]`, what distinguishes high-maturity internal comms teams from low-maturity ones in how they use AI?

- A. High-maturity teams use more advanced models
- B. High-maturity teams use AI to measure outcomes and generate insight; low-maturity teams use it to draft content ✓
- C. High-maturity teams have larger budgets for AI tooling
- D. High-maturity teams use AI across more channels

> **B.** And that is the diagnosis, because drafting is production, production drives volume, and volume is the measured risk. The most common use accelerates the hazard; the uses that would reduce it sit at the far end of a curve three-quarters of the profession has not started climbing.

**Q5.** The benchmark reports 36% of respondents feeling AI-ready, rising to 61% among those with formal governance `[V]`. What is the lesson?

- A. Governance slows adoption but improves compliance
- B. AI-readiness is mostly a function of budget
- C. The variable separating teams getting value is governance rather than tooling, deciding how AI will be used before scaling its use ✓
- D. Readiness surveys overstate capability in every function

> **C.** The teams pulling ahead are not the ones experimenting most, which is the opposite of what every product demonstration implies. High-governance teams are reported as roughly ten times more likely to reach enabled maturity.

**Q6.** Why does this course refuse to teach prompting, voice packs, and survey pipelines?

- A. Because they aren't useful skills for communicators
- B. Because they are already taught at 101 M5, 201 M2 and 201 M3 — and a 300-level course that re-teaches them is selling remediation with a seniority label ✓
- C. Because AI tools change too fast for that material to stay current
- D. Because they belong to the People Analytics track

> **B.** The survey-verbatims-to-themes-to-executive-summary pipeline is literally 201 M3's worked example. Most material aimed at communicators spends half its runtime on remedial content because it is written for people who have never used the tools.

**Q7.** In the send audit, you find that no single person in your organization can see across all channels to produce a total. What should you record?

- A. Your best estimate, presented as the count
- B. That the count is unavailable, with an account of what you tried. This is a finding and scores at full credit ✓
- C. Only the channels you personally own, presented as the total
- D. Nothing, and repeat the audit when the data becomes available

> **B.** In many organizations no single person can see across all channels, and discovering that is worth more than a tidy estimate. The audit is partly an instrument for finding out whether the number exists at all, which is exactly what the opening claim asked you to commit to.

**Q8.** What is the strongest argument *against* this module's claim that over-sending is the function's central AI risk?

- A. That hallucinated facts in an all-company message would be more damaging
- B. That employees have adapted to high message volume and filter effectively
- C. That internal comms carries traffic other functions demand, on deadlines it doesn't set — so volume is a symptom of upstream demand and a kill list is a fantasy about power the function lacks ✓
- D. That measurement of attention is too imprecise to act on

> **C.** It is the real counter-argument and it is strong. A position worth submitting names the traffic you genuinely cannot refuse, then says what you would do about the traffic you can. A is a 101 M6 concern and not specific to this function; B is contradicted by the overload finding.
