## Module brief

This is the right end of the barbell, and it is the end this profession has never been able to hold.

Module 1 gave you a distribution and a program you would kill. Module 2 gave you the diagnosis that
decides what gets built. Modules 3, 4 and 5 gave you three ways to avoid getting hurt. This module
is about the conversation where you have to justify all of it to somebody holding a budget, and it
has one governing sentence:

> ***"We cannot tell if it worked"* and *"it did not work"* produce an identical conversation with a
> CFO.**

That isn't a rhetorical flourish. It is the operational reason measurement is a design constraint
rather than a reporting activity. If your evidence arrives after the decision, it isn't evidence.
It is an explanation, and explanations don't get funded.

There is a version of this module that would tell you to measure everything rigorously and mean it
as encouragement. That version would be useless, because rigorous measurement of learning outcomes
is genuinely hard, frequently confounded, and often impossible inside a budget cycle. **So this
module argues for something narrower and more achievable: one measure that could come back
negative, on one program, with a threshold you agreed before you started.**

One. That is the whole ask, and almost nobody does it.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

1. Read an operating model (what to stop, protect, and reinvest in) off a portfolio triage.
2. Distinguish a citable number from a vendor number, and recognize the laundering pattern between
   them.
3. State honestly what the evaluation evidence in this field does and doesn't establish.
4. Design one falsifiable measure with a pre-committed threshold, working back from a business
   result.
5. Put expiry dates on a curriculum, and explain why a component with no expiry is an unbudgeted
   liability.

## Lesson 1 · The operating model, read off the triage

You already have the input. Module 1's three buckets convert directly into three decisions, and the
conversion is mechanical.

**Bucket one (cheap now) is where you stop and where you cut cost.** Not all of it, and not
immediately. But this is the only bucket where "we should keep doing this the way we do it" needs a
positive argument. The default is that production effort here falls, and the argument to preserve
any given item has to be specific.

**Bucket two (ours only) is where you protect and where you invest.** This is the material that
depends on undocumented organizational knowledge, which means it is the only material a competitor,
a consultancy, or a general-purpose tool can't supply. It is also, in most functions, badly
under-resourced relative to bucket one, because bucket one was where the hours went.

**Bucket three (needs a human on the hook) is where you decide deliberately**, using Module 4's
evidence rather than budget drift.

**The reinvestment line is the one that makes it a strategy rather than a cut.** If your case is
"we can produce the same catalog with less effort," you have proposed a cost saving, and the
organization will take the saving and not the function. If your case is "we're moving effort from
bucket one to bucket two, and here's what bucket two produces that nothing else can," you have
proposed a strategy.

**Which means the hardest sentence in your case is the one naming what you'll stop.** A plan that
adds a priority without removing one is a wish with a Gantt chart. And an L&D function is unusually
prone to this, because everything in the catalog has a stakeholder who asked for it.

## Lesson 2 · Citable numbers and vendor numbers **[V]**

You're about to be sold a great deal, and this profession is the target market. Here's the method,
and then a worked example using a category you'll meet this year.

**Three questions on any claim: what is the evidence, what is the sample, and what would falsify it?**

That is 101 M6's verification instinct and the HRBP track's teardown, aimed at the specific claims
made to a learning function. What follows is the worked example.

### The category: AI role-play and simulated practice

**What the vendors say.** Figures in circulation include **300–500% ROI in the first year**,
**80–90% completion rates against 15–20% for traditional e-learning**, and **70–80% knowledge
retention after 30 days.**

Run the three questions before you run your reaction.

*What is the evidence?* Customer implementations, self-reported, selected by the vendor.
*What is the sample?* Not stated in any of the three.
*What would falsify it?* Nothing offered. There is no threshold below which the claim would be
withdrawn.

And notice the shape: **round ranges.** 300–500. 80–90. 70–80. Every one is a range with round
endpoints, which is the signature of a figure assembled to be persuasive rather than measured. You
have now seen this shape three times in this track — the learning pyramid's percentages, 70-20-10,
and Module 2's own environmental prior, which is why that module refused to cite it.

**What the independent research says.** It is more modest and it is real. A meta-analysis of 65
studies of computer-based simulation with adult workforce learners found higher declarative
knowledge (**+11%**), procedural knowledge (**+14%**), retention (**+9%**), and self-efficacy
(**+20%**) against comparison groups.

**Hold both of those in view, because the gap between them is the lesson.** The independent evidence
supports simulation-based practice. The effects are real, consistently positive, and roughly an
order of magnitude smaller than the vendor claims. That is the normal relationship between measured
effects and marketed ones, and it isn't evidence of dishonesty. It is what happens when one number
is measured against a comparison group and the other is assembled from happy customers.

### The laundering pattern

Now the move to watch for, which is Module 3's *laundered authority* arriving in a business case.

A rigorous study of **in-factory soft-skills training** (human-delivered) reported roughly 250%
ROI within eight months through productivity, attendance, and retention gains. Real study. Real
finding. It appears in material supporting **AI role-play** products.

The study is about a different intervention. The citation is genuine, the number is genuine, and it
doesn't support the claim it is attached to. **A real study for a neighbouring thing is the most
effective form of laundering available**, because every check your procurement process runs will
pass: the source exists, the author is credible, the figure is accurately quoted.

The question that catches it: **was the thing studied the thing being sold?**

### What this means for your own case

The same standard applies to you, and this is the uncomfortable turn. If you put "AI role-play
improves retention by 70%" in a business case, you have become the vendor. If you put "meta-analytic
evidence suggests simulation-based practice produces modest, consistent gains in procedural
knowledge and self-efficacy; here's the baseline I will measure against," you have made a claim
that survives someone checking it.

**The second one is less impressive and it is the one that gets funded twice**, because the first
one only has to be wrong once.

## Lesson 3 · A measure that can fail **[V]**

### What the evaluation evidence actually shows

This field's evaluation record is poor, and the honest description of it is harder than the
commonly-cited version.

The most-repeated figures come from a study of **199 organizations**, in which roughly **35%
evaluated the business results** of their learning programs, with most measuring reaction and
learning and far fewer measuring behaviour change. That study is **approximately a decade old**, and
the figures for behaviour-level measurement vary meaningfully between sources — you'll see 42% and
54% quoted for what should be the same thing.

**We are stating that rather than dressing it up, because this module is about evidence quality and
a lesson that concealed the weakness of its own evidence would fail its own test.** What the data
supports is a direction, not a decimal: **most organizations measure reaction, some measure
learning, few measure behaviour, and fewer still measure results.** That direction has been stable
across every survey anyone has run, which is worth more than any individual figure.

### The design, which is the actual content

Work backwards. This is the only part of this lesson that isn't contested.

**Start from the business result.** Not the learning objective: the thing the organization
actually wants to be different. Fewer safety incidents in the warehouse. Shorter ramp for new
sellers. Lower regretted attrition on one team.

**Then the behaviour that produces it.** What would people have to do differently for that result to
move? Specific and observable.

**Then the capability that behaviour requires.** Now you know what to teach, and (critically)
whether teaching is the right instrument at all, which is Module 2 arriving at the other end of the
chain.

**Then the intervention.**

Most programs are designed in the opposite direction, starting from the intervention and reasoning
forward to a hoped-for result. That direction can't produce a falsifiable measure, because by the
time you get to the result you have no independent way to check it.

### The pre-committed threshold

Here's the part almost nobody does, and it is the module's actual ask.

**Name, before you launch, the observation that would tell you this did not work, and the threshold
at which you would stop.**

*"If the warehouse incident rate hasn't moved by at least 15% within two quarters of full rollout,
we will stop the program rather than extend it."*

Three things that sentence buys you:

**It makes the evaluation real.** An evaluation with no negative outcome available is a reporting
exercise. If every possible result leads to "continue and refine," you were never measuring.

**It makes the CFO conversation different.** You're no longer asking for belief. You're proposing
a bet with a stated losing condition, which is a form of argument finance recognizes and most of
what L&D brings isn't.

**It protects you.** A threshold agreed in advance is a threshold agreed while everyone was still
optimistic. Set afterwards, it will be set to whatever the program achieved.

And one warning carried forward from Module 4: **segment before you average.** If your intervention
produces bimodal results (working for the confident and failing the struggling) a single average
will show moderate success and hide the failure. The threshold should be set on the segment the
program was justified by.

> ### Try this — 3 minutes
> Take your flagship program and try to write its losing condition in one sentence. Most people find
> they cannot, and the reason is almost always that no baseline was taken. That is worth knowing
> before someone asks you for it.

## Lesson 4 · Expiry dates

The last discipline, and the one specific to this moment.

You own AI enablement for your organization, and the subject changes faster than your design cycle.
A twelve-week build on a tool-specific topic is stale before it launches. That isn't a problem to
solve. **It is a property of the material, and the response is architectural.**

Split any curriculum into two layers.

**The durable layer.** Principles with a multi-year half-life. How to brief. What verification is
for. Where the lines are. Why a false premise survives review. These change slowly and are worth
building properly.

**The volatile layer.** Tool specifics, screenshots, model names, statutory dates, vendor
capabilities, adoption statistics. These change in months.

Then structure so the second can be replaced **without touching the first.** Separate blocks,
separate files, marked and dated. When the tool changes, you swap a section rather than rebuilding a
program.

**This course does exactly that to itself**, which is why you have seen `[V]` markers throughout and
a review date at the top of every module. It isn't decoration. The volatile passages are tracked
and re-checked, and the EU position in Module 5 moved nine days before that module was written —
which is the argument for the discipline, made by the discipline.

**And the rule that turns this into a budget line: every component gets an expiry date.** Not a
review date: an expiry. The date at which it is presumed stale unless someone has confirmed
otherwise.

A curriculum with no expiry dates isn't a stable asset. It is **a maintenance liability you have
already taken on and not budgeted for**, and it will be paid either as a rebuild you didn't plan or
as content that quietly goes wrong in your catalog. Module 3's *stale specificity* is that liability
coming due.

## Key takeaways

- ***"We cannot tell if it worked"* and *"it did not work"* produce an identical conversation with a
  CFO.** Evidence arriving after the decision is an explanation, and explanations don't get funded.
- **The operating model reads off the triage**: bucket one is where you stop, bucket two is where you
  protect and invest, bucket three is where you decide deliberately.
- **The reinvestment line makes it a strategy rather than a cut**, and the hardest sentence in the
  case is the one naming what you'll stop.
- **Three questions on any claim: evidence, sample, falsifier.** Round ranges are the signature of a
  figure assembled rather than measured.
- **Vendor claims for AI role-play run around 300–500% ROI; independent meta-analytic evidence for
  simulation-based practice shows gains of roughly 9–20%** `[V]`. Both are real; the gap is what
  happens when one number has a comparison group and the other has happy customers.
- **Laundered authority: a real study of a neighbouring thing.** Every procurement check passes. The
  question that catches it — *was the thing studied the thing being sold?*
- **The evaluation record supports a direction, not a decimal** `[V]`: most measure reaction, few
  measure behaviour, fewer measure results. The figures the argument rests on are a decade old and
  inconsistent between sources, and this module says so.
- **Work backwards (result, behaviour, capability, intervention**) and **name the losing condition
  before you launch.** An evaluation with no negative outcome available is a reporting exercise.
- **Segment before you average**, or Module 4's bimodal result will read as moderate success.
- **Every component gets an expiry date.** A curriculum without them is an unbudgeted maintenance
  liability.

## Take a position

**The claim:** *"A program with no pre-committed losing condition isn't being evaluated. It is being
reported on."*

The strongest counter-argument is that **falsifiability is a standard borrowed from a domain that
can afford it, and importing it here will kill the wrong programs.** Learning effects are slow,
confounded, and often genuinely invisible inside two quarters. Onboarding, leadership development,
and anything whose payoff is a capability the organization needs in three years can't produce a
falsifiable business measure in the window a threshold requires. So a function that only runs
programs with losing conditions will systematically select for the short-horizon, easily-measured,
and unambitious — which is precisely the failure mode critics of the "95% of AI pilots fail"
literature identify, where organizations measured the wrong things at the wrong time and concluded
the intervention was worthless.

There is a sharper version. **Demanding a threshold may simply relocate the dishonesty**: you'll
get thresholds set where they are certain to be met, agreed in advance and meaningless by design,
which is worse than no threshold because it comes with the authority of having been pre-committed.

Your position has to survive both. In particular, say what you would do with a program you believe
in that genuinely can't produce a falsifiable measure inside a budget cycle, because that program
exists in your portfolio, and this module has just told you it is indefensible.

## Applied activity — "The case" *(course close)*

**Time:** 35 minutes · **Submit:** the one-page case plus the reckoning, in a 350–450 word write-up ·
**Graded against the rubric below.** Score doesn't matter. Doing the work is where the learning
lands.

Two parts. The first is the artifact. The second is the point of the course.

### Part one — the case (25 min)

**One page.** Written for whoever holds your budget.

**Step 1 — Stop, protect, reinvest.** Read off your Module 1 triage. What you'll stop, what you
will protect, and where the freed effort goes. Be specific: named programs, not categories.

**Step 2. The measure that can fail.** For **one** program: the business result, the behaviour that
produces it, the capability behind it, and the intervention. Then **the baseline as it stands today**
— an actual number, or an honest statement that no baseline exists and what you'll do about that
before launch. Then **the threshold**: the observation that would tell you to stop, with a date.

**Step 3. Segment it.** Given Module 4, which population would this program serve worst, and does
your measure separate them or average them?

**Step 4 — Expiry dates.** For each component of the case, when it is presumed stale. Durable and
volatile marked separately.

**Step 5. The claim you are not making.** Name one impressive-sounding thing you could have put in
this case and deliberately left out, and say why. This is the evidence-hygiene test applied to your
own document, and it is the step most likely to be skipped.

### Part two — the reckoning (10 min)

You committed a position at the start of each of the six modules. Go back and read them.

- Module 1 — more than half your portfolio is rebuildable in a weekend
- Module 2, your last five requests were diagnosed before they were built
- Module 3, your review would catch a wrong assumption as reliably as a wrong fact
- Module 4 — the human-versus-tool allocation was made deliberately
- Module 5, your AI-assisted assets would pass an accessibility audit
- Module 6. You could produce a baseline a CFO would accept

**Which moved, and why?** Not which were right. Which changed, what changed them, and (the one
worth thinking about) **whether they moved in a consistent direction.** Most people find their
positions were systematically optimistic in one specific way. Name yours.

**The rubric grades the account of the change, never the accuracy of any position.** A learner whose
opening positions were all wrong and who can say precisely why has done better work than one who was
right and can't say how they knew.

- D. Because measurement designed afterwards is more expensive to implement

> **B.** The two states are indistinguishable to the person holding the budget, which means an
> unmeasured program and a failed one are funded the same way: not again.
