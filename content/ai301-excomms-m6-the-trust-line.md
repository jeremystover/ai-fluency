## Module brief

This is the module the rest of the track was building toward, because it is the one place where
your two budgets meet.

Communicators have written in other people's names for a century. That is not a compromise the
profession made under duress; it is the craft. An executive has a position, a stake, and no time,
and somebody skilled turns that into language that carries. Nobody has ever thought that was
dishonest, and nothing in this module argues it is.

**What changed is not that AI can ghostwrite. It is that AI removed the last requirement that the
executive be involved at all.** The ninety minutes it used to take produced a forcing function: the
communicator had to extract a position before they could write, because there was nothing to write
without one. A model doesn't need a position. Given a topic and a tone, it will produce a fluent,
plausible, executive-sounding message about anything — and the exec will read it, recognize nothing
false, and approve it.

That output is not a lie. It is worse than a lie in one specific way: **there is nobody behind it,
and the reader cannot tell.**

This module gives you the evidence that readers can, in fact, partly tell; the reason you can't
detect this from the inside; the message types where the cost is concentrated; the production
pattern that resolves it; and the legal edge that has just landed on exactly this practice.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

1. Explain why perceived professionalism and perceived sincerity diverge under AI assistance, and
   what that means for institutional writing.
2. Describe the perception gap between judging your own AI use and judging someone else's.
3. Apply the message-type gradient rather than a blanket rule, and explain what governs it.
4. Use atomization to multiply genuinely human moments instead of synthesizing new ones.
5. State a voice input floor, and say what the EU AI Act's transparency obligations require of
   synthetic audio and video of a real person.

## Lesson 1 · The divergence **[V]**

Cardon and Coman, publishing in the *International Journal of Business Communication*, ran a two-by-
four study — two directions of communication by four levels of AI assistance — with **1,100
participants**, who evaluated a manager's message congratulating a team on meeting goals and setting
new ones. The sections written or edited with AI were highlighted, so participants knew what they
were looking at.

The headline, as reported by the researchers' institutions: at high levels of AI assistance,
**perceived sincerity fell to somewhere between 40% and 52%, against 83% at low assistance** —
while **perceived professionalism fell only from 95% to somewhere in the 69–73% range.**

Sit with the shape of that rather than the digits. **Two curves that should move together come
apart.** Professionalism holds up reasonably well; the writing really does read as competent, and
participants said so. Sincerity falls off a cliff. And the study's own framing is blunter than the
percentages: managers using medium-to-high AI assistance for routine communication put their
trustworthiness at risk, and at higher perceived levels readers began questioning the manager's
**authorship, integrity, caring, and competence** — four things, only one of which is about writing.

Which gives the finding that makes this module necessary:

> **AI makes the writing better and the writer less believable, at the same time.**

Professionalism is what you are optimizing when you review a draft. Sincerity is what the reader is
actually scoring. Those were the same thing for most of the history of this profession, because the
only way to produce polished prose about your own position was to have one. They have now come
apart, and every quality check in your function is pointed at the curve that didn't move.

**Two honest limitations**, because Module 5 just spent thirty minutes on reading things properly.
The stimulus was one message type — a congratulatory note to a team, which is squarely relational,
the exact place Lesson 3 predicts the penalty concentrates. And participants were *told* which
sections were AI-assisted. Real readers aren't told; they infer, sometimes wrongly. That cuts both
ways: it may overstate the penalty where AI use is invisible, and understate it where a reader
suspects AI in something you wrote yourself.

## Lesson 2 · Why you can't feel this from the inside

The same research contains a finding that is easy to skip past and explains almost everything about
how this goes wrong in practice.

**People rate their own AI use as roughly equivalent across low, medium and high assistance. When
rating someone else's, the magnitude matters enormously.**

That is a perception gap, and it is asymmetric in the worst possible direction. From the inside,
heavy AI assistance feels like light AI assistance — you know what you meant, you know the message
is accurate, you know you would have said something similar, and the tool merely got you there
faster. Your sense that nothing is wrong is genuine and it is not evidence.

From the outside, the same message is scored on a scale you cannot perceive.

Now apply that to the ghostwriting case, where there are three parties instead of two:

- **The communicator** knows exactly how much AI is in the message, and is judging their own use —
  which the research says they will rate as fine at any level.
- **The executive** whose name is on it knows less than the communicator, and is also judging their
  own use, because it is their name. They will rate it as fine too.
- **The workforce** is judging someone else's use, at magnitude, and is the only party in the chain
  whose judgment tracks the actual level.

**So the two people with the power to stop it are the two people structurally least able to detect
the problem, and the people who can detect it have no channel to say so.** They just adjust what
they think of the leader.

This is why the answer to this module cannot be "use judgment." Your judgment is the instrument the
finding says is unreliable here. It has to be a rule you set in advance, which is what the activity
builds.

## Lesson 3 · The gradient, and what to do instead

The bad response to Lesson 1 is a ban. Bans on AI-assisted writing are unenforceable, widely
ignored, and wrong — most of what your function writes is fine to draft with AI and always was.

The useful response is a gradient, and the research points at what governs it. Low-level assistance
— grammar, tightening, structure — is broadly acceptable to readers. The penalty concentrates as
assistance rises, and it concentrates hardest in messages doing relational work.

> **Acceptable AI involvement is inversely proportional to how much the message claims a
> relationship.**

A system-maintenance notice claims nothing. A deadline reminder claims nothing. A message that says
*I noticed what you did*, or *I know this is hard*, or *I'm sorry* claims a relationship — and the
claim is the entire payload. Generating it hollows out the only thing it was carrying.

That gives you a workable scale, from freely AI-drafted to human-only:

**Free.** Logistics, deadlines, system notices, factual updates, FAQ answers, translations of any
of the above. Draft away — Module 5's review discipline is what governs quality here, not this
module's rule.

**Assisted, human-shaped.** Policy explanations, change context, benefits summaries. The facts are
the payload; a human decides framing and checks against source.

**Human-first, AI-edited at most.** Anything from a named leader about a decision that affects
people. Recognition. Condolence. Apology. The layoff message, the hardest case in the function —
where employees read forensically, looking for tells, and where the discovery that it was generated
converts a painful message into a contemptuous one.

**Human only.** Anything where the *fact that a person composed it* is the message. There are fewer
of these than people think and they matter more than everything else combined.

### The constructive half: atomize, don't synthesize

Here is the move that makes this practical rather than merely restrictive, and it is the most
useful production pattern in this track.

The instinct, when a leader has no time, is to **synthesize** — generate a message in their voice
from a topic. That is exactly what Lessons 1 and 2 say will cost you.

The alternative is to **atomize**: capture one genuinely human moment, once, and let AI multiply it
into every format the organization needs.

The leader records twenty minutes of unscripted conversation — an interview, a podcast, a Q&A, a
video call with a transcript. From that single artifact: a transcript, a written summary, pull
quotes, an FAQ, short clips, a manager talking-points kit, translations. Every one of those is
derived from something the person actually said. **AI does an enormous amount of work and invents
no positions**, because the position already exists in the source.

Notice what this does to the economics. Twenty minutes of a leader's time — genuinely obtainable —
produces more usable material than an hour of drafting-and-approval, and every piece of it passes
the trust test, because it descends from something real. It also solves Module 5's source problem
by construction: the transcript *is* the source, so every derived piece can be read against it.

**This is the only place in this track where more AI output is straightforwardly the right answer.**

> ### Try this — 3 minutes
> Take the last message you sent in someone else's name and find the brief it came from. Check it
> for a **specific** — one thing only that person could have contributed. An example they saw, a
> name, a conversation, a reason they changed their mind. If there isn't one, you have just found
> why the message read as generic, and it was not the drafting.

## Lesson 4 · The voice floor and the legal edge **[V]**

Two things to set. One is yours, one is the law's.

### The voice input floor

The rule that makes ghostwriting legitimate has always been implicit. Write it down.

**What must a leader personally supply for a message to be theirs?** A defensible floor has three
elements:

**The position.** Not the topic — the stance. What they think should happen and why. If the leader
cannot state it in two sentences, there is no message yet, and drafting one produces the thing
Lesson 1 measures.

**The specific.** One thing only that person could have contributed — an example they saw, a name, a
conversation, a reason they changed their mind. This is the single highest-value element and it is
the first casualty of a rushed process. It is also what readers use to distinguish a real message
from a generated one, whether or not they could tell you that's what they're doing.

**The stakes.** What they are personally committing to, and what happens if it doesn't work.

If all three exist, ghostwrite freely at any level of AI assistance — the message is theirs and the
craft is yours, which is the arrangement that has always been fine. If any is missing, you don't
have a drafting problem. **You have a message that shouldn't be sent yet, and drafting it well is
the worst available response**, because a fluent message with nothing behind it spends credibility
that a delayed one wouldn't have.

### Article 50, and the carve-out that matters more than the rule

The EU AI Act's transparency obligations under **Article 50 became applicable on 2 August 2026.**
Two parts land on this function.

**Synthetic audio and video of a real person.** Deployers of AI systems that generate or manipulate
image, audio or video content constituting a deep fake must disclose that the content is
artificially generated or manipulated. **Unlike the obligation on AI-generated text, this one is not
limited to publishing on matters of public interest** — which means the AI-dubbed CEO video, the
synthetic voiceover in twelve languages, and the AI avatar in the onboarding module are all in
scope, even though the audience is internal. This is the fastest-moving surface in the track and the
one most likely to have changed by the time you read it.

**And the carve-out, which is the part worth carrying regardless of jurisdiction.** The obligation
on AI-generated *text* falls away where the content has undergone human review or editorial control
**and a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility for the publication.**

Read that again as a design principle rather than a compliance clause. The regulation's answer to
"is this AI content a problem?" is: **is there a named person who takes responsibility for it?** Not
how much AI was used. Not whether it was disclosed. Whether somebody owns it.

That is the same answer this module reached from the trust research, arriving from a completely
different direction — and it is the same answer Module 5 reached about stop-rules, and the same one
Module 8 will reach about the employee-facing assistant. **Who owns this sentence?** A message with
a named owner who supplied the position and stands behind it is fine at any level of AI assistance.
A message with no owner is a problem at any level, disclosed or not.

## Take a position

**The claim:** *"Ghostwriting was always fine. What made it fine was that the executive had to show
up with a position — and that is exactly the requirement AI removed."*

The strongest counter-argument is that **the requirement was never real, and this module is
romanticizing a process that was already hollow.** Communicators have been writing positions *for*
executives, not from them, for decades — inferring what the leader would think from strategy decks
and two lines of email, and getting it approved by someone who read it once. The "forcing function"
of ninety minutes of drafting mostly forced the *communicator* to invent a position, not the
executive to supply one. On that reading, AI didn't remove a safeguard; it removed the pretence of
one, and the honest response is to stop attributing institutional messages to individuals at all —
publish them from the function, which is where they always came from.

That argument has real teeth, because the alternative it proposes is cleaner than the voice floor:
**a message from "the leadership team" that was drafted by comms is not misleading anybody, and a
message under a CEO's signature that they didn't write is, floor or no floor.**

Take a position on that, in writing, in the activity. The strongest submissions say what would
actually happen in their organization if they proposed attributing a major announcement to the
function rather than to a named leader — because that response tells you what the signature is
really doing.

## Applied activity — "Your trust line"

**Time:** 25 minutes · **Submit:** the trust line, the voice floor, and the test, plus a 250–350
word write-up · **Graded against the rubric below.** Score doesn't matter. Doing the work is where
the learning lands.

**Step 1 — Check the prediction (7 min).** Take your most-ghostwritten leader. Look at the last five
messages published in their name and find the brief trail — the email, the meeting note, the
recording, whatever preceded each one. For each: did that leader **supply the position**, or approve
the wording? Count the supplies. Where you can't reconstruct it, say so; that's a finding about your
process, and it scores.

**Step 2 — Write the trust line (6 min).** The list of what will never be AI-drafted in your
function, by category, using the gradient. For each category, one sentence on why it sits there.
Categories, not instances — Module 5's rule applies.

**Step 3 — Write the voice floor (6 min).** For your two most-ghostwritten leaders: what they must
personally supply before a message goes out in their name. Position, specific, stakes — and what you
will do when one is missing and the message is due today. That last part is the rule; the rest is
aspiration.

**Step 4 — Test it against one real message (6 min).** Take a message you already published in a
leader's name. Apply your own floor to it retrospectively. Would it have passed? If not, name which
element was missing, and what you would have done instead — including the atomization option, if
twenty minutes of that leader's time would have solved it.

Then the write-up: your position on the claim above with the counter-argument addressed — including
what would actually happen if you proposed attributing a major announcement to the function rather
than to a named leader — and **the message in the last year you'd most want back.** Naming a real
one is the strongest form of this submission.
