## Calibration prompt — before you start

*One claim. Commit before you read.*

> **"If we consolidated a major piece of our HR service delivery into self-service tomorrow,
> employee satisfaction with HR would dip and recover within two quarters."**

**True of us, or not true of us?** Commit in a sentence.

Then: **how far would it dip?** If your service satisfaction is on a −100 to +100 scale, give the
low point you would expect before recovery. If you use a different scale, say which and give the
number in it.

Almost nobody has a number here, which is the problem, because **the number you would tolerate is
the single variable that determines whether a transformation like this survives**, and most
sponsors discover theirs in the middle of the trough.

## Module brief

Module 2 told you the operating system is where the compression lands. This module is where you
decide what to do about it, and it is the only module in this track that asks you to accept a cost
rather than argue a position.

The case at the centre of it is IBM's, and it is used here for a specific reason: **it includes the
part that usually gets edited out.** Most transformation case studies are told from the far side,
where the numbers are good. This one is told from inside the failure, because that is the part you
need.

Three things get taught, and only the first is widely known:

1. **What the consolidation actually achieved** — the numbers, which are genuinely striking.
2. **The J-curve**: the trough is the shape of the curve, not evidence that the thing is broken.
3. **The sequencing rule**. You have no standing to tell other functions to redesign work until you
   have done it to your own.

And one thing the case makes possible that a cleaner story wouldn't: **the move that makes the cut
credible rather than cold.**

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

1. Describe what happened at IBM including the failure phase, and cite the numbers accurately.
2. Explain the J-curve and why sponsor tolerance is the binding constraint.
3. State the sequencing rule and its consequence for your standing in Module 3's fight.
4. Explain why what you build with the savings determines how the cut is read.
5. Name your own first cut, your sentiment tolerance, and your reinvestment.

## Lesson 1 · The case, including the ugly part **[V]**

IBM consolidated HR service delivery into a self-service platform (AskHR) and the first phase did
not work. **Nobody used it.** Employees and managers went on doing what they had always done, which
was to email or phone an HR person.

So the organization did something most wouldn't have the nerve for. **It shut off the alternative.**
The HR email address and phone line were switched off, and around twenty-one thousand managers were
told their HR partners were no longer available for routine questions.

**Service satisfaction fell to −35.**

That is the number worth sitting with. Not a dip. A collapse, on the metric the function is judged
by, caused deliberately by the function's own decision, visible to every employee and every manager
in the company.

**They held.** And the far side of it is the part that gets quoted: roughly **94% of employee
questions resolved without escalation**, satisfaction recovered to **+74**, and a **40% reduction in
the HR operating budget.**

**A correction on the metric, because it matters for this audience.** The −35 and +74 are an internal
**customer-satisfaction score for the HR service**, not employee Net Promoter Score. The two get
conflated in most retellings. You know exactly what eNPS is and what it would mean for it to hit
−35, and it isn't what happened here. The magnitude of the fall is real and the metric is a service
score.

Two honest limits on the case. It is a single organization, told largely through its own accounts,
with the survivorship problem every transformation case has — the ones that shut off the phone line
and never recovered don't publish. And IBM is a technology company with unusual internal capability.
**What transfers is not the plan. It is the shape of the curve and the fact that someone held
through it.**

## Lesson 2 · The J-curve, and the variable that actually decides

Here's the thing the case teaches that no vendor will:

> **The trough is the shape of the curve. It is not evidence of failure.**

Any transformation that removes a familiar channel before the replacement is fluent produces a
period where service is genuinely worse. Not perceived as worse — *worse*. People can't find
things. Edge cases fall through. The population most affected is the one least able to self-serve.

That period isn't a sign the design was wrong. It is what the transition looks like from inside,
and it ends when usage becomes habitual and the system has absorbed enough edge cases.

**Which means the binding constraint is not the technology, the design, or the change
communications. It is the sponsor's tolerance for the trough**, and almost every failed
transformation fails at the same place: month four, when the numbers are at their worst, the
complaints have reached the executive team, and somebody senior asks whether we should pause and
reassess.

Pausing at the bottom of a J-curve is the one move that guarantees the cost without the benefit. You
have paid the entire disruption and returned to the old state, plus an organization that now knows
these initiatives get abandoned when they get hard, which raises the cost of the next one.

**So the discipline is to set your tolerance in advance, in a number, while everyone is optimistic.**

Say it out loud to whoever will be asking in month four: *"Service satisfaction will fall. I expect
it to reach around X before it recovers. If it goes past Y, or hasn't started recovering by
[date], we stop. Between X and Y, we hold — and I am telling you now so that when you ask me in
month four, I can point at this."*

**Most people set X too shallow**, which is what the calibration prompt is for. Hearing that a
serious organization tolerated −35 and held is what recalibrates it, and this module is the only
place you'll get that number stated plainly rather than skipped.

> ### Try this — 3 minutes
> Ask yourself what your CEO's tolerance is, as a number, for a visible decline in employee
> satisfaction with HR. Then ask whether you have ever discussed it. For almost everyone the answers
> are "I do not know" and "no", and month four is a bad time to find out.

## Lesson 3 · The sequencing rule

Now the consequence that reaches outside your function.

> **You have no standing to tell any other function to redesign its work until you have done it to
> your own.**

This isn't a moral point about leading by example. It is a practical claim about what happens in
the room.

When the COO's team is told their work should be redesigned, the first question is what that
actually costs, not in money, in disruption, in things going wrong, in people leaving. If your
answer is theoretical, you're an advocate. If your answer is *"here's what it cost us: satisfaction
fell to this number, it took this long, these two things broke, and here's what I would do
differently"* — you're a practitioner, and the conversation is different.

**This is also the answer to Module 3.** The scope fight is won by demonstrated competence on a
decision the others can't make. This is that decision. A CPO who has restructured their own
function and can show the framework and the cost has the strongest possible claim in that room, and
one who hasn't is making an argument about authority.

**The order matters, and it is uncomfortable.** It means your function goes first. Not
simultaneously, first. You absorb the disruption before you ask anyone else to, which is expensive
and is the entire source of the standing.

### And the move that makes it credible rather than cold

Here's what stops this reading as a cost exercise, and it is the part of IBM's story that is least
quoted and most important.

**They raised entry-level hiring while cutting the operating budget.**

The reasoning is straightforward and almost nobody follows it: automating the work that junior
people used to do while cutting junior hiring dries the well. In three to five years you have no
mid-level population, because you removed the entry point they came through, and the capability
gap that produces isn't fixable by hiring, because the mid-level market is where everyone else's
gap will be too.

**Which reframes the whole module.** The question isn't *what can I cut*. The question is **what
does the cut fund** — and if the honest answer is "nothing, it is a saving," you have proposed a
cost reduction, which is a complete proposal that doesn't require a CPO to execute. Somebody else
can do that, and eventually will.

The three-part shape, and all three are required:

**What you cut.** Named, specific, with the sentiment cost you'll absorb.
**What it funds.** Named, specific, and preferably capability that compounds.
**And what you protect**: the thing you won't touch even under pressure, which is usually the
entry point or the judgment layer.

## Key takeaways

- **IBM consolidated HR service delivery, nobody used it, so they shut off the email address and
  phone line** and told around 21,000 managers their HR partners were unavailable for routine
  questions `[V]`.
- **Service satisfaction fell to −35. They held.** The far side: ~94% resolved without escalation,
  recovery to +74, and a 40% reduction in the HR operating budget.
- **The −35 and +74 are a customer-satisfaction score for the HR service, not eNPS** `[V]`: the two
  are conflated in most retellings, and this audience knows the difference.
- **The trough is the shape of the curve, not evidence of failure.** Service genuinely gets worse
  before habit forms and edge cases are absorbed.
- **So the binding constraint is sponsor tolerance, not technology or design** — and transformations
  die at month four, when someone senior asks whether to pause and reassess.
- **Pausing at the bottom of a J-curve guarantees the cost without the benefit**, and teaches the
  organization that these initiatives get abandoned when they get hard.
- **Set your tolerance in advance, in a number, while everyone is optimistic.** Most people set it
  too shallow.
- **The sequencing rule: no standing to tell another function to redesign until you have done it to
  your own**, which is also the strongest possible claim in Module 3's fight.
- **What the cut funds is what determines how it reads.** IBM raised entry-level hiring while
  cutting the budget, because automating junior work and cutting junior hiring dries the well in
  three to five years.

## Take a position

**The claim:** *"You have no standing to tell any other function to redesign its work until you have
done it to your own."*

The strongest counter-argument is that **this is a moral intuition dressed as a strategic one, and
acting on it can be actively irresponsible.** Nobody asks the CFO to automate finance before advising
on capital allocation, or the General Counsel to restructure Legal before advising on risk.
Executives advise on things they haven't personally done, constantly, and the alternative (every
function waiting its turn to earn credibility experientially) would paralyse an organization.

There is a sharper version, and it has teeth. **Going first means absorbing the disruption first**,
and in a downturn that is exactly backwards: you cut the capability that manages everyone else's
cuts, at the moment the organization needs it most. A People function in the trough of its own
transformation is a People function that can't support a restructure elsewhere, and the cost of
that is borne by employees in other functions who had no part in the sequencing decision.

Your position has to survive both. Specifically, it has to say **whether the standing you gain is
worth more than the capability you lose while you're in the trough** — and if you think it is, what
you would do if a major restructure landed on your function in month four.

## Applied activity — "The first cut"

**Time:** 30 minutes · **Submit:** the three-part decision plus a 300–400 word write-up · **Graded
against the rubric below.** Score doesn't matter. Doing the work is where the learning lands.

Three things, all specific, all named.

**Step 1. What you would cut (10 min).** The first process or role in **your own function** you
would automate or consolidate. Named. Not a category: the actual thing, with roughly how much
capacity it consumes and who currently does it. If you can't name one, that is a finding and the
write-up should say what it means about your operating-system tier.

**Step 2 — The sentiment cost you'll absorb (8 min).** A number, on your own metric, and the
duration. Then the harder half: **who would have to agree to that number, and have you ever
discussed it with them?** A tolerance you haven't socialized is a tolerance you don't have.

**Step 3. What it funds (8 min).** Named and specific. If your honest answer is that the saving
gets banked, **say so plainly**. That is a legitimate decision in some conditions, and the write-up
should then address why the function should continue to be led by a CPO rather than run down.

**Step 4 — What you protect (2 min).** The thing you won't touch even under pressure. IBM's was
the entry point. Name yours and say why it compounds.

**Step 5. Score the claim (2 min).** Your predicted dip against −35, and against what you now think
your own tolerance is. Direction and cause — **and if your number moved after reading the case, say
what it was anchored on before.**

Then the write-up: the three parts, whether your tolerance has ever been discussed with the person
who will ask in month four, your position on the module's claim with its counter-argument addressed
— **including what you would do if a major restructure landed on your function in month four**, and
the honest one: **whether you would actually do this, or whether you have just written a plan you
have no intention of starting.** The second answer is common and worth admitting.

## Knowledge check — 8 questions

*Unlocks after the applied activity is submitted. Retakes are free and unlimited.*

**Q1.** What did IBM do when the consolidated service platform saw no uptake? `[V]`

- A. Ran a change-management campaign and extended the transition period
- B. Shut off the HR email address and phone line, and told around 21,000 managers their HR partners were no longer available for routine questions ✓
- C. Made self-service mandatory for a pilot population before scaling
- D. Rebuilt the platform based on user feedback before relaunching

> **B.** Removing the alternative is the decision the case turns on, and it is the one most
> organizations will not take. A and D are the moves that feel responsible and leave the old channel
> intact, which is why uptake does not change.

**Q2.** What is the correct characterization of the −35 figure? `[V]`

- A. Employee Net Promoter Score for the company overall
- B. An internal customer-satisfaction score for the HR service — not eNPS, which is how it is usually misquoted ✓
- C. The percentage decline in HR service usage during the transition
- D. A composite index of satisfaction and resolution time

> **B.** The magnitude of the fall is real and the metric is a service score. The distinction matters
> for an audience that knows exactly what eNPS is and what −35 would mean on it.

**Q3.** What does the J-curve say about the trough?

- A. That it can be avoided with sufficient change management
- B. That it indicates a design flaw that should be corrected before proceeding
- C. That service genuinely does get worse, and that this is the shape of the transition rather than evidence of failure ✓
- D. That its depth is proportional to the size of the population affected

> **C.** The important word is *genuinely*. It is not a perception problem to be communicated away —
> people really cannot find things, and edge cases really do fall through, until habit forms.

**Q4.** What does the module identify as the binding constraint on a transformation like this?

- A. The quality of the self-service technology
- B. The sponsor's tolerance for the trough ✓
- C. The change-management resource available
- D. The size of the operating budget being targeted

> **B.** Almost every failure happens at the same place: month four, numbers at their worst,
> complaints escalated, somebody senior proposing a pause.

**Q5.** Why is pausing at the bottom of a J-curve described as the worst available move?

- A. Because restarting requires a new business case
- B. Because it guarantees the full cost of the disruption without any of the benefit, and teaches the organization that these initiatives get abandoned when they get hard ✓
- C. Because the technology investment is typically non-refundable
- D. Because staff who were redeployed can't be returned to their original roles

> **B.** The second half is the expensive part: it raises the cost of the next attempt by everyone,
> not just yours.

**Q6.** What is the sequencing rule, and why does the module say it is practical rather than moral?

- A. That transformations should begin with the smallest function; because risk is contained
- B. That you have no standing to tell another function to redesign until you have done it to your own; because the question they will ask is what it actually costs, and only a practitioner can answer that ✓
- C. That HR must complete its own transformation before the technology is available elsewhere
- D. That functions should transform in order of their exposure to automation

> **B.** And it is also the answer to Module 3: the scope fight is won by demonstrated competence,
> and this is the demonstration.

**Q7.** Why did IBM raise entry-level hiring while cutting the HR operating budget? `[V]`

- A. To offset the reputational effect of the service consolidation
- B. Because automating the work junior people used to do while also cutting junior hiring dries the well: in three to five years there is no mid-level population, because the entry point was removed ✓
- C. Because regulatory requirements mandated minimum graduate intake
- D. Because entry-level roles were cheaper than the automation licences

> **B.** It is the least-quoted part of the case and the one that reframes it: the question is not
> what you can cut but what the cut funds.

**Q8.** A learner's honest answer is that the saving would be banked rather than reinvested. How should that be treated?

- A. As a failure to apply the module, since reinvestment is required
- B. As legitimate in some conditions, provided the write-up then addresses why the function should continue to be led by a CPO rather than run down ✓
- C. As acceptable only if paired with a commitment to reinvest in a later cycle
- D. As evidence the cut was too large for the function to absorb

> **B.** Banking a saving is a real decision and sometimes the right one. What it is not is a
> proposal that requires a CPO — which is a conclusion the learner should reach deliberately rather
> than back into.
