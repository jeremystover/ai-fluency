## Module brief

A CEO convenes the executive team to settle who owns AI. They get six confident, different answers.

The CIO owns the infrastructure and the security posture, so obviously it is theirs. The COO owns
the processes being automated. The CFO owns the investment case and is already being asked for
returns. The General Counsel owns the regulatory exposure, which is mounting. The Chief Digital
Officer was hired for exactly this. And you own the people whose work is being redesigned.

**Nobody in that room is lying, and nobody is wrong.** The question genuinely has no default owner,
which means it will be settled by whoever makes the most credible claim first — and in most
organizations it is being settled right now, by default, in your absence.

The verified picture is not ambiguous about where that leaves you. **In 52% of organizations HR has
no direct involvement in AI strategy at all**; only 28% report HR is regarded as a true strategic
leader with a seat at the table, and a further 19% say HR is seen as a strategic partner *and still
excluded from core decision forums* `[V]`.

This module is about one decision: **what part of this you are claiming, on what basis, and who you
have to convince.** Or — and this is a real answer, held by serious people — **what you are
explicitly declining, and why.**

Both are defensible. What is not defensible is the third option, which is what most functions are
currently doing: **not deciding, and discovering the answer later.**

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

1. Explain why AI ownership has no natural owner and what that predicts about how it gets settled.
2. State the digital-labour argument and identify its strongest weakness.
3. Distinguish a claim that wins from an assertion that loses.
4. Make a defensible decline, including the version where the decision has already been made
   without you.
5. Write your own claim in three sentences, with its basis and its audience.

## Lesson 1 · Why there is no default owner

Most executive scope questions have a gravitational answer. Financial reporting goes to the CFO.
Security goes to the CISO. The pull is strong enough that the fight is usually about edges.

**AI has no such pull**, because it is not a function. It is a capability that lands inside every
function at once, and each executive can see the part of it that is genuinely theirs.

Three consequences worth holding.

**Confidence is not evidence of a good claim.** Everyone in that room is confident because everyone
is looking at a real piece of it. The CIO genuinely does own the security posture. Confidence in
this argument tells you nothing about who should win it.

**It gets settled by precedent, not by principle.** Nobody will convene a governance review to
decide this properly. It will be settled by whoever moves first, builds the thing that works, or
happens to be in the room when the CEO asks. **A scope question decided by default is decided
against the function that was not present** — and the 52% figure is what that looks like at scale.

**And it is being settled now.** Not in a planning cycle. In procurement decisions, pilot approvals,
and who gets invited to the AI steering group that started three months ago.

## Lesson 2 · Digital labour, and the argument that is actually yours **[V]**

The strongest available claim is not "HR should own AI governance." It is narrower and it is much
harder to argue with.

**If agents have owners, budgets, access rights, performance standards, onboarding and offboarding
— that is workforce management.**

Work through it. An agent gets deployed into a process. Somebody has to own it. It consumes budget.
It needs access to systems, granted and revoked. Its output has to meet a standard, and somebody has
to notice when it stops. It gets introduced into a team's workflow, and eventually retired.

**Every one of those is a discipline your function already has**, built for humans, and none of the
other executives has an equivalent. The CIO manages assets and does not manage performance. The CFO
manages spend and does not manage onboarding. Nobody else in the building has a function whose
entire purpose is answering *who does what work, to what standard, with what access, and what
happens when that changes.*

**The precedent exists, and it is worth knowing about.** Moderna combined its People and Digital
Technology organizations under a single leader — a Chief People and Digital Technology Officer — on
the reasoning that workforce planning and technology planning had become one activity, and should
stop being two. It is not the only model, and it is a genuine existence proof that this can be
organized differently. Vendors are also building registry layers for agents — systems of record for
what agents exist, who owns them, and what they can touch — which tells you the market expects
somebody to need that view. **The open question is whether the person who owns the human org chart
also owns the other one.**

### The strongest objection, stated properly

This module would be dishonest if it only made the case, so here is the best version against it.

**The analogy flatters your function.** Agents are not people. They have no careers, no grievances,
no protected characteristics, no development needs, no families, and no capacity to be treated
unfairly in any sense the law recognizes. The disciplines that make HR good at managing humans —
fairness, development, engagement, due process — are largely irrelevant to managing software, and
the disciplines that *are* relevant — version control, monitoring, incident response, access
management — belong to functions that already do them well.

On that reading, "agents are headcount" is a metaphor that will be taken literally by an executive
team that does not want the argument, and **the CPO who wins it inherits an operational burden they
are the wrong function to carry.** That failure is not hypothetical: a People function that ends up
owning agent uptime has acquired the operating-system tier's least strategic work at exactly the
moment Module 2 told them to move the other way.

**The synthesis worth taking into the room**: the claim that survives is usually not *ownership* but
**a named decision right.** Not "we own the agents," but "no agent may be deployed into a process
that affects a person's work, pay, evaluation, or access without our sign-off." That is narrower,
harder to refuse, and does not put you on call at 3am.

## Lesson 3 · What wins, and how to decline **[V]**

### What loses

**"HR should own AI governance."** It loses for a specific reason: it is a claim about *authority*
in a room where every other claimant has one too, so it reduces to who the CEO likes best. It also
carries an implication everyone hears — that the function which has been excluded from the
conversation should now lead it — without supplying a reason.

**"AI is fundamentally about people."** True, unfalsifiable, and it changes nothing. Every executive
has a version of this sentence about their own function.

### What wins

**Demonstrated competence on a specific decision the others cannot make.** Not a claim about
territory — a demonstration that you have already done something the room needs and nobody else
did.

This is why Module 1 comes first. A CPO who has personally used these tools on their own judgment,
whose function has restructured something real, and who can say *"here is the decision framework we
used for which work gets automated, here is where we drew the line on decisions about people, and
here is the evidence it held"* is not making a claim. They are the only person in the room with a
worked example, and the scope follows.

**The narrow decision right, above.** Specific, defensible, and it does not require anyone else to
lose anything they wanted.

### And the decline, which is a real answer

Some CPOs should not claim this. If your function's operating system is genuinely struggling, if you
have no credibility on technology decisions, or if the fight would cost you a relationship you need
more — **declining is legitimate and should be defensible in writing.**

A good decline has three parts: **what you are not claiming**, **what you require anyway** — the
narrow decision right is usually the floor — and **what you are doing instead with the attention you
just saved.** A decline that is simply absence is the 52% outcome with extra steps.

### The awkward case: it is already decided

Frequently the honest position is not a claim or a decline but a fact: **the CIO already selected
the vendor, and you are being handed adoption.**

Two moves, and only the second one works.

*Relitigating the selection* is available and almost always wrong. You will lose, it will cost you
the relationship, and the decision is sunk.

*Claiming the consequences* is available and usually wins, because nobody else wants it: what
happens to the people whose work changes, what the policy is for what it may touch, what the
measurement is, and who is accountable when it produces a bad outcome for a named individual. **You
did not choose the tool. You can still own everything that happens to a person because of it** —
and that is the more durable half of the scope anyway.

  You did not choose the tool; you can still own what happens to a person because of it.

## Take a position

**The claim:** *"Agents with owners, budgets and access rights are headcount, and headcount is
yours."*

The strongest counter-argument is the one Lesson 2 states and does not resolve: **this is a metaphor,
and metaphors make bad org charts.** Agents are software. The disciplines that transfer are
infrastructure disciplines — monitoring, versioning, access management, incident response — and they
belong to a function that already performs them competently. The disciplines your function is
genuinely excellent at do not transfer at all, because software cannot be treated unfairly.

There is a sharper version that should worry you more. **Winning this fight might be the worst
available outcome.** You would acquire the operational overhead of a system nobody else wants to run,
at the exact moment Module 2 argued your budget and attention should be moving *out* of the
operating system and toward governance and counsel. The executive team would experience this as
generosity. In three years you would be the function that runs the agent registry, which is a
sophisticated way of describing an administrative burden.

Your position has to survive that — and specifically it has to say **what you would refuse to accept
even if it were offered to you**, because a claim with no stated limit is how a function acquires
work it did not want.

## Applied activity — "Your claim, in three sentences"

**Time:** 30 minutes · **Submit:** the claim or the decline, plus a 300–400 word write-up ·
**Graded against the rubric below.** Score doesn't matter. Doing the work is where the learning
lands.

**Write your claim in three sentences.** Or write your decline. Both are graded the same way: on
whether the position survives its strongest objection.

**Step 1 — The claim (10 min).** Three sentences, no more.

1. **What part of AI ownership you are asserting.** Specific. If the sentence would work for any
   executive in any company, it is not yet a claim.
2. **On what basis.** Not "because people are involved." What your function can do that the others
   cannot, ideally with a worked example you already have.
3. **Who you have to convince**, by name or by role — and what they currently believe.

*Or the decline*, also three sentences: what you are not claiming, what you require anyway, and what
you will do instead with the attention.

**Step 2 — Name your limit (5 min).** What you would **refuse** even if offered. This is required in
both versions, and it is the step that separates a position from an appetite.

**Step 3 — Run it against the objection (8 min).** Take the strongest case against your position —
for a claim, that the analogy flatters you and the win is a burden; for a decline, that it is the
52% outcome with extra steps — and say specifically what survives it. Not a rebuttal. What survives.

**Step 4 — The already-decided case (5 min).** If a significant AI decision in your organization has
already been made without you, say what it was and **which consequence you are claiming**. If none
has, name the next one that will be and how you find out in time.

**Step 5 — Score the claim (2 min).** Your opening position against what you now think — and if you
predicted three peers' answers, whether you would still predict the same three.

Then the write-up: the claim or decline, the limit, what survived the objection, and your position
on the module's claim. Plus the honest one: **which of the two you actually wrote, and whether that
was a judgment or an avoidance.** Both answers are respectable and only one of them is usually true.
