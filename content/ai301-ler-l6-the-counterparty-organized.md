## Module brief

Every module so far has been about your own work — what you may ask a model, what your record contains,
how your report holds up. This one is about the fact that **the other side of the table got the same
tools, at the same time, and used them faster.**

Three counterparties, and each changes something different about your job.

**The employee**, who now drafts complaints and appeals with a tool that writes in the register of a
solicitor's letter. **The union**, which has statutory information rights and is applying AI to
contract analysis and comparator research across employers. And **the forum** — labor arbitration —
where both sides' advocacy is now AI-assisted and where, unlike a court, there is almost no apparatus
for catching it when that goes wrong.

The module's central rule is short, legally clear, and much harder to operate than it sounds:
**you judge the content, not the tool.** Lesson 3 is how you actually do that at volume, and the
closing argument is about whether the rule survives contact with a queue.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

1. Describe what AI-drafted complaints have changed about volume, register, and substance `[V]`.
2. State the judge-the-content rule and the exposure created by breaching it `[V]`, in both UK and US
   framings.
3. Separate allegations from argument as an intake step, and say which part of that is a permitted AI
   use.
4. Explain how the volume trap runs in reverse for this function.
5. Describe the union's information rights and AI use `[V]`, and why labor arbitration is a forum with
   no detection apparatus for fabricated citations.

## Lesson 1 · What arrived **[V]**

Employees now routinely use general-purpose AI to draft grievances, appeal disciplinary outcomes,
challenge selection for redundancy, and prepare tribunal or agency claims. This is not a trend to
prepare for; it is the present state of your inbox.

Three things changed, and only two of them are problems.

**Length.** A grievance that would have been two pages is commonly **eight to twelve**. Sometimes far
more. The length is not padding in the ordinary sense — it is structure, headings, numbered
paragraphs, statutory references, and a recitation of context.

**Register.** The document reads as though drafted by a professional: formal, organized, cross-
referenced, with the statutory language present and correctly spelled. And critically, **the drafting
quality now tells you nothing about the drafter.** The correlation between "articulate document" and
"articulate, advised, or well-resourced complainant" has been severed, which matters because a great
deal of informal HR triage ran on exactly that correlation without anyone admitting it.

**And substance, which is where it gets difficult.** The grasp of the law may be thin even where the
citations are present. A model asked to write a grievance about an unpleasant interaction will reach
for the vocabulary the situation seems to call for — and the vocabulary available is *bullying*,
*harassment*, *discrimination*, *victimisation*. So minor incidents arrive dressed in
protected-characteristic language, not because anybody is being dishonest, but because that is the
register the tool produces when asked to make a complaint sound serious.

**Two consequences worth being precise about.**

The first is operational: **your obligation per matter did not change.** A twelve-page submission
containing three investigable allegations requires the same investigation as a two-page submission
containing three investigable allegations, plus two hours of reading.

The second is subtler and more important. **The escalated framing is now on the record.** Once a
document alleges discrimination, your response has to address discrimination — including if the
underlying facts describe something else entirely. You cannot decline to engage with the framing on
the basis that you think the model chose it.

## Lesson 2 · You judge the content, not the tool **[V]**

The rule, and the exposure for breaching it.

> **You judge the content, not the tool.**

Declining, downgrading, delaying, or deprioritising a complaint **because it appears AI-generated** is
itself actionable, and it damages you twice over.

**In the UK**, where the clearest authority sits: refusing to process a grievance because it looks
AI-drafted risks breaching the Acas Code, and where the complaint concerns discrimination, adverse
treatment for having raised it can amount to **victimisation** under the Equality Act 2010. **In the
US**, the analogue is retaliation exposure — and, just as importantly, the destruction of the
prompt-and-appropriate-corrective-action posture that the employer's defence depends on. *We did not
investigate promptly because the complaint appeared to be machine-written* is not a defence anybody
wants to advance.

**The jurisdictional caveat is real and stated deliberately:** the rule is the same in both places, the
citation is not, and this module names the UK authority because that is where the direct guidance
exists. Take the specifics to your own counsel.

And then the part that is easy to miss, because it feels like the safe option:

> **Treating an AI-drafted submission with extra scepticism is the same error as treating it with
> extra deference.**

Both substitute a judgment about provenance for a judgment about content. Both leave a trace — in your
notes, in your triage record, in the tone of your acknowledgement letter — and that trace is
discoverable.

**Which produces the practical discipline:** do not record, and do not discuss, your view that a
submission was AI-drafted. Not because the observation is wrong, but because it is **irrelevant to
every decision you are about to make**, and a note saying *"clearly written by ChatGPT"* is a gift to
the other side that costs you nothing to withhold.

## Lesson 3 · Separating allegations from argument

Here is how you operate the rule at volume, and it is the module's method.

A long AI-drafted submission is typically **a small number of factual allegations wrapped in a large
amount of characterization, context, and legal framing.** Twelve pages might contain four things that
happened. And the crucial asymmetry:

> **Only the allegations generate an investigative obligation. The argument does not.**

The argument has to be *acknowledged and addressed*, which is a writing task. The allegations have to
be *investigated*, which is the expensive thing. Conflating them is what turns a twelve-page document
into a twelve-page investigation, and it is why volume feels like it has overwhelmed capacity when what
has actually happened is that the ratio changed.

**The method, and note where AI is permitted:**

**1 · Extract the allegations as a numbered list.** Each one an assertion that something specific
happened, phrased in the complainant's own words where possible. **This is a rung-one and rung-two
task and it is a legitimate AI use** — structural extraction from a document, verifiable by reading the
document. Ask for: the assertion, where in the submission it appears, and whether a date and other
parties are identified.

**2 · Separate the framing.** Statutory characterizations, comparisons to other cases, argument about
what the conduct amounted to. These get addressed in your response and do not become investigation
lines of their own.

**3 · A human confirms the list.** Two failure directions and both matter. Missing an allegation is
Module 4's unresolved-allegation defect arriving at the front of the process instead of the back. And
**collapsing two allegations into one** is the same defect in miniature — you will resolve the merged
version and leave half of it unaddressed.

**4 · Tell the complainant what you understood.** Send the numbered list back: *these are the matters
I have understood you to raise and will investigate.* This is the highest-value step in the module and
it costs ten minutes. It **corrects your extraction before you build on it**, it evidences that you
engaged with the whole submission, and it prevents the argument that you investigated a narrower
version of the complaint — which is Module 4's warning about restating an allegation more narrowly and
resolving the narrower version.

**And the forbidden step, which is the tempting one:** a model may not decide **which allegations are
serious**, which are in scope, or which merit investigation. That is Module 1's triage determination —
rung five — and it is where the invisible failure lives.

**The volume trap, running in reverse.** Elsewhere in this curriculum the volume trap is that cheaper
production means *your* function generates more artifacts. Here:

> **Production got cheaper for the other side, and your obligation per matter did not change.**

Same mechanism, opposite direction, and it is the arithmetic Module 1 described. The response is not to
read faster. It is to stop paying investigative attention to argument.

> ### Try this — 3 minutes
> Take the longest submission you have received. Do not read it. Skim only for sentences asserting that
> something specific happened, and count them. The gap between that number and the page count is the
> whole of this lesson.

## Lesson 4 · The other counterparty **[V]**

Two more surfaces, and the second one is genuinely new.

### The union

A union's right to information is a **statutory entitlement**, not a courtesy you extend. In a
represented workplace, information relevant to the administration of the agreement and to the union's
representative function must generally be provided on request, and refusing carries its own exposure.

What has changed is what unions are doing with it. AI is being applied to **contract analysis,
costing, and comparator research across employers** — reading agreements at scale, modelling proposals,
and finding how comparable employers handled comparable situations. Which means:

> **The information asymmetry that used to favour the employer is closing.**

You have historically known your own data better than the union did, because you held it. That
advantage was substantially a processing advantage, and processing is what became cheap. A union
representative can now analyse the contract you both signed more thoroughly than the person who
negotiated it remembers doing.

Two practical consequences. **Your information response is now more consequential** — what you provide
will be analysed properly rather than filed. And **information requests may increasingly ask about the
systems you have deployed**, which is Module 7's territory and a question you should not be answering
for the first time in a live grievance.

### The forum with no detection

And a finding specific to labor arbitration that is worth knowing before it happens to somebody in
your organization.

Fabricated citations from AI use are a documented problem in litigation. Courts have imposed sanctions
for filings containing invented cases and quotations attributed to real cases that do not contain them,
and three distinct failure types appear: **entirely fictitious cases; fabricated citations to real
cases; and real quotations from real cases that do not support, or actively contradict, the proposition
they are cited for.** The third is the hardest to catch because everything about it looks correct.

Now the arbitration-specific part:

> **In arbitration these failures appear and are largely neither reported nor sanctioned — because
> most arbitrators have no law clerks, no case-law database, and limited power to sanction.**

Read that as an operational fact rather than an invitation. It means labor arbitration — a
closed, precedent-driven forum where past awards and past practice do real work — has **almost no
apparatus for detecting a fabricated authority.** The detection mechanisms that make courts
comparatively safe are all absent.

Which cuts three ways, and all three are yours to manage:

- **Your own briefs.** Every citation in anything you submit gets verified against the actual award or
  decision, by a person, before filing. Not because you will be sanctioned — because you probably will
  not be, and the citation will simply become part of a record that is wrong.
- **The other side's.** If a union brief cites an award, check it. This is now a thing to do rather
  than a thing to assume, and it is cheap.
- **And the corpus itself.** Past practice and prior awards are the substance of contract
  interpretation. **A forum with no detection is a forum where a fabricated authority can enter the
  precedent conversation and stay there.**

## Key takeaways

- **Three things changed about inbound complaints** `[V]`: **length** (two pages to eight or twelve),
  **register** (professional drafting — and the correlation between articulate document and advised
  complainant has been severed), and **substance** (thin grasp of law alongside correct citations, and
  minor incidents arriving in protected-characteristic language because that is the register the tool
  produces).
- **Your obligation per matter did not change**, and **the escalated framing is now on the record** —
  once a document alleges discrimination your response must address discrimination, even if the facts
  describe something else.
- **You judge the content, not the tool** `[V]`. Downgrading or delaying because a submission looks
  AI-drafted risks breaching the Acas Code and can amount to victimisation in the UK; in the US it is
  retaliation exposure plus the loss of the prompt-and-appropriate-response posture.
- **Extra scepticism is the same error as extra deference** — both substitute provenance for content,
  and both leave a discoverable trace. **Do not record your view that a submission was AI-drafted.**
- **Only the allegations generate an investigative obligation. The argument does not** — it gets
  addressed in your response. Conflating them turns a twelve-page document into a twelve-page
  investigation.
- **The method:** extract allegations as a numbered list (a permitted rung-one/two AI use), separate
  the framing, **a human confirms the list**, and **send the numbered list back to the complainant** —
  ten minutes, and it corrects your extraction, evidences engagement, and prevents the
  investigated-a-narrower-version argument. **A model may never decide which allegations are serious.**
- **The volume trap runs in reverse: production got cheaper for the other side and your obligation per
  matter did not change.** The answer is not reading faster; it is not paying investigative attention

- **The union's information right is statutory, and the asymmetry is closing** `[V]` — the employer's

- **Labor arbitration has almost no apparatus for detecting fabricated citations** `[V]` — no clerks, no

## Take a position

**The claim:** *"You judge the content, not the tool — and any triage that treats an AI-drafted
submission differently is a retaliation risk wearing an efficiency costume."*

The strongest counter-argument is that **this is correct as law and naive as practice, because the
resource question is real and does not go away by being ruled out of bounds.**

A function with flat headcount and doubled volume must allocate time somehow. And pretending document
provenance carries no information is itself a choice to ignore a genuine signal: a submission that
misstates the law in six places, cites a statute that does not apply, and escalates a scheduling
dispute into a discrimination claim **is, in fact, less likely to be well-founded.** Treating it
identically to a carefully-reasoned two-page account is not neutrality. It is a refusal to exercise
the judgment you are employed for.

And there is a second, sharper version that should genuinely unsettle you. Under a
strictly-equal-attention rule, **the employee who wrote their own two pages in plain language gets the
same investigative resource as the person who generated forty.** That is its own inequity, and
plausibly a larger one — because the plain-language complainant is more likely to be the one **without
access to tools, advice, or the confidence to escalate.** A rule designed to prevent discrimination
against AI users may in practice disadvantage exactly the people the process exists to protect.

Your position has to answer the operational question: **how does time actually get allocated when
volume exceeds capacity?** Because it does get allocated, by somebody, on some basis — and if the basis
is not stated it will be intuition, which is where the provenance judgment will re-enter unrecorded.

Note that this module has already offered one answer, and you should say whether it is sufficient:
**allocate by the number and seriousness of the allegations, which the extraction step gives you, and
never by the length or register of the document.** That is a stated basis, it is defensible, and it
survives being read out. Whether it survives a queue that is genuinely too long is the thing to argue.

## Applied activity — "Allegations, separated"

**Time:** 25 minutes · **Submit:** the count, the intake procedure, and a 300–400 word write-up ·
**Graded against the rubric below.** Score doesn't matter. Doing the work is where the learning lands.

**No case facts.** You will count allegations in a real submission and **submit only the numbers** —
the page count and the allegation count. Never the allegations themselves, never the parties, never the
subject matter. The procedure you write is generic.

**Step 1 — Count (6 min).** The longest complaint or grievance your function has recently received.
Count the **distinct factual allegations** — assertions that something specific happened, each capable
of being investigated and resolved. Record the page count and the allegation count. If your function
has not received a long AI-drafted submission yet, say so and count the longest you have; the ratio is
still informative.

**Step 2 — Write the intake procedure (12 min).** Generic, for your matter type:

- **How allegations get extracted** as a numbered list, and if a model does the extraction, **the
  prompt you would use** — remembering that it may extract and structure, and may not decide what is
  serious or in scope.
- **How the framing gets separated** from the allegations, and where the framing gets addressed
  instead.
- **Who confirms the list**, by role — and the two failures they are checking for: a missed allegation
  and two allegations collapsed into one.
- **The sentence you send the complainant** with the numbered list back. Write the actual sentence.
- **And your time-allocation basis**, stated: on what grounds does one matter get more investigative
  time than another? This is the *Take a position* argument becoming a procedure.

**Step 3 — The citation check (4 min).** If your matter type ever reaches arbitration or a hearing
where authorities are cited: who verifies citations in anything you file, and what they check. If it
never does, write one line on where else in your work a fabricated reference could enter a record and
not be caught.

**Step 4 — Score the prediction.** Predicted allegation count against actual, with the page count
alongside it.

Then the write-up: your position on the claim above, answering **how time actually gets allocated when
volume exceeds capacity** and whether allocating by allegation count is sufficient; whether the opening
claim turned out to be true of your function; and the honest one — **whether anybody in your function
has ever written down, in a note or an email, that a submission looked AI-generated.** You do not need
to say who.
