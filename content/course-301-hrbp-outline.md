# AI 301 · The Specialist — HRBP track · "Judgment Work" (draft v1)

**Audience:** HR business partners — aligned to a business unit, advising managers and leaders, owning ER, the performance and comp cycles, org change, and the people read on their population.
**Level transition:** L3 The Specialist → L4 The Translator.
**Shape:** 7 modules · ~4h15 · same package as 101 and 201 — read, micro dose, tutor, podcast, one interactive, one AI-graded activity, one knowledge check. Async and self-serve throughout.
**Prerequisite:** AI 101 (or the diagnostic test-out). AI 201 is strongly recommended and not required — modules carry pointers to the 201 material they lean on rather than re-teaching it.
**Tooling stance:** Tool-agnostic. This track teaches judgment, not mechanics; the mechanics were 201's job. Every statistic, statute, vendor claim, and market projection is volatile-layer `[V]` — see Decision 6, because this course inverts the usual stable/volatile ratio.

---

## The through-line

> **AI is improving HR's production, not HR's judgment. This course is about the second thing.**

Earned, not asserted: among HR professionals using AI, 87% report improved efficiency and 75% improved work quality — while **50% report no improvement at all in their decision-making** `[V]`. That gap is the course. Everything the market sells an HRBP lands on the first two numbers. The work that actually distinguishes an HRBP — the advising, the reading of a situation, the call nobody else can make — sits in the third.

## Where the learner stands (design premise)

They finished 201. They have workflows that hold, verification they trust, and a boundary sheet. And they have a growing, unspoken worry that none of it touches the part of their job that matters most — which is correct, and is why this track exists.

The 301 promise, in the learner's words:

> "Stop teaching me to produce faster. The production was never my constraint. Teach me to think better about the calls only I can make — and show me the evidence, not the vendor's."

Two things make this track different from everything above it in the ladder. First, **most of its modules produce no pipeline.** Four of seven are about judgment, argument, and other people's AI use; only two involve building anything. Second, **the evidence is the pedagogy.** This audience has been marketed at relentlessly; the fastest way to earn their attention is to show them the research that contradicts the marketing, including where it contradicts us.

## The spine: calibration as the capstone

101 and 201 used a calibration prompt as a device — predict, then score. **In this track it is the structure.** Every module opens with a prediction about the learner's own judgment or their own situation, and closes by scoring it. The final module reads the pattern across all seven.

This is the honest async answer to "how do you teach judgment": you can't grade whether someone's judgment is good, but you can grade whether they know how good it is — and the direction of a person's misses is the most useful thing they can learn about themselves. It also makes the through-line self-demonstrating: a course about judgment that measures judgment.

## Module 1 · Where you actually are

*~20 min · the shortest module, deliberately first*

Before anything can be taught, the anxiety has to come down — because decisions made from FOMO are bad decisions, and this audience has been sold FOMO for two years straight.

- **Lesson 1:** The anxiety and where it comes from. A third of HR professionals believe their adoption is well behind other organizations `[V]`. Who benefits from them believing that.
- **Lesson 2:** What the data actually says `[V]`. 31% of organizations haven't deployed AI at all. Most of the room is ahead of where it thinks it is — and "everyone just thinks everybody else is so much further ahead."
- **Lesson 3:** Why the gap matters. Adoption driven by comparison buys the wrong things, in the wrong order, for the wrong reasons — and skips the measurement that would have caught it (M3's subject).
- **Interactive:** choice — four claims about AI adoption in HR; identify which is true, which is true-but-misleading, and which is a vendor's framing wearing a statistic. `[V]`
- **Activity:** *"An honest inventory"* — what you actually use AI for today, weekly, by name. Not aspiration, not the plan. **Calibration:** predict the share of organizations with no AI deployed at all, before the reveal.
- **Knowledge check:** 5 questions.

## Module 2 · Sort your own job

*~40 min*

The two-question test — **does this work require presence, and does it require contested judgment?** — and the four quadrants it produces. Then the market context, unflinchingly, because a course that flatters this audience isn't useful to them.

- **Lesson 1:** The two questions, and the quadrants. Presence is not seniority; contested judgment is not difficulty.
- **Lesson 2:** Where AI has actually landed in HR `[V]`. 138 use cases across 16 practice areas, concentrated in recruiting (27%), HR technology (21%), L&D (17%), employee experience (14%) — and least adopted in employee relations, talent management, org design, and compliance. The HRBP's core territory is the least-penetrated part of HR.
- **Lesson 3:** Least-penetrated is not protected. It's the hard part — and hard parts get attacked last, not never. What would have to become true for each quadrant to move.
- **Lesson 4:** The pressure, said plainly `[V]`. Gartner's pods-of-strategic-talent-leaders recommendation and the ratio moving from ~1:423 toward 1:800–1,200 within one to five years; Bersin's independent 30–40%-fewer-people version. Two firms, same direction. What that implies for which quadrant you want to be excellent in.
- **Interactive:** sorting — twelve pieces of real HRBP work into the four quadrants.
- **Activity:** *"One week, sorted"* — log a real week, produce your own quadrant distribution, and name one piece of work you'll defend and one you'll surrender. **Calibration:** predict your presence-and-contested-judgment share before you log.
- **Knowledge check:** 8 questions.

## Module 3 · The honest arithmetic

*~45 min · the trust-earning module*

The module the vendors will never build: what AI actually costs in time, and whether the savings are real — pointed first at your own workflows, then at their claims.

- **Lesson 1:** The productivity paradox `[V]`. Workers believe AI saves them ~11 hours a week; only 13% say their organization is performing significantly better. Measured against that: an average 6.4 hours a week of **botsitting** — feeding context, supervising output, debugging, cleaning up downstream — rarely recognized in workload planning.
- **Lesson 2:** The four-question workflow audit. Total human time *including* review and rework; whether final quality actually improved; where the work moved to; and what the freed capacity became. Only 7% of organizations give any guidance on how to use the time AI saves `[V]` — which is why the fourth question is the one that matters.
- **Lesson 3:** The trap. When content gets cheaper to produce, organizations produce more of it — so you get faster at generating work and slower at deciding which work matters. The HRBP-specific version: more decks, more surveys, more frameworks, no more decisions.
- **Lesson 4:** Their arithmetic, torn down `[V]`. IBM's $300M attrition-prediction figure as a CEO claim never independently audited; HireVue quietly dropping facial analysis when its own data showed <0.25% predictive lift; Amazon scrapping a recruiting tool that taught itself to penalize "women's." Where proof actually clusters — front door and back office, Chipotle 12 days to 4, 7-Eleven 40,000 hours a week — versus where the loudest claims live: assessment, prediction, people analytics. **The heuristic: automating the "yes" is safe; automating the "no" is where the risk starts.**
- **Interactive:** choice — four vendor claims; find the one that survives scrutiny.
- **Activity:** *"One workflow, measured"* — one of your own workflows end to end, with a named, specific destination for the recovered time. Vague destinations ("more strategic work") score low; that's the point. **Calibration:** predict your own weekly botsitting hours, then measure.
- **Knowledge check:** 8 questions.

## Module 4 · Adversarial rehearsal

*~45 min · the signature module · the tutor is the primary surface*

Every other AI use in this curriculum makes your work. This one attacks it. It is the highest-frequency, least-served use in the role: preparing for a conversation you're about to have with someone who will push back.

- **Lesson 1:** Rehearsal, not production. Why a model that agrees with you is worse than no model — and why AI's willingness to argue any position, a liability everywhere else, is the whole point here.
- **Lesson 2:** The adversary set. The high performer who finds this demotivating; the frontline manager who has to explain it and defend their operating numbers; the board member who wants data; the front-page headline if it lands badly. Each surfaces a different failure.
- **Lesson 3:** Making it sting. How to stop the model flattering you — commit your position first, forbid hedging, demand the strongest version of the objection, and ask for the thing you *don't* want to hear. Plus the champion map: feed leadership-call Q&A and ask which individuals and teams are asking the most forward-thinking questions, and what would help them build support.
- **Lesson 4:** Reading its logic. When the output is wrong, ask the model to explain how it got from A to B — then rewrite your rules once you can see the reasoning. (This is 101 M6's verification instinct, aimed at an argument instead of a fact.)
- **Interactive:** choice — four rehearsal transcripts; identify the one that actually attacked the plan rather than flattering the planner.
- **Activity:** *"Run the set"* — a real upcoming decision through the full adversary set, in the tutor. Submit the exchange plus **the three objections you hadn't anticipated**. Graded on whether the rehearsal was adversarial or performed. **Calibration:** predict how many objections you'll fail to anticipate.
- **Knowledge check:** 8 questions.

## Module 5 · Arguing with finance

*~40 min*

HRBPs lose because they arrive with a story and Finance arrives with a model. This module is about arriving with a model — and about the data problem that makes most first attempts worse than useless.

- **Lesson 1:** Story versus model. What Finance is actually listening for, and why "engagement will improve" loses to a number with an assumption attached.
- **Lesson 2:** Three models an HRBP should be able to build — cost of regretted attrition in your unit, real cost of the extra layer, revenue impact of time-to-fill against plan. Rough, defensible, assumption-visible.
- **Lesson 3:** The foundation problem `[V]`. It's a data problem, not a technology problem: most HR teams can't train AI on the information it needs, and you can't put AI on top of a shaky foundation. The HRBP-specific version — *you* are the only person who knows "regrettable attrition" is flagged by whoever closed the req, and that ratings drift by function. **AI on bad HR data produces confident garbage at speed, and confident garbage in front of a CFO is worse than no model at all.**
- **Lesson 4:** Small numbers. Your unit is 90 people; the design team is six. The most common way an HRBP produces confident garbage is running population math on a population too small to bear it — and the fix is stating the interval, not finding a better prompt.
- **Interactive:** choice — four business cases; find the one whose numbers can't carry the claim.
- **Activity:** *"One case, stress-tested"* — a real business case with its own weakest assumption identified **by you, before anyone else finds it.** **Calibration:** predict the cost of one regretted departure in your unit, then build it.
- **Knowledge check:** 8 questions.

## Module 6 · The line

*~45 min · the heaviest module · counsel review required before ship*

Two halves that belong together: the law about the *systems* touching your population, and the law about the *documents* on your desk.

- **Lesson 1:** Where the legal line is `[V]`. As of early 2026, 19 of the most populous states have AI laws touching employment — and 57% of HR professionals in those states are unaware of them. Illinois HB 3773; Colorado's replacement act; Connecticut's disclosure duty when AI influenced a mass layoff. The EU trap: high-risk employment obligations moved to December 2027, but the **emotion-recognition ban and the AI literacy obligation have been in force since February 2025** — and the emotion ban is an absolute prohibition, not a risk to manage.
- **Lesson 2:** The agent doctrine `[V]`. A tool that screens candidates on your behalf can be treated as your agent: employers lose "that's the vendor's problem," vendors lose "we only make the software." Plus the nuance sophisticated employers are already using — bias-testing data may be privileged when counsel curated it and used the results in providing legal advice.
- **Lesson 3:** ER documentation that survives scrutiny. Investigation summaries, PIP language, termination rationale — the highest-stakes writing you do, and the writing most likely to be read years later by someone hostile. Where AI helps (structure, consistency, and the use nobody thinks of: ***"what did I fail to ask?"***), and where it stops cold (credibility determinations, conclusions, characterizations of a person). What uncertain provenance does to a document in discovery.
- **Lesson 4:** What you can do this quarter without waiting for counsel — and the specific list to bring them so the answer isn't a shrug.
- **Interactive:** sorting — ten uses of AI in ER and investigation work: safe / safe with discipline / never.
- **Activity:** *"Inventory and standard"* — every AI system touching your population with its deployer named, three questions for your next vendor conversation, and a one-page ER documentation standard for your own team. **Calibration:** predict how many of your peers know their own state's law.
- **Knowledge check:** 8 questions.

## Module 7 · Manager capability as product

*~40 min · the course lands*

The leverage play, taught honestly — including the evidence that constrains it.

- **Lesson 1:** The demand is not in question `[V]`. Only 8% of HR leaders believe their managers can use AI effectively; only 14% of organizations give managers any support integrating it.
- **Lesson 2:** Catching it in the wild. The AI-drafted review that says nothing, the manager who pasted a complaint into a consumer chatbot, the PIP with a fabricated citation. What to say, what to prohibit, and how to correct without making the manager hide it next time. (101 M8's blame-safe reporting, applied to someone else's mistake.)
- **Lesson 3:** What the evidence says about AI coaching `[V]`. A three-arm RCT of 114 mostly-senior managers found human coaching produced significant gains in goal attainment, stress, and effectiveness; AI coaching showed no statistically significant improvement over control on any primary outcome, with roughly ten times the attrition. **The researcher whose earlier study launched the category co-authored the null result** — which is the model of intellectual honesty this course is asking the learner to practice. Narrowly-scoped bots aimed at one outcome do work; broad ones don't.
- **Lesson 4:** Allocation is an equity decision, and it's yours. Outcomes were predicted most strongly by the coachee's starting self-efficacy and hope — **the employees who most need development are the ones an unsupervised bot serves worst.** Deciding who gets a human is not procurement. And the caveat that keeps us honest: the difficult-conversation rehearsal category is crowded, every number in it traces to a vendor, and we hold our own recommendations to the standard we hold theirs.
- **Interactive:** sorting — ten manager-support needs: bot / human / neither.
- **Activity (course close):** *"One asset, one manager"* — a manager-facing asset built and tested with **one real manager**, plus what they actually did with it. Then **the calibration reckoning**: every prediction from all seven modules, the direction of the pattern, and what it says about where your judgment about AI systematically leans.
- **Knowledge check:** 8 questions.

## The two arguments the course refuses to settle

Both are genuinely contested, and both run as **tutor scenario challenges** rather than prose — the tutor is the surface that can actually argue back, which is the async answer to a room discussion.

1. **Should HR lead AI governance?** 52% of organizations don't involve HR in AI strategy at all — and asked what role HR *should* play, only 16% supported HR leading change management and 15% leading training; for four of six activities, more would rather not be involved than lead `[V]`. That is a self-inflicted wound, and the learner should have to defend a position on it. Attaches to M7.
2. **What is the recovered time actually for?** Said out loud, with a number. Attaches to M3, where it is also the activity's grading edge.

## Decisions (v1)

1. **Async and self-serve. No flipped classroom.** The source material was drafted as a cohort workshop with a live Demo Day; that energy is preserved through mechanisms the product already has — the calibration reckoning (M7), tutor scenario challenges for the two arguments, and one activity (M7) that requires a real human by design. No cohort, roster, or facilitator surface is assumed or needed.
2. **No external prerequisite pointer.** The source outline sent unprepared learners to a third-party intro course; we ship AI 101 for exactly that, and the ladder already routes them.
3. **Vendor-claim teardown lives in M3, not M6.** The source had it doubled into the legal module. Measuring your own claimed savings honestly and measuring a vendor's honestly are the same muscle aimed in two directions — and it leaves M6 room for the ER-documentation half.
4. **Seven modules, numbered from 1.** The source used a Module 0; the data model orders from 1. Nothing requires courses to be the same length as each other, and seven that earn their place beats eight with a passenger.
5. **One anchor statistic per module.** The source is citation-dense — a strength as research, a liability as prose. Each module leads on one number and treats the rest as support or cuts it. Anchors: M1 the 31%; M2 the 138-use-case map; M3 the 87/75/50; M4 none (the only module argued from craft, not data); M5 the foundation problem; M6 the 19 states / 57% unaware; M7 the RCT.
6. **This course inverts the stable/volatile ratio and that's expected.** Roughly 80% of its claims are volatile-layer — statistics, statutes, ratios, vendor specifics — against maybe 20% in 101. `scripts/maintenance-agent.mjs` already walks volatile blocks with web search and proposes patches; this track will be its heaviest customer, which is what makes that story real rather than aspirational. Mark aggressively.
7. **Every statistic traced before drafting.** The course's own standard (101 M6: a claim you can't trace gets cut, never shipped on vibes) applies to us first. Four load-bearing claims are verified: the coaching RCT (de Haan, Terblanche & Nowack, *Human Resource Development International*, 2026, N=114); Glean's Work AI Index 2026 botsitting figure (n=6,000, fielded Dec 2025–Jan 2026); the EU Digital Omnibus deferral of Annex III employment obligations to 2 December 2027; and SHRM's 87% / 75% / 50% split. Unverified and blocking for M6: the state statute effective dates, Mobley's current posture, and the privilege ruling.
8. **The adversary set is a seed, not a source.** The technique came from a vendor listicle. The framework we teach is ours, developed past it — and we don't claim "nothing like this exists" while citing something public that does.

## Open questions for review

- **Track storage.** Separate course id (`ai301-hrbp`) so a learner sees only their own track, mapped from the `roleLabel` we already collect at intake — versus one `ai301` course with role-variant blocks. Leaning separate courses; needs deciding before seeding, and needs an answer for learners whose role has no authored track yet.
- **M7's "one real manager" requirement.** It's the best activity in the course and the only one that depends on another person. Does an IC or consultant learner get an alternate path, or do we accept that this track assumes a manager population?
- **Does M6 want to split?** Legal systems plus ER documentation is the heaviest 45 minutes in the curriculum. Ship as one and watch completion, or split now into eight modules?
- **Does this track need its own diagnostic?** 101's diagnostic places a newcomer. A 301 learner arrives with a 201 history — the calibration record might place them better than a quiz would, and that data already exists.
- **Counsel review of M6** before ship — same gate as 101 M7, and heavier here.
