# AI 301 · DEI — assessment, not an outline

**Why this file has a different name from the others.** The authoring brief flags DEI with a
question rather than a recommendation: *"heavy overlap with pay equity and adverse impact; may be
lessons rather than a track."* That question has to be answered before an outline is written, the
same way the brief tells us to check whether the CHRO audience belongs at 401. I explored the role
and ran the verification first. **The answer is no track** — and the verification turned up
something more urgent than a new course, including a correction to shipped content.

The steelman for a track is in §5, so this can be argued rather than accepted.

---

## 1. The verdict, up front

**DEI should not be a 301 track.** Three independent reasons, any one of which would be
disqualifying on its own:

1. **The subtraction leaves two modules, not seven.** Pay equity belongs to the comp track, adverse
   impact computation to the recruiter track, bias-as-a-property-of-data to 101 M7, the systems
   inventory to HRBP M6, and listening to the EX/comms track. What survives is genuinely excellent
   and genuinely unowned — but it is one subject seen from three angles, not a course.
2. **The audience dispersed, and `roles.ts` needs a single pick.** DEI job postings fell roughly
   43% from their August 2022 peak through July 2024; roles declined without disappearing, and the
   consistent pattern is absorption — responsibilities rolling into broader HR functions, and
   retitling to Culture and Belonging Lead, People Equity Partner, Workforce Inclusion Strategist,
   "People and Culture," "belonging" `[V]`. A learner whose DEI work is now 30% of an HRBP job will
   pick HRBP at intake. Content that follows the work reaches them; a track doesn't.
3. **By the brief's own test, it fails.** *"If a proposed track reads like the HRBP track with
   different nouns, it is wrong."* The best steelman I can build (§5) has two role-discovered
   modules out of seven. HRBP had four, recruiter three, comp three, EX/comms three. Two is a
   distribution problem wearing a course's clothes.

**What to do instead is in §4** — and one of those items is a correction to a module that is
shipped and open to learners right now.

## 2. What the job actually is, in 2026

Not the 2021 job. The function has been reshaped by law and by budget, and the reshaping is the
most interesting thing about it.

- **Measurement and reporting.** Representation, hiring/promotion/attrition rates by group, pay
  gaps, EEO-1, EU reporting. The most data-heavy part, and the part most changed by both AI and law.
- **Selection-process analysis.** Adverse impact testing on hiring, promotion, and termination.
- **Program design.** ERGs, sponsorship, mentorship, inclusive-leadership development.
- **Process advisory.** Structured interviews, calibration design, promotion equity.
- **Being the approver.** The person asked to bless a vendor's fairness claim, and whose name is on
  "we reviewed this."
- **Legal navigation.** Now the largest and least discretionary part of the job.

### What's distinctive

**They are the only function whose core activity is legally encouraged and legally hazardous at the
same time, depending on which office the employee sits in.** Nothing else in the curriculum has
this property.

**Their work product is evidence.** An adverse impact analysis is discoverable. Running it and not
acting on it is worse than never running it — and never running it is also a position with
consequences. Every other function's documents become evidence incidentally; this function's
documents are evidence by design.

**They are structurally the counterparty, not the owner.** They rarely own the hiring process, the
pay model, or the AI procurement decision. They are asked to approve them. That is a different job
from every other track's audience, all of whom own something.

coincidence — it's what happens when a subject is genuinely cross-cutting.

### The two things that survive, and they are good

**The evidence problem.** AI collapsed the cost of generating evidence against your own employer.
An adverse impact analysis that used to require a statistician, a data pull, and a budget approval
now takes four minutes in a chat window — and leaves a trace, in a tool with retention, run by
someone who didn't ask counsel. The old protection was friction. Friction is gone. Privilege is the
partial answer that comp M4 already gestures at, but the *volume* problem is new: the question is
no longer "should we run it" but "what happens when forty people can, casually, and one of them
does." Nothing in the curriculum touches this and it is the sharpest single idea in the whole
exploration.

**The jurisdiction problem.** One program, contradictory regimes, and a model that will confidently
give you one answer because it has no idea which frame you're in. See §6 for why this is harder
right now than it has ever been.

## 4. What to do instead

Ranked by urgency. The first one is live.

**A. Correct 101 M7 Lesson 3. It is shipped, open, and now partly stale.** The lesson currently
reads: *"U.S. federal enforcement agencies have signaled that existing employment law fully applies
to algorithmic decisions"* and *"that expectation is increasingly regulatory, not just prudent."*
Both were true when written and are no longer accurate at the federal level (§6). The doctrinal
claim in the same lesson — *"disparate impact doesn't need intent"* — is statutory and unchanged,
so the fix is surgical, not a rewrite.

And the corrected version is a **better** lesson than the original, because it teaches the thing
this whole subject now turns on: **disparate impact is codified in Title VII by the Civil Rights Act
of 1991; an executive order cannot repeal it; federal agencies deprioritized enforcement; and the
private plaintiffs' bar — which no executive order binds, before courts that remain open — is
picking it up.** The duty did not move. The enforcement posture did. A learner who understands that
distinction can reason about any regulatory reversal in either direction, which is exactly what a
volatile-layer lesson should buy them. It is also the same lesson the EX/comms track's M7 arrives at
independently via the rescinded NLRB memo, which is a good sign it's a real principle rather than a
local fact.

The lesson is already `[V]` and already carries a counsel-review flag, so the mechanism exists.
**This is a maintenance-agent job with a counsel read, not a re-authoring job.**

**B. Add the evidence problem to the comp track's M4, and cross-reference from recruiter R6.**
Comp M4 is "The exclusion zone" and already teaches pay equity regression under privilege — it is
one lesson away from owning this. The addition: friction was the old control, AI removed it, and the
governing question is now who can run the analysis rather than whether it should be run. Recruiter
R6's tool audit should point at it.

**C. Fold the normalization trap into recruiter R4 and the HRBP performance material.** A model
trained on your historical reviews learns your historical bias, so a tool that flags "unusual"
language is flagging deviation from a biased norm — the inclusive-language checker that enforces the
thing it was bought to fix. One paragraph, high value, and it lands better inside a module about
selection design than in a course of its own.

**D. Update the recruiter and comp outlines before they're drafted.** Neither is authored yet, so
this is free. Recruiter R6's "adverse impact you can actually compute" survives intact as *method* —
the four-fifths rule still measures what it measures — but its framing assumes a regulatory posture
that has reversed, and the module should teach the private-litigation channel as the live exposure.
Comp M4's statutory floor names the EU Pay Transparency Directive; §6 has the transposition reality,
which is materially different from what that outline assumes.

**E. Leave the jurisdiction problem for 401.** It is real, it is unowned, and it is a *cross-team
leadership and governance* problem — running one program across contradictory regimes, deciding what
the organization's position is, and defending it. That is the brief's own definition of 401 · The
Translator. A specialist track goes deep in one role's craft; this goes wide across an organization's
posture.

**F. If the DEI audience needs a home at intake, point it at HRBP** — which `roles.ts` already does
by default — and reconsider only if a deployment surfaces a real population of full-time DEI
practitioners.

## 5. The steelman, so this can be argued

The strongest version of a track I can build. Presented so the verdict can be overruled with
something concrete rather than in the abstract.

**"The Fairness Counterparty"** — reframed away from program ownership toward the position the role
actually occupies: the person asked to approve other people's systems and hold the record.

| | Module | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Prediction / where the ground moved | Convention slot |
| 2 | Sort your own job — which DEI work survived the retrenchment | Convention slot |
| 3 | **The evidence problem** — AI collapsed the cost of generating evidence against your employer | **Role-discovered, unowned** |
| 4 | **The jurisdiction problem** — one program, contradictory regimes, 23 of 27 member states themselves non-compliant | **Role-discovered, unowned** |
| 5 | Language at scale and the normalization trap | One good lesson, stretched |
| 6 | Being the approver — reading someone else's bias audit, and what your signature means | Recruiter R6 + HRBP M6 from a different chair |
| 7 | The mandate / what the function is for now | Convention slot |

**Two of seven are role-discovered.** Modules 1, 2, and 7 are the arc convention — which the brief
explicitly warns is not where value lives. Module 5 is a lesson. Module 6 is re-pointed content.

That is the honest count, and it is the argument. If the counter is *"but 3 and 4 are better than
most modules in the curriculum"* — agreed, they are. That's why §4 puts them somewhere they'll
actually get built and read, rather than gating them behind five modules that mostly exist to
surround them.

## 6. Verification, which is why this document exists

I ran these before deciding the shape, not after, and they are the reason the answer is "no track"
rather than "seven modules."

**The US federal posture reversed, and further than I knew.** `[V]`

- **EO 14173** (January 2025) requires federal contractors to certify they operate no "illegal DEI
  programs" and directs the Attorney General toward investigations and litigation.
- **EO 14281** (April 2025) directs agencies to "eliminate" disparate impact liability in civil
  rights enforcement.
- Within a month of that order the **EEOC dropped its pending disparate impact cases** and cut
  financial backing to state agencies bringing similar claims.
- **June 2026: the EEOC's new National Enforcement Plan prioritizes disparate treatment theories
  over disparate impact.**
- **March 2026: a further executive order** targets "racially discriminatory DEI activities" by
  federal contractors and subcontractors — five months ago, and newer than anything in our content.
- Litigation challenging EO 14173 on separation-of-powers and First Amendment grounds is live. The
  landscape is unsettled in both directions.

**And the thing that makes it a teachable principle rather than a news item:** disparate impact was
**codified into Title VII by the Civil Rights Act of 1991.** An executive order did not and could
not eliminate it. Most employment discrimination cases are brought by the private plaintiffs' bar,
which no executive order binds, before federal courts that remain open to the claims — and reporting
indicates private plaintiff attorneys are **stepping up** in Title VII disparate impact cases.
**Federal enforcement retreated; private exposure did not, and may have concentrated.**

**The EU is not the clean mirror image, and that matters.** `[V]` The Pay Transparency Directive
*tightened* while sustainability reporting *loosened*:

- The Pay Transparency Directive's **transposition deadline of 7 June 2026 passed with only four of
  27 member states meeting it** — Slovakia, Italy, Lithuania, and Malta. The Netherlands, Sweden,
  Czechia, and Denmark have confirmed delays to 1 January 2027; the Commission has signalled no
  extension and no carve-out. Twenty-three member states are in some state of non-compliance,
  partial implementation, or resistance.
- **CSRD was scaled back by the Omnibus I directive**: reduced scope, higher thresholds, sector
  standards removed, and — specifically — diversity reporting narrowed to gender distribution at top
  management, with age distribution dropped and the family-leave datapoint made non-mandatory.

So the real picture is not "US retreats, EU advances." It is **four regimes moving in different
directions at different speeds, with the largest bloc's own members out of compliance with their
own directive.** That's what makes the jurisdiction problem hard, and it's also why it belongs at
401 rather than in a specialist's craft course.

**The profession itself.** `[V]` DEI job postings down roughly 43% from the August 2022 peak through
July 2024; Revelio Labs data on Russell 3000 companies showing roles declined without disappearing;
widespread retitling and absorption into broader HR functions; PepsiCo eliminating its chief DEI
officer role in favour of a broader mandate.

**Blocking before any of §4 is drafted.** The Revelio Labs figures against the primary source rather
than secondary reporting. The March 2026 executive order's operative text and scope. The current
posture of litigation against EO 14173. State-level pay transparency and reporting regimes, which
moved independently of the federal picture and which none of this checked. And **every item in §4
touching law needs the counsel gate** — item A especially, because it edits shipped content.

## 7. What I'd want argued

- **Is the audience really gone, or is it hiding?** My §1.2 argument rests on the postings decline
  and the absorption pattern. If you know of a real population of full-time practitioners — in
  regulated industries, in the EU, in federal contracting where the certification requirement
  creates dedicated work — that changes the answer, and it's the argument most likely to overturn
  this.
- **Does the evidence problem deserve its own module somewhere, rather than a lesson in comp M4?**
  It is strong enough that I nearly recommended a two-module micro-track, and I don't have a good
  place for a two-module course in the ladder.
- **Should the jurisdiction problem be pulled forward instead of waiting for 401?** 401 isn't
  started. Real people are making these calls this quarter.
- **Is 101 M7's fix surgical or structural?** I think three sentences and a counsel read. Someone
  who disagrees should say so before the maintenance agent touches a shipped module.
