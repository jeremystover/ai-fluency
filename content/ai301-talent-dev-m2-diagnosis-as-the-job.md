## Module brief

Module 1 left you with a distribution and a killed program. This module is about the end of the
chain that decides whether anything gets onto that list in the first place.

The premise is short and it is the most expensive idea in this track:

> **Building a course used to be expensive, and the expense was doing work nobody counted. It was
> the filter.**

When a program cost six weeks of a designer's time, "this is a process problem, training won't fix
it" was an argument you could win. Not because anyone valued diagnosis — most organizations never
have — but because the alternative was visibly costly, and someone had to sign for it. The
economics did the gatekeeping that the profession was never given the authority to do.

That filter is gone. The honest diagnosis now competes against an afternoon's work, and it loses by
default unless somebody makes it explicit and defends it out loud.

There is a second-order version of this that is worse. The expensive middle also *created* the
diagnosis, as a side effect. You worked out what the problem really was while you were building for
it — in the SME interviews, in the third rewrite of the objectives, in the moment the practice
scenario refused to work because the underlying process was broken. **Nobody scheduled that
diagnosis. It happened because the production was slow enough to think in.** Take the slowness out
and the thinking does not automatically relocate. It just stops.

So this module makes diagnosis a deliberate act with a method, because it is no longer going to
arrive as a by-product of anything.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

1. Distinguish collaboration from diagnosis, and explain why taking the order feels like service and
   functions as abdication.
2. Apply the knowledge-versus-willingness test, and state what training can and cannot fix.
3. Use the environmental-causes prior as a working default — and say honestly what kind of evidence
   it rests on.
4. Explain the accidental-safeguard argument and the SME-displacement version of it.
5. Write a refusal that survives contact: one that names what you *will* do, and accounts for your
   own incentives.

## Lesson 1 · Collaboration is not diagnosis

Two postures look similar from outside and produce opposite work.

**Collaboration** is being easy to work with. You take the request, clarify the scope, agree the
timeline, and deliver something good against it. Everyone leaves the meeting satisfied. This is what
most stakeholders mean when they say a business partner is responsive, and it is genuinely valuable
right up until the request is wrong.

**Diagnosis** is refusing to accept the problem statement as given. Someone tells you what they want
built; you go and find out what is actually happening. Those produce different meetings, and the
second one is less pleasant.

Here is the trap, and it is specific to this profession: **L&D is structurally rewarded for
collaboration and structurally unmeasured on diagnosis.** Delivering the thing that was asked for is
visible, countable, and appreciated within the quarter. Establishing that the thing shouldn't be
built is invisible, uncountable, and creates friction with the person who asked. If you had designed
a set of incentives to eliminate diagnosis from a function, you would have designed these.

Which is why it needs to be a named step with an artifact, rather than a professional value that
everyone endorses and nobody schedules.

**The request is a symptom report, not a specification.** When a manager says "my team needs
training on giving feedback," they have observed something real — feedback in their team is bad —
and then performed an amateur diagnosis and handed you the prescription. You would not accept a
prescription from a patient. You would take the symptom seriously and do your own work.

The reframe that makes this sayable out loud without sounding obstructive: *"Tell me what you saw
that made you think of training."* It accepts the observation, declines the prescription, and cannot
be heard as a refusal, because it isn't one.

## Lesson 2 · Knowledge, willingness, and the prior **[V]**

The first cut, and it is brutally simple.

**Training only ever fixes people who *can't*. It never fixes people who *don't*.**

If someone lacks knowledge or skill, instruction can close that gap — that is what instruction is
for. If someone knows perfectly well how to do the thing and is not doing it, the cause is
somewhere else entirely: the incentives point the other way, the process makes it impossible, the
tools don't support it, their manager rewards the opposite, nobody has ever noticed whether they do
it, or they tried once and it went badly.

**Training a willingness problem is the single most common failure in this profession**, and it is
worse than doing nothing. It costs money, consumes the goodwill you would need for the real
intervention, and — the part that matters — **produces evidence that the problem was addressed.**
The course ran. Attendance was 94%. The satisfaction scores were good. Now nobody will look at the
actual cause for eighteen months.

The test in one question: *if I offered this person a large sum of money to do it correctly
tomorrow, could they?* If yes, it is not a knowledge problem, and no amount of instruction will
touch it.

### The prior, and where it comes from

The performance-technology literature carries a widely-repeated finding: something like **75–80% of
the factors influencing workplace performance are environmental rather than individual** —
information, resources, and incentives rather than knowledge, capacity, and motives. It descends
from Gilbert's Behavior Engineering Model, which is one of the foundational frameworks in the
discipline.

**Now look at that number the way this track is going to teach you to look at numbers**, because it
matters more that you can do this than that you accept the figure.

It is a round range. It is attributed to a *model* rather than to a study. It is cited constantly
and traced rarely. Chase it and you find consensus rather than a sample — the kind of claim that
gets repeated until repetition becomes the evidence. **That is exactly the profile of the learning
pyramid's retention percentages and 70-20-10's ratios**, both of which you will meet in Module 3,
and both of which turn out to be fabrications or retrospective self-reports dressed as findings.

So we are not going to pretend this one is better sourced than it is. Two things are true at once:

1. **As a number, treat it with the suspicion you would apply to a vendor's.** If you find yourself
   putting "research shows 80%" on a slide, you have become the thing Module 3 is about.
2. **As a prior, it earns its place anyway** — because it does not need to be precise to be useful.
   Its job is to set the default assumption you walk into an intake with. "Probably environmental
   until shown otherwise" changes which questions you ask first. It would change them at 60% and at
   85%.

A prior is a starting posture, not a finding. This one is worth holding and not worth citing.

> ### Try this — 3 minutes
> Take the last program your function delivered. Ask the money question about its target audience:
> could they have done the thing correctly tomorrow, for a large enough reward? If the answer is
> yes, you have just identified a willingness problem that received a knowledge intervention — and
> you are almost certainly still carrying the program in your catalog.

## Lesson 3 · The accidental safeguard

Now the argument this module is built on, in full.

Production time was expensive. Everyone in this profession has complained about it — the six-week
build, the SME who won't return your calls, the review cycle that eats a fortnight. It was
genuinely wasteful, and removing it is genuinely good.

**And it was doing three jobs nobody had assigned it.**

**It was the economic filter.** Somebody had to approve six weeks of a designer's time. That
approval was a checkpoint, however crude, and bad requests died at it — not because anyone diagnosed
them, but because they weren't worth the cost. When the cost approaches zero, the checkpoint
disappears silently. Nobody removed it. It just stopped stopping anything.

**It forced the SME conversation.** To build the thing, you had to extract the content from
somebody who knew it. That conversation was slow and awkward, and it was also where you found out
that the real problem was the handoff between two teams, or that the process everyone described
hasn't been followed since 2022. **That was never scheduled as diagnosis. It happened because
production required it.** Now the model can produce a plausible first draft from public knowledge
and a job description, and the SME conversation becomes optional — which means the discovery it
carried becomes optional too, and optional things in a busy quarter do not happen.

**It rationed volume.** A function that could ship four programs a year had to choose four. Choosing
is a form of thinking. A function that can ship forty does not have to choose, and mostly won't.

None of this is an argument for keeping production slow — that would be absurd, and you couldn't
anyway. It is an argument that **three real functions were being performed by a cost you have now
removed, and if you don't rebuild them deliberately, you don't have them.** They were not features
of the profession's discipline. They were side effects of its inefficiency.

The uncomfortable corollary: functions that were *worst* at deliberate diagnosis had the most
protection from the accidental kind, and will feel this collapse hardest. Being bad at this and
slow was survivable. Being bad at this and fast is not.

## Lesson 4 · The refusal that survives

Diagnosis is worthless if the conclusion cannot be delivered. This lesson is about the delivery.

**A refusal that only declines gets routed around — and now it gets routed around to someone who
will generate the course by Friday.** That is new. Five years ago, being told "no" by L&D meant the
requester had to either accept it or find budget. Today it means they open a chat window. Your veto
was never absolute; it is now barely a speed bump, and the only thing that still works is being
*more useful than the alternative* rather than more authoritative.

So the shape that lands has three parts:

**What you found.** The actual cause, stated concretely and without triumph. *"I talked to six
people on the team. Every one of them can describe good feedback accurately. Four of them told me
they gave difficult feedback last year and it went badly for them in their review."*

**What that means training would and wouldn't do.** Specific, not doctrinal. *"A course would
confirm what they already know. It wouldn't touch the thing that's actually stopping them, and it
would tell the team we think the problem is their skill."* That last clause is usually the one that
lands, because it names a cost the requester hadn't considered.

**What you will do instead.** Non-negotiable. This is what separates a diagnosis from an
obstruction. It might be small — a change to the review calibration, a conversation with two
managers, a decision that needs making at a level above the requester. It has to be *something you
are offering to own*, or you have handed back a problem with a lecture attached.

### And your own incentives, honestly

There is a version of this module that would let you off too easily, so here is the counterweight.

**"It's not a training problem" has always been available as a way to avoid work.** It is a
sophisticated-sounding refusal that requires no delivery, and every experienced practitioner has
used it at least once when the real reason was a full quarter. The requester often knows this, which
is why diagnosis can be heard as evasion even when it is rigorous.

The discipline that keeps you honest is the third part above. **A diagnosis that concludes in
something you will own cannot be an avoidance move**, because it costs you something. If your
diagnoses systematically conclude that the problem belongs to another function and requires nothing
from you, that is a pattern worth examining before anyone else examines it.

### And the newest version of the same trade

Everything above was about the *cost of production* falling. There is a second collapse arriving
behind it, and it lands on the refusal itself.

**The structural diagnosis is now cheap to produce too.** Describe a performance problem to a model
and you will get a competent systems analysis in an afternoon — the incentive misalignment, the
handoff failure, the ambiguity in who decides. Often a good one. It will read like the output of a
practitioner who spent two weeks in the business.

That sounds like it strengthens your hand. **Mostly it does the opposite**, for a reason worth
holding:

> **The diagnosis was never the scarce thing. The standing to deliver it was.**

Telling a VP that their problem is how they run their leadership meeting is not hard to *write*. It
is hard to *say*, and it is only survivable if the person saying it has credibility built somewhere
else and something of their own to offer. A generated critique changes none of that — and if you
circulate it as analysis rather than as a conversation you have earned, **it reads as an outsider's
verdict on a function you don't work in**, which is the fastest way to lose the argument you were
right about.

Two practical consequences.

**Use it to prepare, not to deliver.** The model is very good at generating the alternative
explanations you did not think of, which is exactly the failure mode in Lesson 1. Take those into
the conversation as questions.

**And expect it pointed back at you.** The business can run the same analysis on the learning
function, in the same afternoon, and conclude your portfolio has no evidence behind it. That
critique will also be fluent, also plausible, and — given Module 1 — **sometimes right.** The
defensible position is not to have avoided it; it is to have run it on yourself first.

## Key takeaways

- **The request is a symptom report, not a specification.** The requester observed something real
  and then handed you their own amateur diagnosis. *"Tell me what you saw that made you think of
  training"* accepts the first and declines the second.
- **L&D is structurally rewarded for collaboration and structurally unmeasured on diagnosis** —
  which is why diagnosis has to be a named step with an artifact, not a value everyone endorses.
- **Training fixes people who can't. It never fixes people who don't.** The test: could they do it
  tomorrow for a large enough reward?
- **Training a willingness problem is worse than doing nothing**, because it produces evidence that
  the problem was addressed and buys the real cause eighteen months of quiet.
- **The environmental prior is worth holding and not worth citing** `[V]`. A round range attributed
  to a model rather than a study — treat the number the way you'd treat a vendor's, and keep the
  posture it produces.
- **Production time was the filter, it forced the SME conversation, and it rationed volume.** Three
  real jobs performed by a cost you have removed. Rebuild them deliberately or don't have them.
- **A refusal that only declines gets routed around** to someone who will build it by Friday. The
  shape that survives: what you found, what training would and wouldn't do, and what you will own
  instead.
- **A diagnosis that concludes in nothing you own cannot be distinguished from avoidance** — and
  the requester can tell.
- **The diagnosis was never the scarce thing — the standing to deliver it was.** A generated
  structural critique is cheap, fluent and often right, and circulating it as a verdict on a function
  you don't work in loses the argument you were correct about. Use it to prepare; expect it aimed
  back at your own portfolio, where it will sometimes be right.

## Take a position

**The claim:** *"Most training requests should not become training — and cheap production is
exactly why more of them will."*

The strongest counter-argument is that **the economics genuinely changed, and the gatekeeping
posture is a hangover from when they hadn't.** If a job aid costs two hours, the expected cost of
building the wrong thing has collapsed alongside everything else — so the rational response to cheap
production might be to build more, ship faster, and find out empirically which interventions land,
rather than to spend a diagnostic hour protecting a resource that is no longer scarce. On that view,
diagnosis was always partly a rationing mechanism dressed as professional rigour, and defending it
after the rationing became unnecessary is defending the ritual rather than the reason.

It is a good argument and it has a real edge: **it is testable, and this module never tests it.**
Your position has to say what would distinguish "build more and learn from it" from "produce more
and notice nothing" — and if you can't name the difference, notice that the counter-argument has
just walked you into Module 6's problem, which is that this profession has never been able to tell
those two apart.

## Applied activity — "The request, diagnosed"

**Time:** 30 minutes · **Submit:** the diagnosis plus a 300–400 word write-up · **Graded against the
rubric below.** Score doesn't matter. Doing the work is where the learning lands.

Take **one live request currently in your queue.** A real one, ideally one you have already agreed
to, and ideally one you are slightly uneasy about. If your queue is empty, use the most recent thing
you accepted.

**Step 1 — State the request as given (3 min).** Verbatim if you have it. What was asked for, by
whom, and what they said the problem was.

**Step 2 — Find the observation underneath it (7 min).** What did the requester actually *see*?
Separate the symptom they reported from the prescription they attached. If you don't know, that is
your first finding — and the honest version of this step is a note saying you need to go ask, with
the question you would ask written out.

**Step 3 — Knowledge or willingness (5 min).** Apply the money test to the target population. Say
which it is, and what evidence you have. "I think it's a mix" is allowed and common — but then say
which parts are which, because the mixed answer is where the useful specificity lives.

**Step 4 — The environmental scan (5 min).** Walk the prior. What in the environment — incentives,
process, tools, management behaviour, precedent — could be producing this? Name at least two
candidates and say how you would check one of them. You are not required to be right. You are
required to have looked.

**Step 5 — The conclusion, in the three-part shape (8 min).** What you found. What training would
and wouldn't do. **What you will own instead** — and if your conclusion is that it *is* a training
problem, say so plainly, because that is a legitimate outcome and this activity is not a trick.
*"This isn't a training problem"* must be genuinely available; so must its opposite.

**Step 6 — Score the claim (2 min).** Your opening position against what this exercise showed.
Direction and cause.

Then the write-up: the diagnosis, what changed between the request as given and the request as
understood, your position on the module's claim with its counter-argument addressed, and — the
honest one — **whether your conclusion requires anything of you.** If it doesn't, say why that isn't
avoidance.

## Knowledge check — 8 questions

*Unlocks after the applied activity is submitted. Retakes are free and unlimited.*

**Q1.** What is the difference between collaboration and diagnosis in this module's terms?

- A. Collaboration involves stakeholders; diagnosis is done independently by the L&D team
- B. Collaboration accepts the problem statement as given and delivers well against it; diagnosis refuses the problem statement and establishes what is actually happening ✓
- C. Collaboration is appropriate for small requests and diagnosis for large ones
- D. Collaboration is a soft skill and diagnosis is a technical one

> **B.** Both can be done well or badly. The distinction is whether the requester's framing survives
> contact — and L&D is rewarded for the first and unmeasured on the second, which is why diagnosis
> needs to be a scheduled step rather than a value.

**Q2.** Why is training a willingness problem described as worse than doing nothing?

- A. Because it costs more than other interventions
- B. Because it damages the credibility of the L&D function with senior stakeholders
- C. Because it produces evidence that the problem was addressed — the course ran, attendance was high — which buys the real cause a long period of nobody looking ✓
- D. Because willingness problems always escalate if not addressed immediately

> **C.** The cost is real but secondary. The damage is the false negative it creates in the
> organizational record: a problem now marked as handled.

**Q3.** What does the money test establish?

- A. Whether the intervention is worth its budget
- B. Whether the person could perform correctly if sufficiently motivated — which separates a knowledge gap from everything else ✓
- C. Whether incentives are the primary environmental cause
- D. Whether the request came from a manager with budget authority

> **B.** If a large enough reward would produce the behaviour tomorrow, the capability is already
> there and instruction has nothing to add. C is a plausible-sounding distractor: the test rules
> knowledge in or out, it doesn't identify which environmental cause applies.

**Q4.** How does the module treat the 75–80% environmental-causes figure? `[V]`

- A. As a well-established empirical finding that should anchor the diagnosis method
- B. As a fabrication that should be discarded entirely
- C. As a number with weak provenance — a round range attributed to a model rather than a study — that is worth holding as a prior and not worth citing as evidence ✓
- D. As accurate for manufacturing environments but not for knowledge work

> **C.** Both halves matter. It has the same profile as the learning-pyramid percentages the next
> module dismantles, so it doesn't belong on a slide. But a prior only has to change which questions
> you ask first, and this one would do that at 60% or 85%.

**Q5.** What were the three jobs production time was performing?

- A. Quality control, stakeholder management, and budget justification
- B. The economic filter that killed bad requests, the forced SME conversation that produced diagnosis as a by-product, and the rationing that made choosing necessary ✓
- C. Instructional design, content development, and review
- D. Protecting designer headcount, maintaining vendor relationships, and setting expectations

> **B.** None of them were assigned; all three were side effects of the cost. Which is why removing
> the cost removes them silently — nobody decided to stop doing them.

**Q6.** Why does the module say functions worst at deliberate diagnosis will feel the collapse hardest?

- A. Because they have the least experience negotiating with stakeholders
- B. Because they were most dependent on the accidental diagnosis that slow production produced — being bad at this and slow was survivable, being bad at this and fast is not ✓
- C. Because they typically have smaller budgets and less political capital
- D. Because they are more likely to have adopted AI tools early

> **B.** The protection was inefficiency. Remove it from a function with no deliberate practice
> underneath and there is nothing left holding the diagnosis in place.

**Q7.** What makes a refusal survive contact, according to Lesson 4?

- A. Escalating to a sponsor with more authority than the requester
- B. Citing the research base for why training won't address the problem
- C. Naming what you found, what training would and wouldn't do, and what you will own instead ✓
- D. Documenting the refusal formally so the decision is auditable

> **C.** The third part is load-bearing. A refusal that concludes in nothing you own is
> indistinguishable from avoidance, and a requester who can route around you in an afternoon will
> route around you.

**Q8.** A learner's diagnosis concludes that the problem belongs to another function and requires nothing from L&D. How should they treat that?

- A. As the correct outcome — clean handoffs are the goal of diagnosis
- B. As a legitimate possible conclusion that becomes a warning sign if it is the systematic pattern, since a diagnosis costing you nothing cannot be distinguished from avoidance ✓
- C. As evidence the diagnosis was performed incorrectly and should be redone
- D. As a reason to build the training anyway to preserve the stakeholder relationship

> **B.** Any single diagnosis can honestly land there. The module's discipline is about the pattern:
> if yours always do, the requester's suspicion that this is evasion is better evidenced than your
> claim that it's rigour.
