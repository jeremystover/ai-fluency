# AI 301 · The Specialist — Labor & Employee Relations track · "Under Challenge" (draft v2)

**Audience:** ER specialists and investigators, ER centres of excellence, labor relations managers,
contract administrators, and anyone who runs workplace investigations, grievances, discipline
consistency, or the bargaining relationship — including the many people who do both halves because
the company has one of them.
**Level transition:** L3 The Specialist → L4 The Translator.
**Course id:** `ai301-ler` · role id `ler`, label "Labor & Employee Relations".
**Shape:** 8 modules · 20–40 min each · ~4h · same package as the rest of the ladder — read, micro
dose, tutor, podcast, one interactive, one AI-graded activity, one knowledge check. Async and
self-serve.
**Prerequisite:** AI 101 (or the diagnostic test-out) and AI 201. **No sibling-track prerequisite** —
see Decision 2. This track teaches the investigation-documentation craft in its own specialist form
rather than sending the learner into the HRBP track for it.
**Tooling stance:** Tool-agnostic. Every statute, Board decision, General Counsel memorandum,
contract provision, and survey figure is volatile-layer `[V]` — and this track's authorities move
faster than any other's, which is itself L7's subject.

## The through-line

> **Everyone else's AI output is judged on whether it's good. Yours is judged by someone whose job
> is to break it, under a standard of proof, years after you wrote it.**

Earned, not asserted, from two studies that disagree `[V]`. SHRM's practice-area view puts AI
adoption **lowest** in employee relations of anywhere in HR. HR Acuity's tenth annual benchmark finds
that **70% of ER teams experimented with or actively deployed AI for employee relations and
investigations in 2025**, and that only 56% restrict AI to approved, secured tools for confidential
case content. Both are sound; they measure different things. Institutionally this is the least-AI
part of HR. Behaviourally it is one of the most exposed, and **the gap between those two numbers is
unsanctioned use in the highest-stakes documents the company produces.**

## Where the learner stands (design premise)

They do not run a workflow. They run a process that is designed to be attacked, and they are
underwater: case volumes and serious misconduct claims at or near decade highs, **serious
allegations more than doubled since 2021**, and staffing essentially flat at 0.68 professionals per
1,000 employees `[V]`. The grievances arriving are three times longer than they were and read like
they were drafted by counsel, because in a sense they were. They suspect somebody on their team is
pasting case content into a consumer chatbot, and they are probably right.

> "My files get read under oath by people trying to break them. Tell me what AI can touch without
> handing the other side an argument — and don't tell me to be careful, tell me where the line is."

Three things make this track structurally different from the five beside it. **The AI failure that
matters here is fluency, not error** — a model can be accurate to every source it was given and still
destroy a finding, by producing a coherent account of events that were never coherent. **The spine
cannot be a live case**, because case facts must never be submitted anywhere, which forces the whole
track up a level to process. And **the sharpest content is a line another track deliberately
declined to draw**: HRBP M6 flags comparing witness accounts as "approaching credibility" and stops
there, correctly, for a generalist. For a specialist, drawing it is the job.

## The spine: one matter type, at the level of process

Every module advances **one matter type the learner owns end to end** — a harassment intake process, a
discipline consistency standard, a grievance step, a bargaining subject. Not a case. Never a case.

| Module | What it does to the matter type |
|---|---|
| L1 | Place its work on the evidentiary ladder; find where AI already sits |
| L2 | Write the rule for what a model may do with conflicting accounts |
| L3 | Decide the record: what is captured, by what, with whose consent |
| L4 | Rebuild its report template around the elements that get attacked |
| L5 | Design the comparator query you cannot currently run |
| L6 | Prepare for a counterparty who arrives drafted |
| L7 | Locate every authority you rely on by layer, and date it |
| L8 | Commit to a change, a measure, and a kill condition |

**No activity in this track may accept case facts.** Not the AI-graded submission, not the operator
review queue, not the tutor. Rubrics carry it as a rejection condition rather than a caution — a
submission containing identifiable case content is returned, and the content says so before the first
activity. Artifacts are process artifacts: a triage rubric, a conflict-handling rule, a records
decision, a report template, a query design, a bargaining position, a dated authority map.

**Every module opens with a claim the learner must contest**, per the convention adopted across the
ladder — a factual assertion about their own function, committed to before any content and checked
against evidence during the module. For this track, **evidence means process and aggregate evidence**:
substantiation rates, cycle times, whether a required process exists, what the case management system
can actually query. Never a case. L8 reads the pattern; the rubric grades evidence of updating.

## L1 · The gap and the ladder

*~35 min · the diagnosis and the decomposition, together, because the second explains the first*

**Claim to contest:** *"Nobody on your team is putting case content into an unapproved tool."*

- **Lesson 1:** The two studies that disagree `[V]`, and why both are right. The practice-area view
  puts employee relations at the bottom of AI adoption in HR; the ER-specific benchmark puts 70% of
  ER teams at experimented-or-deployed in 2025, with 56% restricting AI to approved secured tools for
  confidential case content. Institutional adoption versus practitioner behaviour. The sample, stated
  because this module is about evidence: 274 US organizations of 1,000+ employees, 8.8 million
  employees, ±5.9 points at 95%, fielded early 2026 on calendar-2025 practice — **it says nothing
  about employers under a thousand people.**
- **Lesson 2:** The squeeze that explains the behaviour `[V]`. Serious misconduct allegations more
  than doubled since 2021; staffing moved 0.6 → 0.68 per 1,000. Unsanctioned AI use in this function
  is not indiscipline, it is arithmetic — which is why a prohibition nobody can comply with produces
  concealment rather than compliance.
- **Lesson 3:** The evidentiary ladder — the decomposition, and the spine of the whole track.
  **Observation → corroboration → inference → finding → determination.** What each rung is, in the
  words an investigator already uses. Then the permission map: AI may help at observation
  (organizing, transcribing with care — L3), at corroboration (locating what exists, what conflicts,
  what is missing), and at inference **only** as a generator of possibilities a human then tests. It
  stops absolutely between inference and finding. **The rung boundary, not the task, is the control** —
  and it is a five-position instrument where 101 M7 gave you a binary, which is the upgrade this role
  needs.
- **Lesson 4:** Intake triage, and the worst place in the curriculum for the automate-the-yes
  heuristic. Accelerating someone through a process is visible and recoverable; filtering them out is
  invisible. Here the "no" is a complaint downgraded at intake — and **that person never appears in
  your substantiation data, so the failure mode is structurally unmeasurable**, in the one function
  where the excluded person had a statutory right to be heard. Which is why 38% of organizations
  having no required investigation process `[V]` is a triage finding rather than a process-hygiene one.
- **Interactive:** sorting — twelve pieces of real LER work product onto the five rungs.
- **Activity:** *"The ladder, and what's already on it"* — your matter type's work products placed on
  the ladder, and an honest map of where AI is *already* being used in your function, by whom, in
  what tool, sanctioned or not. **Aggregate and process evidence only.** Blanks and "I don't know" are
  findings. The claim above is the prediction: committed before Lesson 1, checked at the end.

## L2 · Contested accounts

*~30 min · the signature module*

**Claim to contest:** *"A summary of three witness statements is a summary."*

- **Lesson 1:** There is no ground truth in your source material. Every other role in this
  curriculum feeds a model documents, data, or market information — material with a fact behind it.
  You feed it **two or more people telling incompatible stories, where the incompatibility is the
  evidence.**
- **Lesson 2:** Summarization harmonizes, and that is the failure. A model asked to summarize
  conflicting accounts produces a coherent narrative, because coherence is what summarization *is*.
  It will not lie to you. It will be accurate to every source and still **smooth the discrepancy that
  the investigation exists to surface.** This is a new failure mode: not the model being wrong — 101
  M6's subject — but the model being faithful and fluent and destroying the finding anyway. A
  coherent account of events that were never coherent is not a summary, it is a conclusion nobody
  reached.
- **Lesson 3:** The line, drawn. **A model may locate conflicts; only a human may resolve them.**
  Locating is factual and checkable — *A says Tuesday, B says Thursday, the calendar says Wednesday.*
  Resolving is a credibility determination. And the trap in between, which is where careful people go
  wrong: asking which account is **more consistent with the other evidence** is resolution wearing an
  analytical costume. Preponderance of the evidence requires weighing; weighing *is* the
  determination; a model that weighs has made your finding for you and you cannot say how.
- **Lesson 4:** Silence, and what a model does with it. An account that omits something is not an
  account that denies it — and models fill gaps, because filling gaps is what they are for. The
  omission is often the most important thing in a statement and it is exactly what a fluent summary
  disappears. So: never ask what an account *establishes*; ask what it **contains, omits, and
  contradicts**, and keep those three answers separate on the page.
- **Interactive:** choice — four model outputs on the same three witness statements; find the one
  that located conflicts without resolving any.
- **Activity:** *"The conflict rule"* — a written rule for your team covering what a model may be
  asked about conflicting accounts, what it may never be asked, the exact prompt patterns that stay
  on the safe side of the line, and how a conflict gets recorded so a human resolution is visible as
  a human resolution. Graded on whether the rule is operable by a colleague without you in the room.

## L3 · The interview and the record

*~30 min · counsel review required before ship*

**Claim to contest:** *"A verbatim transcript of an investigatory interview is better evidence than
your notes."*

- **Lesson 1:** What recording does to candour `[V]`. Employment counsel are consistent on this:
  recording intake interviews, witness statements, and investigative meetings may chill candour and
  discourage participation — which **undermines the promptness and effectiveness the employer's own
  defense depends on.** The tool that improves your record can degrade the investigation the record
  is of.
- **Lesson 2:** A record that would not otherwise exist `[V]`. Transcripts are discoverable, and
  transcription creates a comprehensive timestamped account where previously there were notes. Every
  offhand remark, every clumsy question your investigator asked, preserved and producible. This cuts
  genuinely both ways — it protects you against *that is not what I said* and it preserves the
  sentence you wish you had phrased differently — and the decision is a judgment, not a default.
- **Lesson 3:** Three exposures that are not about accuracy `[V]`. **Consent** — two-party consent
  states including California, Florida, and Pennsylvania require every participant to agree, and
  "the bot joined the call" is not agreement. **Biometrics** — a February 2026 class action alleges a
  major platform's live transcription captured voice biometric data without consent under Illinois
  law, which makes the transcription feature itself the exposure. And **the disparity finding, which
  is the sharpest fact in this module**: if transcription quality is systematically worse for
  speakers of particular ethnicities or linguistic backgrounds, the resulting records may themselves
  support a bias allegation. A worse transcript for a non-native-speaking respondent is a
  due-process problem and a discrimination problem at the same time.
- **Lesson 4:** Fabrication in a record you will rely on `[V]`. Notetakers misattribute and invent —
  and a hallucinated line in an investigation transcript is discoverable evidence that you produced.
  Which yields the discipline: a machine transcript is **an aid to your notes, never a substitute for
  them**, it gets reviewed against your contemporaneous record before it is filed, and an unreviewed
  transcript does not go in the file. Then the representation dimension: Weingarten rights in an
  investigatory interview that could lead to discipline, and what a recording plus a representative
  changes about the room. *Privilege is deliberately out of scope here and flagged — see Decision 9.*
- **Interactive:** sorting — ten recording and transcription scenarios: fine / fine with disclosed
  consent and review / don't.
- **Activity:** *"The records decision"* — for your matter type: what gets captured, by what tool,
  with what consent language, who reviews the machine output against contemporaneous notes before
  filing, what is retained and for how long, and the one thing you have decided **not** to record and
  why. A defensible "we don't record" is a full-credit answer.

## L4 · The report as an instrument

*~30 min · the highest-frequency output in the role, taught as a specialist would need it*

**Claim to contest:** *"Your report template was designed to record what you found, not to survive
someone attacking it."*

The HRBP track teaches a generalist to write ER documents safely. This is the specialist version,
and it is a different subject: not *how to use AI on a document* but **what the document has to do**,
and therefore what AI may touch in each part of it.

- **Lesson 1:** The report has legal work to do, and structure is where it does it. Not coherent
  headings — the **elements a finder of fact will look for**: what was alleged, what was investigated,
  what evidence was gathered and from whom, what could not be established and why, what was found,
  and on what standard. A report organized as a narrative is a report whose gaps are invisible; a
  report organized as elements makes its own gaps visible to you first, which is the entire point.
- **Lesson 2:** The completeness pass, specialized — and it is a different question than the
  generalist one. Not "what did I fail to ask?" but **"which allegation did I fail to resolve?"** The
  classic defect is a report that investigates three allegations and makes findings on two, and it is
  the defect opposing counsel finds first because it is mechanical to spot. A model is genuinely good
  at this check because it is a coverage audit against a list, not an evaluation of anyone: allegation
  in, finding out, flag the mismatches. **Safe, high-yield, and it belongs at rung two.**
- **Lesson 3:** Maintaining the rung boundary sentence by sentence. L1's ladder, applied to prose:
  *"raised his voice twice, per two witnesses"* is an observation; *"was hostile"* is an inference
  presented as one; *"intended to intimidate"* is a determination smuggled into a clause. A model is
  unusually good at flagging where the register has slipped — and this is the one place where letting
  it edit your language is safe, **because it is auditing the rung, not reaching the conclusion.**
  The rule: it may tell you a sentence has climbed a rung; it may never tell you which rung is right.
- **Lesson 4:** Provenance, and why the chain matters more than the sentence. The questions asked
  under oath are who wrote this, when, based on what, and did it change. So: contemporaneous notes
  retained separately from the report; the file's chronology intact; AI involvement recorded as a line
  in the file notes with its role and date; and the rule that governs all of it — **a model's phrasing
  must never become a finding you did not independently reach.** A document whose production you
  cannot describe is weaker than one you can, and that was true before any of this.
- **Interactive:** choice — four passages from investigation reports; find the one where a
  determination is hiding inside an observation.
- **Activity:** *"The template, rebuilt"* — your matter type's report template restructured around
  elements rather than narrative, with the allegation-to-finding coverage check written in, the rung
  discipline stated for whoever uses it next, and the provenance lines the file will carry. **Template
  only — no completed report, real or invented from a real matter.**

## L5 · What you've done before

*~30 min · the module with the best return and the least glamour*

**Claim to contest:** *"You cannot currently answer 'how have we handled this before?' without asking
someone who remembers."*

- **Lesson 1:** Disparate treatment is proven by comparators. Not by whether your decision was fair —
  by whether a similarly situated person in a different protected class was treated differently. That
  is the analysis opposing counsel will run against your case history, using discovery, with time you
  do not have. **They will run it. The question is whether you ran it first.**
- **Lesson 2:** Most teams cannot `[V]`. Only 32% of organizations track substantiation by issue
  type — the granularity that would surface a pattern in harassment, discrimination, or retaliation
  handling. A case management system full of narrative text is a filing cabinet, not a record you can
  interrogate, and this is the one place in the function where AI's core competence — retrieval and
  summarization across a corpus you own — lines up exactly with a legal exposure you already have.
- **Lesson 3:** The line. **AI surfaces the precedent; a human decides whether it governs.**
  Retrieval is assist. A recommended outcome is a determination — rung five — and it is forbidden
  however well-reasoned it looks. And the corollary that keeps this honest: **consistency is not
  uniformity.** Identical conduct with different aggravating factors properly produces different
  outcomes; what the file has to contain is *why*, written by the person who decided. An AI that
  flattens your case history into an average is manufacturing the disparate-treatment argument on the
  other side's behalf.
- **Lesson 4:** A legal comparator is not a control group. A comparator is **one similarly situated
  individual whose treatment differed**, and a single one can carry a claim. A control group is a
  population assembled to support an inference about an average effect, where one case tells you
  nothing. Borrowing statistical intuitions here is dangerous in both directions: dismissing a lone
  comparator as *n* of 1 is how employers lose, and treating your case history as a dataset to be
  averaged is how they manufacture the pattern against themselves. Same word, different instrument.
- **Lesson 5:** Where this corpus may and may not live. An investigation corpus is the worst-case
  material for a general enterprise assistant — comp files, disciplinary records, accommodation
  documentation, legal-hold threads — and an assistant inherits the querying user's access, which is
  wider than anyone intended. This module wants that corpus searchable anyway, so the scoping rule is
  the content: **scoped to the ER team's own access, never joined to the enterprise assistant, and its
  existence is itself a discovery consideration** you raise with counsel before you build it.
- **Interactive:** choice — four comparator analyses; find the one that produced a determination while
  claiming to produce a precedent.
- **Activity:** *"The query you can't run"* — the single comparator question you most need answered
  about your matter type, written precisely; what your current system would return today; what would
  have to be true of your records for it to return an answer; and the scoping rule for where such a
  corpus may live. **Submit the query design and the aggregate gap, never the cases.**

## L6 · The counterparty, organized

*~25 min*

**Claim to contest:** *"An AI-drafted grievance tells you something about the merits."*

- **Lesson 1:** What arrived `[V]`. Employees now routinely use general-purpose AI to draft
  grievances, appeal discipline, challenge selection for redundancy, and prepare claims. A grievance
  that was two pages is commonly 8–12. The register is formal, the statutory references are present,
  and the drafting is better than the drafter's own — while the grasp of the law, and sometimes of the
  facts, may be thin. Minor incidents get restated in the vocabulary of bullying, harassment, and
  discrimination, because that is the vocabulary the model knows the situation calls for.
- **Lesson 2:** You judge the content, not the tool. This is the rule and it is not a courtesy.
  Downgrading, delaying, or refusing a complaint because it reads as AI-generated is itself
  actionable, and it destroys the prompt-and-appropriate-response posture your defense depends on.
  *Jurisdictional note stated in-lesson:* the clearest authority is UK — the Acas Code and
  victimisation under the Equality Act 2010 — and the US analogue is retaliation exposure plus loss of
  the affirmative defense. The rule is the same in both; the citation is not, and we say so.
- **Lesson 3:** The volume trap, running the other way. Elsewhere in this curriculum, cheaper
  production means *your* function makes more artifacts. Here, **production got cheaper for the other
  side and your obligation per matter did not change** — which is the mechanism behind L1's
  arithmetic. The operational response is not to read faster: it is to separate, on intake,
  **allegations from argument** — the 8–12 pages typically contain a small number of factual
  allegations wrapped in a large amount of characterization, and only the allegations generate an
  investigative obligation. Extracting the allegation list is a legitimate rung-two use; deciding
  whether an allegation is serious is not.
- **Lesson 4:** The other counterparty. A union's right to information is a statutory entitlement,
  not a negotiation, and it now includes questions about the systems you have deployed. Unions are
  using AI for contract analysis, costing, and comparator research across employers, which means **the
  asymmetry that used to favour the employer's data access is closing.**
- **Interactive:** choice — four intake responses to an AI-drafted grievance; find the one that judged
  the tool instead of the content.
- **Activity:** *"Allegations, separated"* — a written intake procedure for a long AI-drafted
  submission: how allegations get extracted from characterization, what a model may and may not do in
  that extraction, who confirms the list, and how the complainant is told what is being investigated.
  Written generically. **No submitted grievance, real or paraphrased.**

## L7 · Three layers of law

*~40 min · the floor · the heaviest module in the track · counsel review required before ship*

**Claim to contest:** *"You can name, for every labor authority you rely on, whether it is a statute,
a Board decision, or a General Counsel memorandum — and when it last changed."*

Most legal training in this field teaches the rules. The rules here move at three different speeds,
and **advice built on the wrong layer expires.** So this module teaches the stack before the content.

- **Lesson 1:** The three layers `[V]`. **Statute — stable.** Section 7's protection of concerted
  activity, the duty to bargain, Weingarten. Decades old, unchanged by any of this. **Board doctrine —
  flips with administrations.** In *Endurance Environmental Solutions* (10 December 2024) the Board
  reversed the 2019 *MV Transportation* "contract coverage" test and restored **clear and unmistakable
  waiver**, retroactively — making a general management-rights clause much weaker cover for a
  unilateral change. **General Counsel priorities — fastest of all.** **GC 23-02**, on electronic
  monitoring and algorithmic management interfering with Section 7 rights, was **rescinded on 14
  February 2025** in GC 25-05, among 29 memoranda withdrawn, with case backlog cited. A practitioner
  who memorised GC 23-02 in 2024 is wrong today. One who concludes from the rescission that the
  exposure is gone is also wrong, **because the statute never moved** — a rescinded memorandum
  withdraws a prosecutorial theory, not a right.
- **Lesson 2:** And the layer that just moved under all of it `[V]`. The Board lost quorum for nearly
  a year and regained it on **7 January 2026**, when Scott Mayer and James Murphy — confirmed 18
  December 2025 — were sworn in alongside General Counsel Crystal Carey, leaving David Prouty as the
  sole remaining member of the previous majority. Two consequences you can act on: the composition
  that decided *Endurance* no longer holds, so **the waiver standard is in play and should be
  re-checked before you rely on it**; and matters implemented during the quorum gap are now reachable
  by a Board working through its backlog. There was no forum for a year. There is one now.
- **Lesson 3:** The duty to bargain over AI — settled doctrine, unsettled application `[V]`, and the
  distinction is the lesson. **Settled:** a change to wages, hours, or terms and conditions of
  employment is a mandatory subject, and a mandatory subject cannot be changed unilaterally without
  notice to the union and a genuine opportunity to bargain. AI that monitors, scores, allocates, or
  disciplines plainly touches terms and conditions. **Not settled:** there is **no Board decision
  applying that to AI.** SAG-AFTRA filed a charge against Llama Productions in May 2025 arguing a duty
  to bargain before replacing workers with AI, and the Board has yet to opine on AI-induced layoffs.
  Secondary sources state the conclusion as if it were law. It is a strong inference from settled
  doctrine with a pending charge and no holding — and *unverifiable is not the same as false* applies
  to law exactly as it applies to a vendor claim. Under clear-and-unmistakable waiver your existing
  management-rights clause probably does not cover it; the practical answer is to bargain it or to
  negotiate explicit language.
- **Lesson 4:** What unions have actually won, and the convergence `[V]`. CWA's contract at Microsoft
  subsidiary ZeniMax requires notice to the union when AI implementation affects bargaining-unit work,
  and became a template reused elsewhere in tech. NewsGuild-CWA holds roughly 85–90 agreements with AI
  language, 58 of them ratified newsroom contracts. **The UPS Teamsters contract prohibits using
  sensor data as the sole basis for discipline and requires human review of context before
  AI-generated performance data is acted on** — which is 101 M7's assist/decide line, written into a
  collective bargaining agreement by the other side of the table. About 38% of union members report at
  least one provision on automated management or surveillance, most commonly notice. **Notice, human
  review, and records are also the duties the AI employment statutes keep converging on** — arriving
  here by contract rather than by legislation, which means in a represented workplace you may owe them
  years before a regulator asks.
- **Lesson 5:** Section 7 without a union, which is why this module is not conditional. Protected
  concerted activity does not require representation. AI monitoring that chills employees discussing
  pay, hours, or conditions is exposure in a non-union workplace on the same statutory basis — and the
  rescission of GC 23-02 removed the theory, not the section. Then: what you can do this quarter
  without waiting for counsel, and the specific list to bring them so the answer is not a shrug.
- **Interactive:** sorting — ten propositions about AI and labor law: statute / Board doctrine /
  General Counsel priority / not established at all.
- **Activity:** *"The authority map, dated"* — every legal proposition your matter type relies on,
  each tagged by layer, with the date it last changed and the date you last checked, plus the two you
  discovered you could not source. Predict, before Lesson 1, how many you would have mislabelled.

## L8 · Findings that hold

*~20 min · the course lands*

**Claim to contest:** *"Your function's product is closed cases."*

- **Lesson 1:** The product is a finding that holds. Cycle time and closure rate are the metrics this
  function is measured on and neither is the product — a case closed fast on a record that collapses
  under challenge is a liability that has not been recognized yet. What holding looks like: the rung
  boundaries visible in the file, the conflicts recorded as conflicts, the human resolution
  attributable to a human, every allegation resolved, and the comparator question answered before the
  decision rather than after the complaint.
- **Lesson 2:** What to measure instead `[V]`, and this function has an unusual advantage — the
  benchmark exists. Substantiation by issue type, which only 32% track. Repeat-respondent detection.
  Whether a required process exists at all, which 38% cannot claim. Time-to-first-contact rather than
  time-to-close. And the one nobody measures: **what share of your matters could withstand the
  comparator analysis opposing counsel would run.**
- **Lesson 3:** The honest capacity conversation. Allegations doubled, staffing flat — so AI here is
  not a productivity story, it is a triage story, and the question is which rung you spend the
  recovered minutes on. The answer this track argues for is **rung two**: corroboration, the
  unglamorous work of establishing what exists, what conflicts, and what is missing, which is where
  cases are won and lost and where AI is genuinely permitted.
- **Lesson 4:** The bet, with a baseline and a kill condition. One change to your matter type, what
  you would measure, what you would stop doing to fund it, and **what you would turn off** — including
  the possibility that the right answer is a tool your team is already using without sanction.
- **Interactive:** choice — four proposed changes; find the one that improves cycle time and weakens
  the record.
- **Activity (course close):** *"One change, one measure, one condition"* — one page: the change, the
  baseline from your own aggregate data, the measure at 90 days, the funding line, and the written
  condition under which you would reverse it. Then **the delta**: every claim you contested in L1–L7,
  which turned out to be true of your function, and what changed. Graded on the account of the change
  and the evidence behind it, never on which way you guessed.

## Prerequisite map

- 101 and 201 assumed. **No sibling-track prerequisite** (Decision 2).
- L1 first, always — the ladder is the vocabulary every later module uses, and L4 applies it to prose.
- L2 before L3 (the record only matters once you know what may be asked of it) and before L4 (the
  report is where locate-versus-resolve becomes visible on the page).
- L3 before L4 in practice order — you gather before you write — though either order works for a
  learner who does not run interviews personally.
- L5 after L1 (a comparator analysis is a rung-two use and needs the boundary first).
- L6 after L1 (its volume argument depends on L1's arithmetic).
- L7 open order after L1, but before any AI deployment affecting a represented population.
- L8 last — it carries the delta reckoning.

## Decisions (v2)

1. **Async and self-serve.** No invented surfaces. Positions committed in the activity, argued by the
   tutor.
2. **No sibling-track prerequisite, and the craft is taught here — reversing v1.** v1 made this the
   first track to require a module from another 301 track, on the reasoning that HRBP M6 already owns
   the ER documentation craft and re-teaching it would violate the subtraction discipline. **That was
   the wrong reading of the discipline.** `trackForRole` resolves a learner to exactly one 301 course
   id, so an LER learner will never open the HRBP track — meaning the horizontal overlap costs the
   *learner* nothing and a cross-track prerequisite would send them into another role's course for one
   lesson, which is worse for them than a well-aimed duplicate. The subtraction against 101 and 201 is
   unchanged and absolute; the subtraction against siblings is a maintenance concern, not a pedagogical
   one. Written up as brief §3a, which now governs every remaining track.
3. **Which produced L4, and it earns its place rather than filling a gap.** Report-writing is the
   highest-frequency output in this role and the generalist track gives it one lesson. The specialist
   version is a different subject — not *how to use AI on a document* but **what the document has to
   do**, and therefore what AI may touch in each part: elements rather than narrative, the
   allegation-to-finding coverage check rather than "what did I fail to ask?", and the rung boundary
   maintained sentence by sentence, which is L1's ladder applied to prose. That last one is the only
   place in the track where a model may edit the learner's language, **because it audits the rung
   rather than reaching the conclusion.**
4. **The signature module still comes from reading a sibling track rather than from exploring the
   role.** HRBP M6's knowledge check marks "flagging inconsistencies between witness accounts" as
   approaching credibility and declines to resolve it. L2 resolves it: **a model may locate conflicts;
   only a human may resolve them**, with the trap in the middle being the request for which account is
   more consistent with the other evidence. Worth recording as a method: **read the shipped siblings
   for the lines they decline to draw**, which is a different exercise from subtracting their content
   and is apparently more productive.
5. **The spine is a matter type, not a matter, and no activity may accept case facts.** Every other
   track runs on a live artifact — a requisition, a cycle, a capability, a register, a question. The
   equivalent here would be a live investigation, and case content must never reach an AI-graded
   submission, an operator review queue, or the tutor. So the spine sits at process level and **the
   rubrics carry it as a rejection condition, not a caution.** Clearest instance in the set of a role
   property dictating product mechanics, and it needs building into the rubric schema rather than into
   prose.
6. **It interacts with the assessment convention, and the interaction is stated in-content.**
   Activities demand evidence from the learner's own organization rather than self-report. Here that
   means process and aggregate evidence only — substantiation rates, cycle times, what the case system
   can query. Both rules hold; a learner left to reconcile them alone will reconcile them wrongly, so
   L1 says it explicitly before the first activity.
7. **Eight modules, 20–40 minutes, ~4h.** One more than v1, because Decision 3 added a module rather
   than a fragment. Matches the People Analytics track's runtime. L7 at ~40 min is the heaviest — three
   layers of authority plus the bargaining duty plus contract language plus Section 7 — and ships as
   one, consistent with the other legal modules, with completion watched.
8. **The decomposition is an evidentiary ladder, not a quadrant.** Observation → corroboration →
   inference → finding → determination. Discovered from the role — it is how investigators are trained
   to write — and it does work no quadrant would: **AI's permission changes at every rung and stops at
   a specific boundary**, turning 101 M7's binary into a five-position control. L4 then applies it to
   prose, which is what makes it a working instrument rather than a diagram.
9. **Verified before v1 was written, and it changed three things.** Confirmed: HR Acuity's *Tenth
   Annual Employee Relations Benchmark Study* with full methodology (Isurus Market Research, fielded
   23 January – 24 March 2026 on calendar-2025 practice, 274 US organizations of 1,000+ employees
   representing 8.8 million employees, ±5.9 points at 95%) and its findings — decade-high volumes,
   serious allegations more than doubled since 2021, staffing 0.6 → 0.68 per 1,000, 70%
   experimented-or-deployed, 56% restricting AI to approved tools for confidential case content, 38%
   with no required process, 32% tracking substantiation by issue type; the rescission of GC 23-02 on
   14 February 2025 via GC 25-05; *Endurance Environmental Solutions* (10 December 2024) restoring
   clear-and-unmistakable waiver retroactively over *MV Transportation*; the Board's quorum loss and
   restoration on 7 January 2026 with Mayer, Murphy, and General Counsel Carey; the CWA/ZeniMax,
   NewsGuild-CWA, and UPS Teamsters provisions; the AI-notetaker exposures including the February 2026
   biometric class action and the transcription-disparity finding; and the AI-drafted grievance pattern
   with the judge-the-content rule.

   **The three changes.** *(a)* The opening is the collision between the practice-area finding and the
   ER-specific one — two sound studies measuring different things, whose gap is unsanctioned use. *(b)*
   I was going to write that unilateral AI implementation is an unfair labor practice. **There is no
   holding** — SAG-AFTRA's May 2025 charge is pending and the Board has not opined on AI-induced
   layoffs — so L7 teaches settled doctrine with unsettled application. *(c)* The three-layer structure
   replaced a flat list of authorities, because they visibly move at different speeds and one of them
   moved during the research.
10. **Unverified and blocking before drafting.** **Privilege architecture** for AI-assisted
    investigations — dual-purpose problems, waiver risk from third-party tools, who the client is.
    Important, unverified, and therefore deliberately scoped out of L3 with a note rather than
    half-taught; strongest candidate for expansion once verified, and it would most naturally join L4.
    **Weingarten's application to recorded or AI-assisted interviews**, where I found no authority.
    **Any published labor arbitration award** addressing AI-assisted work product or fabricated
    citations. And **the waiver standard's status under the Board seated 7 January 2026** — the single
    most likely thing here to have changed by the time L7 is drafted, and it should be re-checked first.
11. **Two vendor-sourced instruments, both labeled.** HR Acuity is vendor-sponsored but independently
    fielded with a disclosed sample and margin of error, which is what makes it usable where the
    People Ops track's data anchor was not. The AI-drafted-grievance evidence is largely UK employment
    counsel, stated as such, with the US analogue named. The "83% of HR directors" figure circulating
    with that material has no published methodology and is not used.
12. **Counsel review required for L3 and L7.** Second track needing two gates. L7's is the heavier and
    the content states it.
13. **What drafting proved wrong, recorded after all eight modules shipped.** Seven items.

    *(a)* **Decision 10 was right about which fact would move, and it moved further than expected.**
    The waiver standard's status under the Board seated 7 January 2026 was flagged as the single item
    most likely to have changed by the time L7 was drafted. It changed twice. In January 2026 the new
    two-member majority declined to overturn a Biden-era precedent in *Lodi Volunteer Ambulance Rescue
    Squad* under the Board's unwritten three-vote convention — which read as stability for six months.
    Then on **7 August 2026**, five days before L7 was drafted, the Senate confirmed James Macy and
    David Prouty, producing a **3–1 majority that satisfies the three-vote condition.** The module says
    so in its own text: its subject moved during its production, and concealing that would have taught
    the wrong lesson.

    *(b)* **L7 has five layers, not three.** The outline's three — statute, Board doctrine, General
    Counsel priority — are the ones that differ by *speed*. Drafting surfaced two more that practitioners
    routinely mistake for law: **rulemaking**, which runs on a different clock and is undone by different
    mechanisms, and **contract**, which binds regardless of who runs the Board. The contract layer had
    to be a layer rather than a lesson, because L7's own Lesson 4 is entirely about obligations that
    arrive by agreement, and the sorting exercise cannot test layer confusion without a bucket for the
    kind of confusion practitioners actually have. The module title survived; its content is five.

    *(c)* **The best fact in L7 was in the case's facts, not its holding.** *Endurance Environmental
    Solutions* was outlined as a doctrine example. Drafting found that the employer had installed video
    cameras in a fleet of roughly **400 trucks** and defended on a management-rights clause reserving
    the right to *"implement changes in equipment"* — and lost. That single phrase is the module's
    strongest teaching object, because every learner's clause contains language of about that
    generality, and it converts an abstract standard into an instruction: go and read your own clause.

    *(d)* **L7's sorting exercise needed five buckets and a durability axis.** The outline specified
    four (statute / doctrine / GC priority / not established). With the contract layer added the count
    is five, and the buckets had to be *ordered* — the schema uses rank to distinguish over- from
    under-assignment. Ordering them by **durability** makes over-assignment mean *treated as more
    settled than it is*, which is the module's expensive direction and matches L1's ladder, where
    over-assignment is also the expensive direction.

    *(e)* **L6's counterparty exercise found a better wrong answer than the one outlined.** The plan was
    a team that visibly judged the tool — a note saying the submission looked AI-generated. Drafting
    produced something worse and more likely: a team whose triage note reads *"the statutory
    characterisations do not appear to reflect independent legal advice"*, which believes it assessed
    merit, and which defends itself with *"the file says nothing about AI."* **A provenance judgment
    does not stop being one because the word is left out.** That became a line in the module.

    *(f)* **L6's *Take a position* counter-argument has an inequity limb the outline did not have.** The
    resource objection was planned. Drafting surfaced the sharper version: under a strictly-equal-
    attention rule, **the employee who wrote their own two pages in plain language gets the same
    investigative resource as the person who generated forty** — and the plain-language complainant is
    more likely to be the one without access to tools, advice, or the confidence to escalate. A rule
    designed to prevent discrimination against AI users may disadvantage the people the process exists
    to protect. It is the strongest counter in the track.

    *(g)* **L8's choice exercise inverted.** The outline said *find the one that improves cycle time and
    weakens the record*, and assumed that one would be the forbidden option. It is not. The forbidden
    option — AI triage at intake — is easy to spot once L1 has landed. The genuinely hard answer is
    **AI-drafted investigation reports**, where every number in the pilot is good, nothing is
    prohibited, a human still reaches the finding, and the record degrades in three ways no pilot metric
    can see. The exercise is stronger for it, and it makes L4's *polish is not strength* argument land
    as a measurement problem rather than a stylistic preference.

## Open questions for review

- **Does labor relations eventually want its own track?** L7 and half of L6 are the labor half. In
  heavily unionized industries — manufacturing, transport, healthcare systems, public sector — labor
  relations is a distinct function with its own craft, and a track that gives it one module plus half
  of another may under-serve it. Resolved for now by making Section 7 the non-conditional spine so the
  floor works for everyone; revisit if the audience skews unionized.
- **Where else does §3a reverse a call already made?** This outline's v1 is the clearest case, but the
  People Ops outline cut two things partly on horizontal grounds — a statistic because a sibling track
  anchors on it, and a privilege topic because a sibling covers it — and both cuts happen to survive on
  merit. Worth one pass over the three outlined tracks to check nothing else was withheld from a
  learner who will never see the alternative.
- **The shared evidence library is now carrying real weight rather than tidy.** §3a permits duplication and
  names drift as its real cost. Three facts already appear in three or more tracks — the coaching
  trials, *Mobley*, and the SHRM adoption figures — and with duplication sanctioned that number will
  grow fast. Authoring the HR-wide volatile facts once, referenced from each track, is what keeps the
  permission from producing six diverging copies.
- **Should works-council consultation move here from People Ops M7?** It sits there as a deployment
  dependency, which is right for a systems owner; in Europe it is core LR work. Under §3a the answer
  may simply be *both*, specialized differently — deployment consultation there, bargaining obligation
  here — which is a cleaner resolution than the boundary-drawing v1 attempted.
- **The comparator/control-group boundary with `ai301-analytics`.** L5 Lesson 4 draws it, and both
  tracks are better for it — but the People Analytics track was outlined in parallel and does not yet
  carry the reciprocal note. Worth one line there, since a learner who takes both will otherwise
  import statistical intuitions into a legal analysis where a single instance is dispositive.
- **The case-history index.** L5 teaches the scoping rule for a searchable investigation corpus.
  Whether we also ship a reference architecture is a product decision — and it is exactly the corpus a
  general enterprise assistant should never index, so L5 and People Ops M6 must be read together by
  whoever builds it.
- **The rejection condition needs schema support.** Decision 5 requires rubrics to return submissions
  containing identifiable case content. That is a rubric-schema capability, not prose, and it is the
  one thing in this outline that cannot be delivered by writing content alone.
