# AI 301 · The Specialist — Labor & Employee Relations track · "Under Challenge" (draft v1)

**Audience:** ER specialists and investigators, ER centres of excellence, labor relations managers,
contract administrators, and anyone who runs workplace investigations, grievances, discipline
consistency, or the bargaining relationship — including the many people who do both halves because
the company has one of them.
**Level transition:** L3 The Specialist → L4 The Translator.
**Course id:** `ai301-ler` · role id `ler`, label "Labor & Employee Relations".
**Shape:** 7 modules · 25–40 min each · ~3h35 · same package as the rest of the ladder — read, micro
dose, tutor, podcast, one interactive, one AI-graded activity, one knowledge check. Async and
self-serve.
**Prerequisite:** AI 101 (or the diagnostic test-out) and AI 201. **This track additionally assumes
`ai301-hrbp` M6** — the ER documentation craft, the completeness pass, the stop line, and the
provenance discipline are taught there and are not repeated here. Learners who skipped it get a
pointer, not a re-teach.
**Tooling stance:** Tool-agnostic. Every statute, Board decision, General Counsel memorandum,
contract provision, and survey figure is volatile-layer `[V]` — and this track's authorities move
faster than any other's, which is itself Module 6's subject.

Exploration and full verification log: `content/course-301-ler-exploration.md`.

## The through-line

> **Everyone else's AI output is judged on whether it's good. Yours is judged by someone whose job
> is to break it, under a standard of proof, years after you wrote it.**

Earned, not asserted, from two studies that disagree `[V]`. HRBP M2 teaches — correctly, from SHRM —
that AI is **least** adopted in employee relations of any practice area in HR. HR Acuity's tenth
annual benchmark finds that **70% of ER teams experimented with or actively deployed AI for employee
relations and investigations in 2025**, and that only 56% restrict AI to approved, secured tools for
confidential case content. Both are sound; they measure different things. Institutionally this is the
least-AI part of HR. Behaviourally it is one of the most exposed, and **the gap between those two
numbers is unsanctioned use in the highest-stakes documents the company produces.**

## Where the learner stands (design premise)

They do not run a workflow. They run a process that is designed to be attacked, and they are
underwater: case volumes and serious misconduct claims at or near decade highs, **serious
allegations more than doubled since 2021**, and staffing essentially flat at 0.68 professionals per
1,000 employees `[V]`. The grievances arriving are three times longer than they were and read like
they were drafted by counsel, because in a sense they were. They suspect somebody on their team is
pasting case content into a consumer chatbot, and they are probably right.

> "My files get read under oath by people trying to break them. Tell me what AI can touch without
> handing the other side an argument — and don't tell me to be careful, tell me where the line is."

Three things make this track structurally different from the four above it. **The AI failure that
matters here is fluency, not error** — a model can be accurate to every source it was given and still
destroy a finding, by producing a coherent account of events that were never coherent. **The spine
cannot be a live case**, because case facts must never be submitted anywhere, which forces the whole
track up a level to process. And **the sharpest content is a line a shipped module deliberately
declined to draw**: HRBP M6 flags comparing witness accounts as "approaching credibility" and stops
there. For a specialist, drawing that line is the job.

## The spine: one matter type, at the level of process

Every module advances **one matter type the learner owns end to end** — a harassment intake process, a
discipline consistency standard, a grievance step, a bargaining subject. Not a case. Never a case.

| Module | What it does to the matter type |
|---|---|
| L1 | Place its work on the evidentiary ladder; find where AI already sits |
| L2 | Write the rule for what a model may do with conflicting accounts |
| L3 | Decide the record: what is captured, by what, with whose consent |
| L4 | Design the comparator query you cannot currently run |
| L5 | Prepare for a counterparty who arrives drafted |
| L6 | Locate every authority you rely on by layer, and date it |
| L7 | Commit to a change, a measure, and a kill condition |

**No activity in this track may accept case facts.** Not the AI-graded submission, not the operator
review queue, not the tutor. Rubrics carry it as a rejection condition rather than a caution — a
submission containing identifiable case content is returned, and the content says so before the first
activity. Artifacts are process artifacts: a triage rubric, a conflict-handling rule, a records
decision, a query design, a bargaining position, a dated authority map.

**Every module opens with a claim the learner must contest**, per the convention adopted across the
ladder — a factual assertion about their own function, committed to before any content and checked
against evidence during the module. For this track, **evidence means process and aggregate evidence**:
substantiation rates, cycle times, whether a required process exists, what the case management system
can actually query. Never a case. L7 reads the pattern; the rubric grades evidence of updating.

## L1 · The gap and the ladder

*~35 min · the diagnosis and the decomposition, together, because the second explains the first*

**Claim to contest:** *"Nobody on your team is putting case content into an unapproved tool."*

- **Lesson 1:** The two studies that disagree `[V]`, and why both are right. SHRM's practice-area
  view puts employee relations at the bottom of AI adoption in HR; HR Acuity's ER-specific benchmark
  puts 70% of ER teams at experimented-or-deployed in 2025, with 56% restricting AI to approved
  secured tools for confidential case content. Institutional adoption versus practitioner behaviour.
  The sample, stated because this module is about evidence: 274 US organizations of 1,000+ employees,
  8.8 million employees, ±5.9 points at 95%, fielded early 2026 on calendar-2025 practice — **it says
  nothing about employers under a thousand people.**
- **Lesson 2:** The squeeze that explains the behaviour `[V]`. Serious misconduct allegations more
  than doubled since 2021; staffing moved 0.6 → 0.68 per 1,000. Unsanctioned AI use in this function
  is not indiscipline, it is arithmetic — which is why a prohibition nobody can comply with produces
  concealment rather than compliance.
- **Lesson 3:** The evidentiary ladder — the decomposition, and the spine of the whole track.
  **Observation → corroboration → inference → finding → determination.** What each rung is, in the
  words an investigator already uses. Then the permission map: AI may help at observation
  (organizing, transcribing with care — L3), at corroboration (locating what exists, what conflicts,
  what is missing), and at inference **only** as a generator of possibilities a human then tests. It
  stops absolutely between inference and finding. **The rung boundary, not the task, is the control.**
- **Lesson 4:** Intake triage, and the worst place in the curriculum for HRBP M3's heuristic.
  Automating the "yes" is comparatively safe; automating the "no" is where risk starts — and here the
  "no" is a complaint that was downgraded at intake. **That person never appears in your
  substantiation data, so the failure mode is structurally unmeasurable**, exactly as HRBP M3
  predicts, in the one function where the excluded person had a statutory right to be heard. Which is
  why 38% of organizations having no required investigation process `[V]` is a triage finding rather
  than a process-hygiene finding.
- **Interactive:** sorting — twelve pieces of real LER work product onto the five rungs.
- **Activity:** *"The ladder, and what's already on it"* — your matter type's work products placed on
  the ladder, and an honest map of where AI is *already* being used in your function, by whom, in
  what tool, sanctioned or not. **Aggregate and process evidence only.** Blanks and "I don't know" are
  findings. **Prediction is the claim above**: committed before Lesson 1, checked at the end.

## L2 · Contested accounts

*~30 min · the signature module · the line a shipped module declined to draw*

**Claim to contest:** *"A summary of three witness statements is a summary."*

- **Lesson 1:** There is no ground truth in your source material. Every other role in this
  curriculum feeds a model documents, data, or market information — material with a fact behind it.
  You feed it **two or more people telling incompatible stories, where the incompatibility is the
  evidence.**
- **Lesson 2:** Summarization harmonizes, and that is the failure. A model asked to summarize
  conflicting accounts produces a coherent narrative, because coherence is what summarization *is*.
  It will not lie to you. It will be accurate to every source and still **smooth the discrepancy that
  the investigation exists to surface.** This is a new failure mode: not the model being wrong — 101
  M6's subject — but the model being faithful and fluent and destroying the finding anyway. A
  coherent account of events that were never coherent is not a summary, it is a conclusion nobody
  reached.
- **Lesson 3:** The line, drawn. HRBP M6 marks "flagging inconsistencies between witness accounts" as
  approaching credibility and stops. Here is the resolution: **a model may locate conflicts; only a
  human may resolve them.** Locating is factual and checkable — *A says Tuesday, B says Thursday, the
  calendar says Wednesday.* Resolving is a credibility determination. And the trap in between, which
  is where careful people go wrong: asking which account is **more consistent with the other
  evidence** is resolution wearing an analytical costume. Preponderance of the evidence requires
  weighing; weighing *is* the determination; a model that weighs has made your finding for you and
  you cannot say how.
- **Lesson 4:** Silence, and what a model does with it. An account that omits something is not an
  account that denies it — and models fill gaps, because filling gaps is what they are for. The
  omission is often the most important thing in a statement and it is exactly what a fluent summary
  disappears. So: never ask what an account *establishes*; ask what it **contains, omits, and
  contradicts**, and keep those three answers separate on the page.
- **Interactive:** choice — four model outputs on the same three witness statements; find the one
  that located conflicts without resolving any.
- **Activity:** *"The conflict rule"* — a written rule for your team covering what a model may be
  asked about conflicting accounts, what it may never be asked, the exact prompt patterns that stay
  on the safe side of the line, and how a conflict gets recorded so a human resolution is visible as
  a human resolution. Graded on whether the rule is operable by a colleague without you in the room.

## L3 · The interview and the record

*~30 min · counsel review required before ship*

**Claim to contest:** *"A verbatim transcript of an investigatory interview is better evidence than
your notes."*

- **Lesson 1:** What recording does to candour `[V]`. Employment counsel are consistent on this:
  recording intake interviews, witness statements, and investigative meetings may chill candour and
  discourage participation — which **undermines the promptness and effectiveness the employer's own
  defense depends on.** The tool that improves your record can degrade the investigation the record
  is of.
- **Lesson 2:** A record that would not otherwise exist `[V]`. Transcripts are discoverable, and
  transcription creates a comprehensive timestamped account where previously there were notes. Every
  offhand remark, every clumsy question your investigator asked, every joke, preserved and
  producible. This cuts genuinely both ways — it protects you against *that is not what I said* and it
  preserves the sentence you wish you had phrased differently — and the decision is a judgment, not a
  default.
- **Lesson 3:** Three exposures that are not about accuracy `[V]`. **Consent** — two-party consent
  states including California, Florida, and Pennsylvania require every participant to agree, and
  "the bot joined the call" is not agreement. **Biometrics** — a February 2026 class action alleges a
  major platform's live transcription captured voice biometric data without consent under Illinois
  law, which makes the transcription feature itself the exposure. And **the disparity finding, which
  is the sharpest fact in this module**: if transcription quality is systematically worse for
  speakers of particular ethnicities or linguistic backgrounds, the resulting records may themselves
  support a bias allegation. A worse transcript for a non-native-speaking respondent is a
  due-process problem and a discrimination problem at the same time.
- **Lesson 4:** Fabrication in a record you will rely on `[V]`. Notetakers misattribute and invent —
  and a hallucinated line in an investigation transcript is discoverable evidence that you produced.
  Which yields the discipline: a machine transcript is **an aid to your notes, never a substitute for
  them**, it gets reviewed against your contemporaneous record before it is filed, and an unreviewed
  transcript does not go in the file. Then the representation dimension: Weingarten rights in an
  investigatory interview that could lead to discipline, and what a recording plus a representative
  changes about the room. *Privilege is deliberately out of scope here and flagged — see Decision 8.*
- **Interactive:** sorting — ten recording and transcription scenarios: fine / fine with disclosed
  consent and review / don't.
- **Activity:** *"The records decision"* — for your matter type: what gets captured, by what tool,
  with what consent language, who reviews the machine output against contemporaneous notes before
  filing, what is retained and for how long, and the one thing you have decided **not** to record and
  why. A defensible "we don't record" is a full-credit answer.

## L4 · What you've done before

*~30 min · the module with the best return and the least glamour*

**Claim to contest:** *"You cannot currently answer 'how have we handled this before?' without asking
someone who remembers."*

- **Lesson 1:** Disparate treatment is proven by comparators. Not by whether your decision was fair —
  by whether a similarly situated person in a different protected class was treated differently. That
  is the analysis opposing counsel will run against your case history, using discovery, with time you
  do not have. **They will run it. The question is whether you ran it first.**
- **Lesson 2:** Most teams cannot `[V]`. Only 32% of organizations track substantiation by issue
  type — the granularity that would surface a pattern in harassment, discrimination, or retaliation
  handling. A case management system full of narrative text is a filing cabinet, not a record you can
  interrogate, and this is the one place in the function where AI's core competence — retrieval and
  summarization across a corpus you own — lines up exactly with a legal exposure you already have.
- **Lesson 3:** The line, which is People Ops M5's drawn over people instead of data. **AI surfaces
  the precedent; a human decides whether it governs.** Retrieval is assist. A recommended outcome is
  a determination — rung five — and it is forbidden however well-reasoned it looks. And the corollary
  that keeps this honest: **consistency is not uniformity.** Identical conduct with different
  aggravating factors properly produces different outcomes; what the file has to contain is *why*,
  written by the person who decided. An AI that flattens your case history into an average is
  manufacturing the disparate-treatment argument on the other side's behalf.
- **Lesson 4:** A legal comparator is not a control group — stated because `ai301-analytics` M6 now
  teaches natural experiments and the vocabularies collide. A comparator is **one similarly situated
  individual whose treatment differed**, and a single one can carry a disparate-treatment claim. A
  control group is a population assembled to support an inference about an average effect, and one
  case tells you nothing. Borrowing statistical intuitions here is dangerous in both directions:
  dismissing a lone comparator as *n* of 1 is how employers lose, and treating your case history as a
  dataset to be averaged is how they manufacture the pattern against themselves. Same word, different
  instrument.
- **Lesson 5:** Where this corpus may and may not live. People Ops M6 says never index investigation
  files into a general enterprise assistant, and it is right. This module wants exactly that corpus
  searchable — so the scoping rule is the content: **scoped to the ER team's own access, never joined
  to the enterprise assistant, and its existence is itself a discovery consideration** you should
  raise with counsel before you build it, not after.
- **Interactive:** choice — four comparator analyses; find the one that produced a determination while
  claiming to produce a precedent.
- **Activity:** *"The query you can't run"* — the single comparator question you most need answered
  about your matter type, written precisely; what your current system would return today; what would
  have to be true of your records for it to return an answer; and the scoping rule for where such a
  corpus may live. **Submit the query design and the aggregate gap, never the cases.**

## L5 · The counterparty, organized

*~25 min*

**Claim to contest:** *"An AI-drafted grievance tells you something about the merits."*

- **Lesson 1:** What arrived `[V]`. Employees now routinely use general-purpose AI to draft
  grievances, appeal discipline, challenge selection for redundancy, and prepare claims. A grievance
  that was two pages is commonly 8–12. The register is formal, the statutory references are present,
  and the drafting is better than the drafter's own — while the grasp of the law, and sometimes of the
  facts, may be thin. Minor incidents get restated in the vocabulary of bullying, harassment, and
  discrimination, because that is the vocabulary the model knows the situation calls for.
- **Lesson 2:** You judge the content, not the tool. This is the rule and it is not a courtesy.
  Downgrading, delaying, or refusing a complaint because it reads as AI-generated is itself
  actionable, and it destroys the prompt-and-appropriate-response posture your defense depends on.
  *Jurisdictional note stated in-lesson:* the clearest authority here is UK — the Acas Code and
  victimisation under the Equality Act 2010 — and the US analogue is retaliation exposure plus the loss
  of the affirmative defense. The rule is the same in both; the citation is not, and we say so.
- **Lesson 3:** HRBP M3's volume trap, running the other way. There, cheaper production means *your*
  function makes more artifacts. Here, **production got cheaper for the other side and your obligation
  per matter did not change** — which is the mechanism behind L1's arithmetic. The operational
  response is not to read faster: it is to separate, on intake, **allegations from argument** — the
  8–12 pages typically contain a small number of factual allegations wrapped in a large amount of
  characterization, and only the allegations generate an investigative obligation. Extracting the
  allegation list is a legitimate rung-two use; deciding whether an allegation is serious is not.
- **Lesson 4:** The other counterparty. A union's right to information is a statutory entitlement, not
  a negotiation, and it now includes questions about the systems you have deployed. Unions are using
  AI for contract analysis, costing, and comparator research across employers, which means **the
  asymmetry that used to favour the employer's data access is closing.** Comp M5 owns the general
  principle that the counterparty has AI too; what is specific here is that this counterparty can
  compel information and has a professional advocate.
- **Interactive:** choice — four intake responses to an AI-drafted grievance; find the one that judged
  the tool instead of the content.
- **Activity:** *"Allegations, separated"* — a written intake procedure for a long AI-drafted
  submission: how allegations get extracted from characterization, what a model may and may not do in
  that extraction, who confirms the list, and how the complainant is told what is being investigated.
  Written generically. **No submitted grievance, real or paraphrased.**

## L6 · Three layers of law

*~40 min · the floor · the heaviest module in the track · counsel review required before ship*

**Claim to contest:** *"You can name, for every labor authority you rely on, whether it is a statute,
a Board decision, or a General Counsel memorandum — and when it last changed."*

Most legal training in this field teaches the rules. The rules here move at three different speeds,
and **advice built on the wrong layer expires.** So this module teaches the stack before the content.

- **Lesson 1:** The three layers `[V]`. **Statute — stable.** Section 7's protection of concerted
  activity, the duty to bargain, Weingarten. Decades old, unchanged by any of this. **Board doctrine —
  flips with administrations.** In *Endurance Environmental Solutions* (10 December 2024) the Board
  reversed the 2019 *MV Transportation* "contract coverage" test and restored **clear and unmistakable
  waiver**, retroactively — making a general management-rights clause much weaker cover for a
  unilateral change. **General Counsel priorities — fastest of all.** **GC 23-02**, on electronic
  monitoring and algorithmic management interfering with Section 7 rights, was **rescinded on 14
  February 2025** in GC 25-05, among 29 memoranda withdrawn, with case backlog cited. A practitioner
  who memorised GC 23-02 in 2024 is wrong today. One who concludes from the rescission that the
  exposure is gone is also wrong, **because the statute never moved** — a rescinded memorandum
  withdraws a prosecutorial theory, not a right.
- **Lesson 2:** And the layer that just moved under all of it `[V]`. The Board lost quorum for nearly
  a year and regained it on **7 January 2026**, when Scott Mayer and James Murphy — confirmed 18
  December 2025 — were sworn in alongside General Counsel Crystal Carey, leaving David Prouty as the
  sole remaining member of the previous majority. Two consequences you can act on: the composition
  that decided *Endurance* no longer holds, so **the waiver standard is in play and should be
  re-checked before you rely on it**; and matters implemented during the quorum gap are now reachable
  by a Board working through its backlog. There was no forum for a year. There is one now.
- **Lesson 3:** The duty to bargain over AI — settled doctrine, unsettled application `[V]`, and the
  distinction is the lesson. **Settled:** a change to wages, hours, or terms and conditions of
  employment is a mandatory subject, and a mandatory subject cannot be changed unilaterally without
  notice to the union and a genuine opportunity to bargain. AI that monitors, scores, allocates, or
  disciplines plainly touches terms and conditions. **Not settled:** there is **no Board decision
  applying that to AI.** SAG-AFTRA filed a charge against Llama Productions in May 2025 arguing a duty
  to bargain before replacing workers with AI, and the Board has yet to opine on AI-induced layoffs.
  Secondary sources state the conclusion as if it were law. It is a strong inference from settled
  doctrine with a pending charge and no holding — which is HRBP M3's *unverifiable is not the same as
  false*, applied to law. Under clear-and-unmistakable waiver, your existing management-rights clause
  probably does not cover it; the practical answer is to bargain it or to negotiate explicit language.
- **Lesson 4:** What unions have actually won, and the convergence `[V]`. CWA's contract at Microsoft
  subsidiary ZeniMax requires notice to the union when AI implementation affects bargaining-unit work,
  and became a template reused elsewhere in tech. NewsGuild-CWA holds roughly 85–90 agreements with AI
  language, 58 of them ratified newsroom contracts. **The UPS Teamsters contract prohibits using
  sensor data as the sole basis for discipline and requires human review of context before
  AI-generated performance data is acted on** — which is 101 M7's assist/decide line, written into a
  collective bargaining agreement by the other side of the table. About 38% of union members report at
  least one provision on automated management or surveillance, most commonly notice. **Notice, human
  review, and records are the same duties HRBP M6 identifies as the recurring statutory obligations** —
  arriving here by contract rather than statute, which means in a represented workplace you may owe
  them years before a regulator asks.
- **Lesson 5:** Section 7 without a union, which is why this module is not conditional. Protected
  concerted activity does not require representation. AI monitoring that chills employees discussing
  pay, hours, or conditions is exposure in a non-union workplace on the same statutory basis — and the
  rescission of GC 23-02 removed the theory, not the section. Then: what you can do this quarter
  without waiting for counsel, and the specific list to bring them so the answer is not a shrug.
- **Interactive:** sorting — ten propositions about AI and labor law: statute / Board doctrine /
  General Counsel priority / not established at all.
- **Activity:** *"The authority map, dated"* — every legal proposition your matter type relies on,
  each tagged by layer, with the date it last changed and the date you last checked, plus the two you
  discovered you could not source. **Prediction:** how many you would have mislabelled before Lesson 1.

## L7 · Findings that hold

*~25 min · the course lands*

**Claim to contest:** *"Your function's product is closed cases."*

- **Lesson 1:** The product is a finding that holds. Cycle time and closure rate are the metrics this
  function is measured on and neither is the product — a case closed fast on a record that collapses
  under challenge is a liability that has not been recognized yet. What holding looks like: the rung
  boundaries visible in the file, the conflicts recorded as conflicts, the human resolution
  attributable to a human, and the comparator question answered before the decision rather than after
  the complaint.
- **Lesson 2:** What to measure instead `[V]`, and this function has an unusual advantage: the
  benchmark exists. Substantiation by issue type — the thing only 32% track. Repeat-respondent
  detection. Whether a required process exists at all, which 38% cannot claim. Time-to-first-contact
  rather than time-to-close. And the one nobody measures: **what share of your matters could withstand
  the comparator analysis opposing counsel would run.**
- **Lesson 3:** The honest capacity conversation. Allegations doubled, staffing flat — so AI in this
  function is not a productivity story, it is a triage story, and the question is which rung you spend
  the recovered minutes on. The answer this track argues for is **rung two**: corroboration, the
  unglamorous work of establishing what exists, what conflicts, and what is missing, which is where
  cases are actually won and lost and where AI is genuinely permitted.
- **Lesson 4:** The bet, with a baseline and a kill condition. One change to your matter type, what
  you would measure, what you would stop doing to fund it, and **what you would turn off** — including
  the possibility that the right answer is a tool your team is already using without sanction.
- **Interactive:** choice — four proposed changes; find the one that improves cycle time and weakens
  the record.
- **Activity (course close):** *"One change, one measure, one condition"* — one page: the change, the
  baseline from your own aggregate data, the measure at 90 days, the funding line, and the written
  condition under which you would reverse it. Then **the delta**: every claim you contested in L1–L6,
  which turned out to be true of your function, and what changed. Graded on the account of the change
  and the evidence behind it, never on which way you guessed.

## Decisions (v1)

1. **Async and self-serve.** No invented surfaces. Positions committed in the activity, argued by the
   tutor.
2. **This is the first track with a 301 prerequisite.** `ai301-hrbp` M6 owns the ER documentation
   craft — structure, the completeness pass, language discipline, the stop line, the provenance rule —
   and re-teaching it would be exactly the failure the authoring brief warns about. So this track
   assumes it and builds past it. That makes `ai301-ler` the first track in the ladder that depends on
   a sibling rather than only on the rungs below, which is a real change to the prerequisite model and
   should be a deliberate one.
3. **The signature module exists because a shipped module declined to draw a line.** HRBP M6's
   knowledge check marks "flagging inconsistencies between witness accounts" as approaching
   credibility and stops. Right for a generalist; insufficient for a specialist who does it weekly.
   L2 draws it: **a model may locate conflicts; only a human may resolve them** — and the trap in the
   middle is asking which account is more consistent with the other evidence, which is resolution in
   an analytical costume. This is the best material in the track and it was found by reading the
   shipped module rather than by exploring the role.
4. **The spine is a matter type, not a matter, and no activity may accept case facts.** Every other
   track runs on a live artifact — a requisition, a cycle, a capability, a register. The equivalent
   here would be a live investigation, and case content must never reach an AI-graded submission, an
   operator review queue, or the tutor. So the spine sits at process level and **the rubrics carry it
   as a rejection condition, not a caution.** This is the clearest instance yet of a role property
   dictating product mechanics, and it needs building into the rubric schema rather than into prose.
5. **It interacts with the assessment convention and the interaction is stated.** Activities now
   demand evidence from the learner's own organization rather than self-report. Here that means
   process and aggregate evidence only — substantiation rates, cycle times, what the case system can
   query. Both rules hold; a learner left to reconcile them alone will reconcile them wrongly, so L1
   says it explicitly before the first activity.
6. **Seven modules, 25–40 minutes, ~3h35.** Matches the HRBP and recruiter counts. L6 at ~40 min is
   the heaviest here — three layers of authority plus the bargaining duty plus contract language plus
   Section 7 — and ships as one module, consistent with the other three legal modules, with completion
   watched.
7. **The decomposition is an evidentiary ladder, not a quadrant.** Observation → corroboration →
   inference → finding → determination. Discovered from the role — it is how investigators are trained
   to write — and it does work no quadrant would: **AI's permission changes at every rung and stops at
   a specific boundary**, which turns 101 M7's binary into a five-position control. Comp uses five work
   types; the shape recurs, the content does not.
8. **Verified before this outline was written, and it changed three things.** Confirmed: HR Acuity's

## Open questions for review

- **Is a 301-to-301 prerequisite acceptable?** Decision 2 makes this track depend on HRBP M6. The
  alternative is duplicating the documentation craft, which the brief forbids, or moving that lesson
  out of HRBP M6 into here — which would weaken a shipped module for a generalist audience that
  genuinely needs it. Current answer: assume it and cross-reference. Needs a product answer for how a
  prerequisite in a *sibling* track surfaces in the path.
- **Does HRBP M6 Lesson 3 get trimmed once this exists?** My answer is no, but the two will drift
  without an explicit cross-reference in both directions, and only one direction is in my control.
- **Does labor relations eventually want its own track?** L6 and half of L5 are the labor half. In
  heavily unionized industries — manufacturing, transport, healthcare systems, public sector — labor
  relations is a distinct function with its own craft, and a track that gives it one module plus half
  of another may under-serve it. Resolved for now by making Section 7 the non-conditional spine so the
  floor works for everyone; revisit if the audience skews unionized.
- **Should works-council consultation move here from People Ops M7?** It sits there as a deployment
  dependency, which is right for a systems owner; in Europe it is core LR work. Current split: People
  Ops keeps deployment consultation, this track takes the US duty to bargain. A European learner may
  find that arbitrary.
- **The comparator/control-group boundary with `ai301-analytics`.** L4 Lesson 4 draws it, and both
  tracks are better for it — but the People Analytics track was outlined in parallel with this one and
  does not yet carry the reciprocal note. Worth one line there, since a learner who takes both will
  otherwise import statistical intuitions into a legal analysis where a single instance is dispositive.
- **The case-history index.** L4 teaches the scoping rule for a searchable investigation corpus.
  Whether we also ship a reference architecture is a product decision — and it is exactly the corpus
  People Ops M6 says not to index, so the two modules must be read together by whoever builds it.
- **The rejection condition needs schema support.** Decision 4 requires rubrics to return submissions
  containing identifiable case content. That is a rubric-schema capability, not prose, and it is the
  one thing in this outline that cannot be delivered by writing content alone.
