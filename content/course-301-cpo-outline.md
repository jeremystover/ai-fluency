# AI 301 · The Specialist — CPO / CHRO track · "The Decision Series" (draft v1)

**Audience:** Sitting Chief People Officers and CHROs — P&L exposure, a board relationship, a peer
C-suite fight to win, and accountability for decisions that cannot be walked back.
**Level transition:** L3 The Specialist → L4 The Translator.
**Course id:** `ai301-cpo` (not yet registered in `src/shared/roles.ts` — register only when the
track is complete).
**Shape:** 6 modules · 25 min each · **~2h30, the shortest track on the ladder, deliberately.**
Same package as the rest — read, micro dose, tutor, podcast, one interactive, one AI-graded
activity, one knowledge check. Async and self-serve.
**Prerequisite:** AI 101 (or the diagnostic test-out) and AI 201. **The exclusions are the
product** and the track says so out loud: no AI fundamentals, no prompting primer, no tool tour, no
vendor showcase, no "three types of AI." Those are commodity, and free elsewhere.
**Tooling stance:** Tool-agnostic. Every statistic, statute, case study, and vendor claim is
volatile-layer `[V]`. This track's statutory surface moved *nine days* before this outline was
written — see M5.

> **One-line positioning:** *Every other program teaches CPOs about AI. This one teaches them about
> their job.*

---

## Why this track exists, and why I argued against it

The authoring brief asks for a placement check before any CHRO track gets outlined: does this
audience belong at 401 rather than 301? **I ran that check and concluded 401. That conclusion was
wrong**, and the record of why is in `course-301-cpo-exploration.md` rather than buried here,
because the error is instructive and the brief's own hard-won lessons predicted it.

Short version: I subtracted **topics** rather than **decisions**. Asked whether vendor evaluation
was covered, the answer was yes, in three places — so the scope fight looked redundant. It isn't:
M3 is not about evaluating a vendor, it is about whether digital labor is workforce and who owns
the registry. Asked whether policy was covered, the answer was yes, comp M4 — so governance looked
redundant. It isn't: M5 is not about drafting a policy, it is about owning a trust deficit you do
not control. The brief warns about exactly this failure — *"explorations compare topics; outlines
compare designs, and topics look far more alike than designs do"* — and a topic-level subtraction
against a role I had explored but not designed for produced a false negative.

The second error: I read 401's published blurb as a specification of an *audience* when it is a
specification of a *rung*. Coaching other functions, evaluating vendors, running pilots, owning
policy — an HRBP who grows into leading change does all of that. **401 is for everyone at L4.**
See Decision 2 for what that leaves a CPO who finishes this track.

## The through-line

> **The scarce resource moved from information to nerve. "We don't have the data" is no longer
> available as shelter.**

## Where the learner stands (design premise)

Not anxious, and not a champion in waiting. **The most skeptical person in an optimistic room.**
Roughly 80% of executives expect AI to improve bottom-line performance within three years, while
just 5% of CHROs expect even half of HR's own work to be AI-enabled in that window, and people
leaders are measurably the most cautious C-suite function on workforce readiness `[V]`. At the same
time, in 52% of organizations HR has no direct involvement in AI strategy at all; 28% report HR is
regarded as a true strategic leader with a seat at the table, and a further 19% say HR is seen as a
strategic partner *and still excluded from core decision forums* `[V]`.

> "I am accountable for what this does to our people, and in half of companies like mine the
> function isn't in the room where it gets decided. Don't teach me what a transformer is. Tell me
> which fight to pick, what to cut, and what to tell the board."

**This is not a change-agent curriculum.** Every competing program runs the same arc — fear →
literacy → tools → use cases → ethics → implementation plan — and aims the learner at becoming an
AI Champion who goes and convinces their organization. That is a good curriculum for an HR
director. It is the wrong altitude for the person who has to *decide*.

## The organizing logic

Competing programs organize by **AI capability** (what is AI, three types, prompting, agents) or by
**HR functional domain** (recruiting, L&D, performance, rewards). Neither maps to how a CPO's
accountability works.

**This track organizes by the decisions the role has to make.** Six modules, six decisions, six
committed positions.

## The spine: six positions, committed

Every module produces **an artifact that is a decision written down** — not a reflection, not a
plan. Every module opens with a prediction and ends on a contestable claim the learner takes a
position on. M6 gathers the five predictions and scores the delta.

**Assessment is track-record based, not confidence-based** (Decision 5). Never *"how confident are
you?"* — always *"what did you actually do last quarter?"* Self-reported AI confidence is worthless
at this altitude; behaviour is not.

| Module | The decision |
|---|---|
| M1 | What you will personally do, weekly |
| M2 | Where your time and budget are actually going, against where value is moving |
| M3 | What part of AI ownership you are claiming — or explicitly declining |
| M4 | What you will cut in your own function, and the sentiment cost you will absorb |
| M5 | Which unreviewed decision about a person you are going to go find |
| M6 | Your first bet, its baseline, and the date you report against it |

## M1 · Fluency you can't delegate

*~25 min · opens with the five-prediction gate · the only module about the participant personally*

**The gate (5 min, no teaching).** Five numbers, committed cold, each paid off in a named module:

1. Last quarter's calendar, split across fiduciary/governance, the operating system, and strategic
   counsel. Three percentages. *(M2)*
2. What share of enterprise genAI pilots show measurable P&L impact? *(M6)*
3. If you shut off the human HR channel tomorrow, how far would service satisfaction fall before it
   recovered? *(M4)*
4. What share of enterprises have a CHRO leading AI workforce strategy? *(M5)*
5. Name one consequential decision about a person in your company currently being shaped by a
   system nobody in HR has reviewed. *(M5 — and "I can't name one" is itself a finding)*

- **Lesson 1:** The credibility chain — personal fluency → functional transformation → enterprise
  standing. **Argued from craft, not from a statistic** (Decision 7). The available number points
  the right way and cannot carry the claim: in Culture Amp's 2026 AI in HR study, HR professionals
  reporting transformation in core processes were nearly three times as likely to say AI made them
  better leaders — **70% against 25%** — from **264 respondents drawn from the vendor's own
  community**, cross-sectional, which establishes a correlation and says nothing about which
  direction it runs `[V]`. The lesson says that out loud, because this audience checks, and because
  M6 asks them to hold vendors to exactly this standard. You cannot lead Job B without doing Job A,
  and the reason is structural rather than statistical.
- **Lesson 2:** What executive-altitude practice actually looks like `[V]`. Not a tool demo — a
  standing habit. The CHRO who records his meetings, keeps them in a private model, and every
  Monday asks what he did well, what he did poorly, and what to change. A CPO generating candidate
  hypotheses for senior searches personally rather than commissioning them.
- **Lesson 3:** The delegation trap. This is the one thing on your agenda with no budget line and
  no IT ticket — and the one thing that cannot be handed to a chief of staff, because delegated
  fluency produces confident wrongness at the top of the house, where it is most expensive and
  least likely to be corrected by anyone in the room.
- **Lesson 4:** The standard this track holds you to. Not confidence, track record. Every artifact
  in this course asks what you did, not how you feel about it — and the first one is due before the
  next module.
- **Interactive:** choice — four executive AI practices; find the one that builds judgment rather
  than output.
- **Activity:** *"The standing practice"* — set up **one** recurring personal use, run it at least
  twice, and report what it surfaced that you would otherwise have missed. Graded on whether it is
  a practice rather than an experiment.
- **Claim:** *"You cannot lead a transformation you have not personally performed."*
  **The strongest counter:** CEOs lead finance transformations without doing the accounting, and
  demanding personal craft fluency of executives may confuse craft with leadership. A CPO's scarce
  hours might be better spent winning M3's scope fight than on a Monday journaling habit — and the
  Culture Amp correlation runs both ways, since leaders who were already good at this are the ones
  who transformed their processes.
- **Knowledge check:** 8 questions.

## M2 · The shape of your job is changing

*~25 min · the frame · load-bearing for everything after it*

- **Lesson 1:** The three tiers — **fiduciary and governance**, **the operating system**,
  **strategic counsel.** What each actually contains, and why the role has always been described as
  one job when it is three with different economics.
- **Lesson 2:** The compression thesis. **AI compresses the middle and expands both ends.**
  Governance gets harder and gains new surface area. The administrative core is where automation
  lands. Strategic counsel gets more powerful — because the constraint was never intelligence, it
  was the cost of knowing things.
- **Lesson 3:** The scarce resource moved from information to nerve. *"We don't have the data"* is
  no longer available as shelter, and a function that used it as shelter for twenty years has to
  find out what it actually thinks.
- **Lesson 4:** Reading your own numbers against the thesis. Where the calendar and the budget
  disagree with each other, and what a mismatch between them predicts about the next two years.
- **Interactive:** sorting — twelve pieces of real CPO work into the three tiers.
- **Activity:** *"Calendar and budget, mapped"* — last quarter's calendar and this year's HR budget
  against the three tiers. **Both numbers**, and where they disagree. **Calibration:** score
  prediction 1.
- **Claim:** *"The middle of your function is where the automation lands, and the middle is where
  your budget is."*
  **The strongest counter:** the tiers are not separable in practice. Governance credibility is
  *earned* by running the operating system well, and a CPO who lets the middle hollow out may find
  the strategic counsel tier evaporates with it — because nobody takes advice from a function that
  cannot run payroll.
- **Knowledge check:** 8 questions.

## M3 · The scope fight

*~25 min · the highest-variance module · this is where the disagreement will be*

- **Lesson 1:** The scene. A CEO convenes the executive team on who owns AI and gets six confident,
  different answers. Nobody is lying; the question genuinely has no default owner.
- **Lesson 2:** The existence proof `[V]`. Moderna merged People and Digital Technology under a
  single leader — Chief People and Digital Technology Officer — reframing the work from separate
  workforce planning and technology planning into unified **work planning**.
- **Lesson 3:** The claim, stated plainly. If agents have owners, budgets, access rights,
  performance standards, onboarding and offboarding — **that is workforce management.** Vendors are
  already building the registry layer `[V]`; the question is only whether the person who owns the
  human org chart also owns the other one.
- **Lesson 4:** Winning it, or declining it well. What "HR should own AI governance" loses to, and
  what specific competence wins. Then the honest alternative: **declining is a legitimate answer**
  and has to be defensible — including the version where the CIO already selected the vendor and
  you are being handed adoption rather than a decision.
- **Interactive:** choice — four ownership claims; find the one an executive team would actually
  grant.
- **Activity:** *"Your claim, in three sentences"* — what part of AI ownership you are asserting, on
  what basis, and who you have to convince. **Or explicitly decline, and write why.** Both are
  graded the same way: on whether the position survives the strongest objection to it.
- **Claim:** *"Agents with budgets and access rights are headcount, and headcount is yours."*
  **The strongest counter:** the analogy flatters the function. Agents are capital equipment with a
  conversational interface — they do not have careers, grievances, or protected characteristics,
  and the disciplines that make HR good at managing humans are mostly irrelevant to managing
  software. A CPO who wins this fight may inherit an operational burden they are the wrong function
  to carry.
- **Knowledge check:** 8 questions.

## M4 · The function you have to cut

*~25 min · restructuring your own house, and the trough*

- **Lesson 1:** The case in full, including the ugly part `[V]`. IBM consolidated HR service into
  AskHR; nobody used it; so they shut off the HR email address and phone line and told 21,000
  managers their HR partners were no longer available for questions. **Service satisfaction fell to
  −35.** They held.
- **Lesson 2:** Where it landed `[V]`. Roughly 94% of employee questions resolved without
  escalation, satisfaction recovered to +74, and a **40% reduction in the HR operating budget.**
- **Lesson 3:** **The J-curve.** The trough is the shape of the curve, not evidence of failure —
  and the reason most transformations die is that the sponsor's tolerance was set below the trough
  before anyone measured how deep it goes. Naming your tolerance in advance is the whole discipline.
- **Lesson 4:** **The sequencing rule**, and the move that makes the cut credible rather than cold.
  You have no standing to tell other functions to redesign work until you have done it to yourself.
  And IBM raised entry-level hiring while cutting the operating budget `[V]` — on the reasoning that
  gutting the junior pipeline dries the well in three to five years. **What you build with the
  savings is the part that determines whether this reads as strategy or as a cost exercise.**
- **Interactive:** choice — four transformation trajectories; find the one whose sponsor will
  abandon it at month four.
- **Activity:** *"The first cut"* — the first process or role in your own function you would
  automate, **the sentiment cost you are prepared to absorb**, and what you would build with the
  savings. All three, specifically. **Calibration:** score prediction 3 against IBM's trough.
- **Claim:** *"You have no standing to tell any other function to redesign its work until you have
  done it to your own."*
  **The strongest counter:** this is a moral argument dressed as a strategic one. Nobody asks the
  CFO to automate finance before advising on capital allocation. Insisting on self-cutting first may
  simply delay enterprise value by a year to buy the CPO a credibility badge — and in a downturn,
  cutting your own function first is how you lose the capacity to manage everyone else's cuts.
- **Knowledge check:** 8 questions.

## M5 · The trust you own but don't control

*~25 min · counsel review required before ship*

Governance taught as **a property of every decision rather than a compartment** — which is the
critique this track makes of the field, and it does not require abolishing the module where the
hard edges live. This is that module.

- **Lesson 1:** The asymmetry `[V]`. Employees trust AI least exactly where HR operates: decisions
  about their careers. Roughly a quarter of candidates believe AI evaluates them fairly. Only about
  13% of enterprises have a CHRO leading AI workforce strategy. **The CPO owns the trust deficit
  whether or not they own the design decisions** — and that gap is the module.
- **Lesson 2:** The statutory floor, which moved nine days before this was written `[V]`.
  **Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 — the Digital Omnibus on AI — was published 24 July 2026 and entered
  into force 27 July 2026, six days before the AI Act's original high-risk deadline.** Employment
  high-risk obligations moved from **2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027**; embedded systems to August
  2028. **But most Article 50 transparency obligations still applied from 2 August 2026.** So the
  CPO who thinks the deadline passed and they are exposed is wrong, and the CPO who thinks it all
  got delayed is also wrong. Knowing which half moved is the literacy this module is for.
- **Lesson 3:** The clean rule, and why it survives every rollback. **AI is never the
  decision-maker.** Automate the assembly, not the reasoning — because HR's actual product is
  manager judgment, and a policy that outlives a statute is worth more than one that tracks it.
  Roughly half of organizations have an AI policy and only a quarter of those believe it is clear
  and durable `[V]`; the gap between having a policy and having governance is this lesson.
- **Lesson 4:** The scenario nobody has an answer for. An employee confides in the company's AI
  coaching tool for six weeks — isolation, belonging. It offers support. It never escalates. She
  resigns and alleges the company knew and did nothing. Read it, sit in it, and notice that your
  policy probably does not address it.
- **Interactive:** sorting — ten systems touching decisions about people: reviewed / needs review /
  should not be running.
- **Activity:** *"The unreviewed decision"* — identify one consequential decision about a person
  currently being made or shaped in your company by a system nobody in HR has reviewed. **Name it.**
  If you cannot find one, that is a finding too, and it probably means you have not looked hard
  enough. **Calibration:** score predictions 4 and 5.
- **Claim:** *"You own the trust deficit whether or not you owned the design decision."*
  **The strongest counter:** accountability without authority is a recipe for a CPO who takes the
  blame for the CIO's architecture. The honest response might be to *refuse* ownership of the trust
  deficit until the design authority comes with it — which is the opposite of what this module
  recommends, and is exactly the position M3's decliners took.
- **Knowledge check:** 8 questions.

## M6 · The number you'll have to defend

*~25 min · measurement as a design constraint · the course lands*

- **Lesson 1:** The famous number, and what it actually says `[V]`. MIT's *GenAI Divide* found 95%
  of enterprise genAI pilots delivered no measurable P&L impact — from 52 executive interviews, 153
  survey respondents, and 300 public deployments. **State the sample, because this course demands
  that of vendors.** Success was defined as measurable KPI impact within six months, and critics
  argue it may describe organizations measuring the wrong things at the wrong time rather than
  technology failing. Gartner's read puts roughly 28% of use cases meeting ROI expectations, and
  47% of CHROs report no clear AI productivity measurement at all `[V]`.
- **Lesson 2:** The hard lesson. ***"We can't tell if it worked"* and *"it didn't work"* produce an
  identical conversation with a CFO.** Which means measurement is not a reporting activity; it is a
  design constraint that binds before the first pilot starts.
- **Lesson 3:** The survival rule. **Place your first bets where you already have trusted
  baselines** — even when those are not the highest-value problems — because you have to survive
  the first budget review to get to the second. The highest-value problem with no baseline is how
  ambitious programs die in month eighteen.
- **Lesson 4:** The board conversation. The same number, aimed upward: what a board actually asks
  about human capital and AI, what a comp committee needs, and why a defended number beats an
  impressive one in a room that has seen a lot of impressive numbers.
- **Interactive:** choice — four proposed first bets; find the one that survives eighteen months.
- **Activity (course close):** *"The first bet"* — four fields: the bet, its baseline metric,
  **today's value of that metric**, and the date you will report against it. If you cannot fill in
  field three, that is the lesson. Then **the delta reckoning**: all five opening predictions, what
  moved, and why. The rubric grades the account of the change, never the accuracy of either end.
- **Claim:** *"A bet you cannot baseline today is a bet you will lose in eighteen months, whatever
  the technology does."*
  **The strongest counter:** the baseline rule systematically steers the function toward measurable
  and therefore unambitious work — exactly the trap MIT's critics identify. The transformations
  worth doing may be precisely the ones with no clean baseline, and a CPO who only bets where they
  can measure has optimized for surviving budget reviews rather than for changing the company.
- **Knowledge check:** 8 questions.

# Integration

## The spine call

**The human outline's spine wins outright and is adopted whole** — all six modules, their titles,
their sequence, and their artifacts. This is the least-modified integration of the five tracks, for
one reason: it is the only human brief that arrived with **a market teardown attached**, and the
teardown does work no amount of role exploration could substitute for.

The finding that carries it: every competing program organizes by AI capability taxonomy or by HR
functional domain, and **nobody organizes around the decisions the role has to make.** That is a
curriculum-design insight, not a topic list, and it is what makes this track defensible against a
crowded market rather than a sixth entry in it.

Four things in the brief that no exploration would have produced:

1. **Module 300 / M1 — personal fluency as the credibility precondition.** Nothing in the
   curriculum treats an executive's own practice as a prerequisite for leading. It is also the only
   thing on a CPO's list requiring no budget and no IT ticket, which makes it the right opener.
2. **The scope fight.** Digital labor as headcount, with the registry layer as the concrete stake.
   I had this filed under "vendor evaluation, already covered." It is not that at all.
3. **The trough and the sequencing rule.** The J-curve as a named object, and "no standing to tell
   other functions to redesign until you have done it to yourself." Both teachable, both absent
   from the market.
4. **"We can't tell if it worked" and "it didn't work" are the same CFO conversation.** The sharpest
   line in the brief, and it converts measurement from a reporting chore into a design constraint.

## What survives from my analysis

- **The skeptic finding, as the design premise.** CHROs are the most cautious C-suite function — 5%
  expecting half of HR work AI-enabled within three years against ~80% of executives expecting
  bottom-line impact. This replaces the FOMO-reduction opening the HRBP track uses, and it is a
  better read of this audience than "anxious professional needing encouragement," which is what the
  whole market assumes.
- **The exclusion data, with its sample.** 52% / 28% / 19%, from SHRM's *State of AI in HR 2026*
  (n=1,908, fielded December 2025). Sharper than the ~13% figure the brief uses for the same point,
  and it belongs in M5 Lesson 1 alongside it.
- **The 401 boundary, stated the other way round.** See Decision 2.

## What changed from the brief

1. **Async conversion.** Fifth human brief, fifth flipped-classroom design — and the most
   live-dependent of the five. The conversions: *positions collected before the session* → activity
   submissions, which the product already does; *claimers versus decliners* (M3) → the claim
   mechanism plus the tutor, which reads every block and will argue; *the closing open question*
   → a genuinely unsettled claim, which the product handles well. **One real loss, stated plainly:**
   "hearing IBM's −35 in a room of peers recalibrates faster than any slide" is true, and async
   cannot fully reproduce it. The prediction gate plus the reveal recovers part of it. That is a
   partial substitute, not an equivalent.
2. **Modules renumbered 300–305 → M1–M6.** The data model orders from 1, the same call the HRBP and
   Talent Development tracks made — and here the original numbering also collides with course-level
   numbering.
3. **M5 keeps the module even though ethics is a property.** The brief's critique of the field is
   right: competitors make ethics a compartment, so it is absent everywhere else. But threading it
   through every module and having a module where the statutory edges live are not in tension — that
   is what the other four tracks do. M5 is that module, and it carries the counsel-review gate.
4. **The EU AI Act content is replaced, and the replacement is better.** See the correction below.
5. **The board conversation gets a home.** The brief's own gap list names it as something nobody
   teaches, and then no module carries it. It lands as M6 Lesson 4, where defending a number upward
   is the same skill as defending it to a CFO.
6. **Time budget held at ~2h30, for a different reason than the brief gives.** The brief sets ≤2.5h
   because more than that produces four people who did the pre-work. Async has no live session as
   forcing function, so that particular risk changes shape — but the conclusion holds and is
   stronger here than for any other role. This is the shortest track on the ladder and should stay
   that way.

## Corrections found before drafting

**The EU AI Act claim is wrong, and it is nine days old.** The brief states the Act is "fully
applicable as of 2 August 2026 with HR algorithms classified high-risk." What actually happened:
**Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the Digital Omnibus on AI, was published in the Official Journal on 24
July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July 2026** — six days before that deadline. Employment
high-risk obligations moved to **2 December 2027**; Annex I embedded systems to August 2028.
**Most Article 50 transparency obligations did still apply from 2 August 2026.**

The correction improves the lesson. A module that teaches "the deadline passed, you are exposed" is
wrong; one that teaches "it all got delayed" is also wrong; the useful thing is knowing which half
moved. **This also affects two other tracks** — HRBP M6 and recruiter R6 both carry the deferral as
a *proposal*, and it is now adopted law. Flagged as a follow-up.

**The IBM metric is mislabelled.** The brief reports "HR's eNPS fell from +19 to –35." The verified
figure is an internal **customer-satisfaction score**, which fell to −35 and recovered to +74. For
an audience that knows exactly what eNPS is, shipping that mislabel would cost more credibility
than the number buys. The +19 starting point did not verify and should be dropped unless sourced.

**Moderna's specifics are partly unverified.** Tracey Franklin's title and the People/Digital
Technology merger are confirmed. The "3,000+ custom GPTs" and "~5,800 people" figures did not
surface and need a targeted check — M3 works without them, since the org-design move is the point.

## Decisions (v1)

1. **This is a 301 track, not 401.** The placement check ran, returned the wrong answer, and was
   overturned by the market teardown plus an actual design. Recorded in the exploration doc rather
   than quietly fixed, because the failure mode — subtracting topics instead of designs — is one
   the brief already warns about and will recur.
2. **What 401 is for, now that this exists.** 401 is the rung where a specialist learns to lead
   beyond their own function — for everyone, not for CHROs. The CPO is the one role that arrives at
   301 *already* leading beyond their function, which is why their 301 looks like other people's
   401. **The honest consequence: a CPO who completes this track may find 401 reinforcing rather
   than necessary**, and the ladder should not pretend otherwise. That is a property of this role,
   not a defect in the ladder.
3. **The exclusions are stated in the content.** Third track to do this, and the most important
   place for it — the market's tonal mismatch at the top end (a ~$2,000 senior credential whose lab
   covers AI music creation) is precisely what this audience is guarding against.
4. **Six modules, all load-bearing.** The brief offers a four-module fallback (M1, M3, M4, M6 as
   load-bearing; M2 folding into a live opening and M5 into M4). **Rejected for the async product:**
   M2 is the frame everything else reads from and has nowhere to fold to without a live session, and
   M5 is the counsel-gated module — dropping it would make this the only track on the ladder with no
   floor.
5. **Track-record assessment, adopted course-wide.** "Never *how confident are you*, always *what
   did you actually do last quarter*" is a real improvement and belongs in every track's rubric
   guidance. It sits comfortably with the prediction gate, which asks for estimates of facts and
   outcomes rather than self-rated confidence — but the distinction should be written into the
   convention so nobody adds a confidence scale later.
6. **One anchor per module — and M1 deliberately has none.** M1 argued from craft (Decision 7); M2
   none, argued from the tier frame; M3 the Moderna org move; M4 the IBM trough; M5 the trust
   asymmetry; M6 the MIT 95% *with its sample stated*. Everything else supporting or cut.
7. **The Culture Amp finding is demoted from anchor to supporting — settled after verification.**
   The brief leads M1 on it. Chased to source it is Culture Amp's *2026 AI in HR Study*, released
   22 July 2026: 70% versus 25%, from **264 HR professionals in the vendor's own community survey**,
   cross-sectional. Three problems for a module opening a track aimed at the most skeptical
   executive in the C-suite — a small self-selected sample, a vendor with an interest in the
   result, and a correlation with no direction. **Opening the course on a citation this audience
   can dismiss in one line is the worst possible place to lose them**, and M1's claim is a craft
   argument that does not need propping up. The number stays, stated with its sample and its
   causal limit; the argument leads.
