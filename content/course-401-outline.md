# AI 401 · The Translator — Course Outline · "Load Paths" (approved)

**Audience:** Everyone at L4 — anyone who has finished a 301 role track and now has to lead
beyond their own desk. HR directors, People leaders, COE heads, HRBPs growing into change work,
and anyone accountable for AI adoption in work they do not personally perform. **Not a CHRO
course** — see Decision 2.
**Level transition:** L4 The Translator (the ladder's top rung).
**Course id:** `ai401` · module ids `ai401-m1` … `ai401-m10`. Not role-split: 401 is one course.
**Shape:** 10 modules · 20–35 min each · ~4h30 · same package as the rest of the ladder — read,
micro dose, tutor, podcast, one interactive, one AI-graded activity, one knowledge check. Async
and self-serve.
**Prerequisite:** AI 101 (or the diagnostic test-out), AI 201, and a 301 role track. **Advisory,
not enforced** — 401 declares no strong prerequisites, internal or external, so every module stays
clickable for the demo. See Decision 9.
**Tooling stance:** Tool-agnostic. Every statistic, statute, vendor claim, and named case is
volatile-layer `[V]`.

> **One-line positioning:** *Every other program teaches leaders to adopt AI. This one teaches them
> to keep the organization standing while it happens.*

**Excluded, and named as excluded in M1:** AI fundamentals, prompting, tool tours, vendor
showcases, and a standalone change-management module. The first four are 101 and 201. The fifth is
Decision 5.

## The through-line

> **AI lowers the cost of individual production and raises the cost of collective coherence.**

Everything in the course follows from that one sentence. The seven symptoms leaders report —
turf conflict, orphaned work, integration debt, stalled collective learning, isolation, unowned
decisions, metrics that produce themselves — are not seven problems. They are one problem seen
from seven positions, and the position you occupy determines which one you notice first.

## The mental model: load paths, not blueprints

An architect's job is not to draw the finished building. It is to determine what is load-bearing,
specify the structure, and set tolerances for loads that cannot be predicted precisely.

That resolves the course's central tension. **You cannot pre-specify the destination. You can
specify what must hold.** Said in M1, it inoculates the whole course against vision-document
drift — because from that point on the deliverable is a spec with a trigger, and a spec with a
trigger is the one leadership artifact that can be wrong in a way you would notice.

## Where the learner stands (design premise)

They have a build of their own — 201's shipped workflow — and a role they now know in depth. What
they do not have is authority. **CPO M3 is "win the scope fight and own it." This course is "you
did not win it, and you still have to lead."** That distinction is the reason 401 exists as a
separate rung rather than a longer 301, and it is why Step 7 of the framework (name the owner) is
load-bearing rather than tidy.

> "I am responsible for how this lands in teams I don't run, doing work I don't do, using tools I
> didn't choose. Don't tell me to build a coalition. Tell me what has to hold, and how I'd know it
> stopped holding."

## The repeated framework

One framework, run three times — at a team, at a function, at the organization. It is
altitude-invariant, and M2 demonstrates the invariance rather than asserting it. Seven steps:

1. **Name the load.** What is this unit actually responsible for holding up?
2. **Find what's carrying it.** Which structures, roles, and rituals bear that load today —
   including the ones nobody designed?
3. **Mark what's dissolving.** Which of those is AI removing, weakening, or making optional?
4. **Specify the replacement.** A structural spec, not a drawing. What must be true, not what it
   must look like.
5. **Set the tolerance.** How much variation, speed, or volume can this absorb before it fails?
6. **Name the trigger.** What observable condition means the spec is wrong and you replan?
7. **Name the owner.** When the trigger fires, who actually decides? It may not be you.

Steps 5–7 are the accountable ones and the reason this cannot degrade into a vision statement.
**Step 7 is load-bearing for the whole course:** most of this audience cannot change the
organization directly, and a framework that never asks who decides quietly assumes an authority
the course elsewhere says they do not have.

The framework is introduced in M2. Every module after it is one application of it to a specific
dissolved structure.

## The capstone thread — "What We're Keeping Standing"

401 carries an accumulating artifact, which no 301 track does. The learner picks one unit in M1,
specs it in M2, and every module after contributes one component. M10 assembles them into a
**vision deck addressed to a named audience.**

The deck exists because a spec and a deck carry different loads:

> **A spec is what you manage with. A deck is what people follow.** The market's mistake is
> producing a deck and calling it a plan. The opposite mistake — producing a spec and calling it
> leadership — is the one this course would make without it.

Four rules keep it from becoming a slogan, and all four are rubric-enforceable:

1. Every claim traces to a line in the framework.
2. At least one named thing you are **not** doing.
3. At least one falsifiable trigger, on a slide, not in an appendix.
4. **The anti-slogan test:** could a reasonable person disagree with this? A vision nobody could
   disagree with is a mission statement.

The product already supports the thread: the activity screen renders "your build so far" from
prior submissions and passes the same trail to the grader, so M10's feedback can reference the M2
spec with no new schema.

## M1 · Load Paths

*20 min · framing · the shortest module in the course, on purpose*

**Claim to contest:** *"Someone in my organization is evaluating what AI is doing to how we work
together — not to our output, to our working relationships."*

- **Lesson 1:** The thesis and the mechanism `[V]`. Production cost falls, coherence cost rises,
  and the seven symptoms are one problem. The anchor: only **6% of leaders say they are making
  progress designing human–AI interaction**, while **organizational factors account for more than
  twice the reported AI impact of individual factors (67% vs 32%)** — n=20,000 knowledge workers
  across 10 markets, fielded Feb–Apr 2026. Individual fluency is not where the variance lives, and
  the whole 101→301 ladder has been building individual fluency.
- **Lesson 2:** Cultural debt `[V]`. The cost accumulated when AI scales faster than the norms and
  accountability structures around it. **42% of workers say their organization rarely evaluates
  AI's impact on people**; **80% are concerned colleagues use AI to appear more productive than
  they are**; **60% of executives use AI in decision-making and 5% say they manage it well.** The
  debt is invisible because nobody has been assigned to look at it.
- **Lesson 3:** Load-bearing versus decorative. The mental model, and the move that makes it
  useful: **the structures most likely to be load-bearing are the ones nobody designed** — the
  undocumented handoff, the person everyone asks, the meeting that exists for a reason no one can
  state. Nothing designed them, so nothing protects them.
- **Lesson 4:** Tolerances, not drawings. Why the course's deliverable is a spec with a trigger,
  and what that rules out. Names the exclusions, and points forward to where change management
  lives (Decision 5).
- **Interactive:** sorting — twelve things a People function does, into *load-bearing and holding*
  / *load-bearing and dissolving* / *decorative*.
- **Activity:** *"One structure"* — name one structure in your organization that was load-bearing,
  that nobody designed, and that AI is currently removing. One paragraph on what it was actually
  carrying. This names the unit the rest of the course threads.
- **Aggregation:** how many people in your organization could name who is accountable for AI's
  effect on how teams work together? A count, checked.
- **Claim:** *"The structures most likely to be load-bearing are the ones nobody designed."*

## M2 · The Frame

*35 min · the longest module and the spine*

**Claim to contest:** *"My organization's AI plan names a destination and a date."*

- **Lesson 1:** Transitional versus transformational change `[V]`. The distinction that invalidates
  the change model most of this audience was trained on: transitional change has a destination you
  can specify before you begin; transformational change has a future state that emerges. AI
  adoption is the second, and every roadmap written as if it were the first is a credibility loan
  you will have to repay. **See the verification record — the "leaders who admit uncertainty
  accelerate adoption" claim did not survive checking, and this lesson argues from consequence
  instead.**
- **Lesson 2:** The seven steps, taught one at a time with a worked example.
- **Lesson 3:** Demonstrating invariance. The same seven rows held at team, function, and
  enterprise altitude, side by side, before the learner writes their own. The columns change
  scope; the rows do not change at all.
- **Lesson 4:** What makes a trigger real. Observable, dated, and owned by someone — and the test:
  **if no condition would make you replan, you have not written a spec, you have written a hope.**
- **Interactive:** choice — four planning artifacts; find the one that cannot fail.
- **Activity:** *"The frame, at your altitude"* — the seven steps completed for the unit named in
  M1. Must contain at least one falsifiable trigger and a named owner. Addressed to the person who
  would have to approve replanning, with a predicted response.
- **Rehearsal:** the executive who wants a three-year roadmap and reads *"I can't tell you where
  we'll be"* as weakness. Run until the learner holds the position without either capitulating to
  a fake roadmap or sounding evasive.
- **Claim:** *"A roadmap for a destination you cannot specify is a credibility loan, and you repay
  it in the quarter you miss."*

## M3 · Somebody Else's Load

*25 min · the convergence point after nine role tracks · no anchor statistic, by design*

**Claim to contest:** *"I could state the top metric my finance partner is measured on, in their
words, without looking it up."*

- **Lesson 1:** What your track made you unable to see. Expertise compresses the world into the
  shape of your own throughput — the recruiter sees a sequence of openings, the comp analyst a set
  of cycles, the ER specialist a series of matters. Each is one load out of six the unit carries.
  M1's model, run on yourself.
- **Lesson 2:** Activity and consequence. HR's credibility problem with finance, legal, and IT is
  not that HR is non-technical. **HR reports activity; they think in consequence.** The
  translation is the unit of account, not the vocabulary.
- **Lesson 3:** Reading another function's load. Four things findable without their cooperation —
  what they are measured on, what their year contains, their real AI exposure, whose asks they are
  already absorbing — and the one you must ask for: **what do they think People is for?**
- **Lesson 4:** The question behind the question. Step 1 run conversationally.
- **Interactive:** choice — the same request arrives from four functions; match each to what it is
  actually asking.
- **Activity:** *"The partner load sheet"* — for one function you depend on and do not run.
- **Aggregation:** can you state your finance partner's top metric right now — yes / no / I could
  guess.
- **Claim:** *"You cannot name what another function is holding up, and you have been designing for
  them anyway."*

## M4 · Ownership Contracts

*25 min · role differentiation*

**Claim to contest:** *"Everyone on my team could name who owns the handoff between us and the
team we work with most."*

- **Lesson 1:** The data `[V]`. **92% say their responsibilities have grown beyond their original
  job description in the last year**; the heaviest AI users are **nearly twice as likely to take on
  work from other teams** and **twice as likely to handle specialist tasks without looping in the
  expert**; non-AI users are **7x more likely to say their role has not changed** — n=1,000 US
  knowledge workers, fielded May–June 2026.
- **Lesson 2:** The reframe. **Large teams hide bad interfaces.** Humans between functions absorbed
  ambiguity — clarifying requirements, chasing data, translating between systems. AI did not create
  the turf problem. It removed the people papering over it.
- **Lesson 3:** Redundancy is sometimes correct. The design question is where you want redundancy
  and where you want a single owner — and to decide it rather than let it be settled by whoever
  moves fastest.
- **Lesson 4:** Writing the contract at the workflow, not the reporting line.
- **Interactive:** choice — four boundary situations; find the one where conceding is correct.
- **Activity:** *"The ownership contract"* — addressed to the peer you share the boundary with,
  with a predicted response. **Send/don't-send is graded**, and *"not yet, and here is what has to
  be true first"* is frequently the right answer.
- **Rehearsal:** the peer who believes they own it. **Roughly half the runs should end with the
  learner correctly conceding** — this is a diagnosis module, not a persuasion module.
- **Claim:** *"AI did not create your turf problem. It removed the people who were absorbing it."*

## M5 · The Invisible Work Audit

*25 min · ambiguity absorption · the most immediately useful module in the course*

**Claim to contest:** *"If one specific person on my team dropped 30% of their capacity, I could
name what would stop happening."*

- **Lesson 1:** Span of control as a variable, not a ratio. It widens when work is modular,
  standards are clear, metrics are reliable, and people have judgment to operate autonomously; it
  narrows when work is novel, cross-functional, ambiguous, high-risk, or politically sensitive.
  **The error is using one ratio everywhere.**
- **Lesson 2:** Coverage, not headcount. Stop asking *how many people do I need* and start asking
  *which functions genuinely require a human and which can AI absorb.* Two complications: AI can
  *raise* optimal team size where individual leverage rises faster than coordination cost, and a
  four-person pod has no margin for a weak hire.
- **Lesson 3:** The orphaned-task answer. On a large team, homeless work gets absorbed by whoever
  has slack. On a lean team there is none, so the work either becomes an explicit ownership
  decision or **it silently stops happening** — and nobody notices, because nothing was ever
  written down saying it was happening.
- **Lesson 4:** Where middle managers sit in this, and why they are the load-bearing failure point.
- **Interactive:** sorting — twelve absorbed tasks into *named owner* / *absorbed by someone* /
  *would silently stop*.
- **Activity:** *"The invisible work inventory"* — one team, the tasks absorbed by no one's job
  description, who absorbs them, and what happens to each if that person's capacity drops 30%.
- **Aggregation:** how many items did you find, and how many had no named owner?
- **Claim:** *"Inventory the absorbed work before you shrink the team, because after you shrink it
  the evidence is gone."*

## M6 · Integration Capacity

*35 min · the counterintuitive core, and the whole of procurement*

**Claim to contest:** *"My organization can name the rate at which it can absorb change."*

- **Lesson 1:** Work moves faster at the edges and slows as it comes together. Outputs arrive in
  greater volume and take more effort to reconcile. Decisions get made faster in isolation and take
  longer to stabilize when they collide. **Local optimization increases variation, and variation is
  paid for in coordination.**
- **Lesson 2:** The conclusion. **Individual AI adoption can increase total coordination cost while
  decreasing production cost** — which is why the early adopter charging ahead independently fails.
  They generate integration debt faster than the organization retires it.
- **Lesson 3:** The discipline. An organization has a maximum rate at which it can absorb
  individually-generated change, and almost nobody measures it. **You do not trade speed against
  risk. You govern speed against absorption.**
- **Lesson 4:** Procurement as architecture `[V]`. **Lock-in is integration debt with a contract
  attached.** Switchability as an architectural property. The slow-down signals: autonomy claims
  without guardrails or a safe mode; no answer on accuracy and hallucination rates for your use
  cases; no clarity on who owns the system after the launch team moves on. Plus the HR-specific
  layer — bias audit results, adverse impact testing by region, and the question vendors deflect:
  **who holds liability when the system produces a discriminatory outcome.** Explicitly *not* 101
  M3's quote-reading; this is the layer above it.
- **Interactive:** choice — four adoption trajectories; find the one generating integration debt
  fastest.
- **Activity:** *"The absorption limit"* — your unit's current integration capacity, one thing
  presently exceeding it, and the tolerance you would set. Addressed to whoever controls the pace.
- **Rehearsal:** the high-performing early adopter who is genuinely producing more and genuinely
  creating integration debt. The learner has to slow someone down without punishing excellence.
- **Claim:** *"The person creating the most value in your organization this quarter may also be
  creating the most debt, and both are true at once."*

## M7 · Codification

*25 min · collective learning*

**Claim to contest:** *"There is a place where what my team has learned about using AI is written
down, and someone maintains it."*

- **Lesson 1:** Individual learning velocity rose sharply. Collective learning velocity did not
  move. One-time training cannot build habits for a technology that changes every few weeks.
- **Lesson 2:** Prompt fluency is necessary and insufficient. Value arrives when practice is
  codified into shared artifacts, measured through workflow telemetry, and circulated through
  tracked cycles. Without codification, discoveries stay trapped with individuals — and **collective
  learning that depends on individual memory and goodwill does not survive a resignation.**
- **Lesson 3:** Embed rather than classroom `[V]`. The mechanism worth copying, with its evidence
  stated honestly: the applied evidence here is largely vendor-supplied and the module says so.
- **Lesson 4:** The handoff to M8. **Asking a colleague a question was doing two jobs at once** — it
  moved information and it built the relationship. AI replaced the first and deleted the second.
  Codification restores the information half. It does not restore the other half.
- **Interactive:** sorting — codification artifacts into *durable* / *person-dependent* /
  *theater*.
- **Activity:** *"The trapped practice"* — one piece of practice currently in one person's head,
  and the codification design: where it lives, who maintains it, how you would know it is used.
- **Aggregation:** does your team have any shared codification at all — yes / no.
- **Claim:** *"Codification is not documentation. Documentation nobody maintains is a fossil of a
  practice, and it is worse than nothing because it looks like coverage."*

## M8 · Designed Need

*25 min · help-seeking · the module with no market equivalent*

**Claim to contest:** *"Most people on my team had a conversation with a colleague last week that
went beyond transactional work."*

- **Lesson 1:** Lead with the good news, because it is what makes this hard `[V]`. Among AI users at
  large organizations, **62% say AI decreased their stress and burnout risk** and **86% feel more
  productive** — n=2,150 full-time employees across seven countries at organizations of 3,500+, all
  active AI users, fielded March–April 2026. **This module is not an argument against AI adoption
  and must not be read as one.**
- **Lesson 2:** The rest of the same study `[V]`. **33% rarely or never have a workplace
  conversation that goes beyond transactional work in a given week. 14% took time off in the past
  year due to loneliness or isolation — 20% among Gen Z, who are 12x more likely than Gen X to
  report feeling completely disconnected. In the last 12 months, 76% used AI for advice, 52% to
  brainstorm, and 37% for companionship.**
- **Lesson 3:** The mechanism. Help-seeking was the organization's social infrastructure — how trust
  got built, how norms transmitted, how newcomers found mentors, how people learned what
  opportunities existed. **AI removed the need for the transaction without replacing the function it
  served.**
- **Lesson 4:** Why this is structural rather than pastoral. When workplace relationships weaken,
  access to opportunity narrows, because connection is how people find sponsors and hear about
  internal moves. **A mobility and retention problem with a two-year fuse.** Then the design
  question, stated plainly: *why does anyone on your team still need anyone else on your team?* If
  the honest answer is "they don't," you have a retention problem that will not appear in any
  dashboard for eighteen months.
- **Interactive:** choice — four proposed interdependencies; find the one that is manufactured
  social time.
- **Activity:** *"One designed interdependency"* — a genuine reason two people must work together.
  Structural, not recreational.
- **Aggregation:** what proportion of your team had a non-transactional conversation with a
  colleague last week, to your knowledge? **Most will not know, and not knowing is the finding.**
- **Claim:** *"Ask why anyone on your team still needs anyone else on it. If the honest answer is
  'they don't,' that is a retention problem with a two-year fuse."*

## M9 · The Floor

*25 min · **counsel-review gate, stated at the top of the module** · the limit*

**Claim to contest:** *"I could name, today, one decision about a person in my organization that is
being shaped by a system nobody in People has reviewed."*

- **Lesson 1:** You are the deployer `[V]`. The distinction that decides who carries the obligation:
  the vendor builds, you deploy, and most employment obligations land on the deployer regardless of
  what the vendor's marketing says. The four duties that recur across every regime — notice, bias
  auditing, a right to human review, and record-keeping — reused verbatim from shipped tracks.
- **Lesson 2:** The European position, and which half moved `[V]`. Reused verbatim from the shipped
  HRBP and recruiter modules and the CPO track's correction.
- **Lesson 3:** The agent theory `[V]`. *Mobley v. Workday* and what it means for how you buy —
  reused verbatim. **Neither party gets to point at the other.**
- **Lesson 4:** The rule that outlives the statute. **AI is never the decision-maker; automate the
  assembly, not the reasoning.** And the enforcement problem this course is actually about: a
  prohibition nobody can comply with produces concealment rather than compliance, so a floor
  without an exception path is a floor people route around.
- **Interactive:** sorting — ten uses touching decisions about people into *permitted* /
  *permitted with conditions* / *prohibited*.
- **Activity:** *"The unreviewed decision"* — name one, or record the search that failed and why
  that is itself a finding. Plus the exception path you would write.
- **Claim:** *"A floor with no exception path is not a floor. It is a detour sign."*

## M10 · The Operating System, and the bet

*30 min · legible accountability · the course lands*

**Claim to contest:** *"The metrics my organization uses to track AI would still look good if AI
were producing no value at all."*

- **Lesson 1:** Where culture becomes real. McKinsey's four building blocks `[V]` — understanding
  and conviction, role modeling, talent and skills, and reinforcement through formal mechanisms —
  mapped onto instruments: **the first two are influence work, the third is capability work, the
  fourth is authority work.** You cannot influence your way to new promotion criteria. If metrics,
  incentives, decision rights, and rituals keep rewarding the old behavior, the new behavior dies
  regardless of how well everything upstream went.
- **Lesson 2:** Goodhart, hard `[V]`. Measure AI consumption and you get AI consumption. The worked
  example: a UK law firm put a **£1 million bonus pool against collectively hitting one million
  Copilot prompts — and hit it more than four months early.** The target was met. Nothing in the
  target could tell anyone whether it was worth meeting.
- **Lesson 3:** What to measure instead, and the pilot that produces a defensible number: an agreed
  baseline, a pre-committed decision rule, a stated tolerance for the trough, and a kill condition.
  **"We can't tell if it worked" and "it didn't work" produce an identical conversation with a
  CFO** — carried from the CPO track with credit.
- **Lesson 4:** The deck. Spec versus deck, the four rules, and the anti-slogan test.
- **Interactive:** choice — four proposed metrics; find the one that will produce itself.
- **Activity (course close):** *"What We're Keeping Standing"* — the deck, assembled from the
  course's artifacts and addressed to a named audience; the bet with today's baseline value and a
  review date; **what you would turn off**; and the delta reckoning across all ten opening claims.
- **Rehearsal:** the audience that agrees with everything and does nothing. The learner has to
  detect enthusiastic assent and convert it into a commitment with a date.
- **Claim:** *"A metric that cannot embarrass you is not a measurement, it is a mirror."*

## Where change management lives

Distributed deliberately. A standalone change-management module is the single most cliché-prone
thing in leadership training and would undo the drift guards.

| Idea | Home |
|---|---|
| Transitional vs. transformational change | M2, where it justifies triggers over roadmaps |
| The four fears — being found out; accountability without control; stepping into the unknown; losing your professional edge | the casting brief for every rehearsal antagonist |
| McKinsey's four building blocks | M10, mapped to instruments |
| Resistance as diagnosis | M4's conceding rehearsals and M6's early-adopter case |
| Middle managers as the load-bearing failure point | M5, where their absorbed work is what the audit finds |
| The trough, and tolerance set before it | M6 (absorption) and M10 (thresholds measured inside a decline) |

It is named as a topic in M1 Lesson 4 and pointed forward. It does not get a module.

## Mechanics, applied course-wide

- **Every artifact is addressed to a named person outside the course.** Grade the artifact and the
  learner's prediction of the response. A real reply is a second submission. **No rubric ever
  depends on one arriving.**
- **Send/don't-send is a graded decision** on M4 and M10, available on all others. Timing is most
  of the skill in a claim, and a course that only rewards sending teaches half the job.
- **Assessment is track-record based.** Never *"how confident are you."* Always *"what did you
  actually do last quarter."* (CPO Decision 5, adopted course-wide.)
- **Every strategy artifact carries at least one falsifiable trigger condition.** Mechanically
  enforceable, and it kills vision-cliché at the source.
- **Rehearsal antagonists are sometimes right.** If every counterpart is an obstacle, the course is
  a persuasion trainer and has contradicted its own diagnostic frame.
- **The rehearsal evidence caveat.** Applied evidence for AI rehearsal in HR is vendor-supplied;
  the theoretical case rests on behavioral therapy and medical simulation. With the mechanic in
  four modules, the course says so rather than implying more.

## Prerequisite map

- 101, 201, and a 301 track assumed but **not enforced** (Decision 9).
- M1 first — it names the unit the whole course threads.
- M2 before everything after it; the framework is the vocabulary.
- M3 before M4 and M6 (both address artifacts to another function).
- M5 before M6 in practice order — you inventory before you govern pace — though either works.
- M7 before M8; M7 Lesson 4 sets up M8's mechanism explicitly.
- M9 open order after M2, but before any procurement or deployment decision the learner has
  scheduled.
- M10 last — it carries the deck and the reckoning.

# Integration

## The spine call

**The human exploration's spine wins outright and is adopted whole** — the load-path mental model,
the seven-step framework, the altitude-invariance demonstration, the dissolved-structure module
map, and the mechanic alternation. Five things in it no amount of adapting the Claude outline would
have produced:

1. **A mechanism rather than a premise.** "AI lowers the cost of individual production and raises
   the cost of collective coherence" *generates* the module list. The Claude outline had a shared
   condition (accountability without authority) and a list of responsibilities, which is a topic
   list wearing a curriculum's clothes.
2. **"Tolerances, not drawings" solves drift definitionally.** The Claude version proposed
   procedural guards against vision-cliché. This makes the deliverable structurally incapable of
   being one.
3. **Step 7 embeds the authority question in the framework**, so it is asked in every module rather
   than asserted once in an opening diagnosis.
4. **The invariance table.** Showing the same seven rows hold at three altitudes beats asserting it,
   and it is the best single piece of pedagogy in either document.
5. **M8 · Designed Need**, and M7's close setting it up. Nothing in the market, nothing in the
   Claude outline.

## What survives from the Claude outline

- **The two shipped promises the exploration did not cover.** Four places already promise 401 by
  name — the `ai401` card in `content/modules.json`, `src/shared/levels.ts`, `src/shared/goals.ts`
  (the `strategy` and `coach` goals both point here), and AI 201 M8's closing lesson. Checked
  against the map, *evaluating vendors* was covered well and *coaching other functions* partially,
  but **running pilots that produce evidence** was not covered and **owning the policy** was
  scattered across two modules with no artifact. Both now land: pilot design in M10 Lesson 3, policy
  in M9.
- **The floor module and the counsel gate.** Every track on the ladder has one; the CPO outline

## What changed from the exploration

1. **Renumbered.** The data model orders from 1 and the original numbering collides with
   course-level numbering — the same call the CPO, HRBP, and Talent Development tracks made.
2. **Two modules added** (M3, M9) and **one module's contents redistributed** (404's compliance
   layer to M9; 407 gains pilot design and the deck). Eight modules became ten; the time budget went
   from ~3h40 to ~4h30, which is the ExComms track's shape and the current ceiling.
3. **The vision deck is in, as the capstone thread.** The course owner asked for it directly. It is
   reconciled with the drift guards by the spec-versus-deck distinction and four rubric-enforceable
   rules, and it solves a real structural gap: the exploration had excellent per-module artifacts
   and no accumulating object.
4. **The opening mechanism is a contested claim, not a prediction.** Course-wide convention: a
   confidence rating is self-report and self-report is not evidence. Numeric fields are retained
   only where an honest measurable exists to close against — which, because of cohort aggregation,
   now buys a peer comparison it did not buy before.
5. **The change-management distribution is adopted unchanged**, including the reasoning. The Claude
   outline proposed a standalone module and was wrong; the exploration's argument against it is the
   same drift argument, applied more consistently.

## Decisions

1. **401 is one course, not role-split.** The Translator's work is the same whether the learner
   arrived from comp or from ER; the variable is authority, not function, and M2's Step 7 carries
   it. `trackForRole` is not involved.
2. **401 is for everyone at L4, and explicitly not a CHRO course.** Recorded in the CPO track's
   Decision 2 and adopted here: the CPO is the one role that arrives at 301 already leading beyond
   their function, which is why their 301 looks like other people's 401. The reconciliation this
   outline adds: **CPO M3 is "win the scope fight and own it"; 401 is "you didn't win it and you
   still have to lead."** A CPO who takes 401 will find M2, M6, M8, and M10 new and M5 and M9
   partly familiar. That is a property of the role, not a defect in the ladder.
3. **Vertical subtraction runs against three rungs, and the third rule is new.** 101 and 201
   subtract **absolutely**. 301 subtracts **only at the four invariants** (contested opening,
   decompose-your-job, the floor, the closing bet), because every learner completed a different
   role track. Anything taught in exactly one 301 track is fair game at 401 and is named below.
   *Proposed as an addition to the authoring brief.*
4. **Two vertical collisions caught in drafting, both named in-content.** M7's shared libraries sit
   directly above **201 M2** (context packs) and **201 M7 Lesson 3** (library hygiene, review
   cadence) — the personal-to-collective distinction is real, and M7's opening states it before
   teaching anything. M6 Lesson 4 sits above **101 M3 Lesson 4** (reading a vendor quote, spotting a
   thin wrapper); the lesson says explicitly that it is the layer above, not a repeat.
5. **No standing change-management module.** See the table above.
6. **Every artifact addressed to a named person, and no rubric depends on a reply.** The role
   property dictating product mechanics, as the LER track's case facts did for that track.
7. **The interactive per module is specified at outline time**, not left to drafting — five
   sortings and five choices, listed per module above.
8. **Aggregation is used where the finding is uncomfortable to hold alone** (M1, M3, M5, M7, M8),
   and rehearsal where a person has to be moved (M2, M4, M6, M10). M9 uses neither; it uses the
   sorting engine and a counsel gate.
9. **No enforced prerequisites, internal or external.** The demo requires every module clickable.
   Prereqs are stated in-content and carry an `unlockHint`; nothing is declared *strong*, which is
   the only prerequisite kind that locks an open module. The `ai401` card flips `locked` → `open`
   at the integration pass, and **module rows go in only when the track is complete.**
10. **The rehearsal mechanic's evidence is stated rather than implied.** It appears in four modules
    and its applied evidence is vendor-supplied.
